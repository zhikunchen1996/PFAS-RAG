## 1. Overview and scope

Foam fractionation (FF) is consistently used in this evidence base as a **non‑destructive phase‑separation step**: PFAS are transferred from bulk water into a smaller foam/foamate volume, which then requires further management. Across the studies, FF is applied to:

- Landfill leachate [R01, R02, R03, R04]  
- AFFF‑impacted groundwater in a drinking‑water NF + FF train [R05]  
- Mixed environmental waters (landfill leachate, AFFF‑groundwater, WWTP effluent) [R06]  
- PFAS‑enriched foamate from soil washing [R07]  
- Distilled and field water spiked or containing PFAS, used to develop FF + electrochemical oxidation trains [R08]

Below, “[Sxx]” refers to the structured records (R09–R10).

## 2. When is foam fractionation useful as a concentration step?

### 2.1 Landfill leachate

**Laboratory ozone foam fractionation**

- Ozone FF removed **>90% of long‑chain PFAS** from landfill leachate under optimized conditions (ozone gas flow ~1 L/min, ozone dose ~43 mg/L) while limiting foamate wettability (liquid fraction) to ~4%, i.e., a small residual foamate volume [R02, R11–E03].  
- Volume‑reduction factors and enrichment factors for total PFAS are not reported in the excerpts, but the minimized wettability indicates deliberate volume reduction.

**Pilot‑scale continuous FF**

- In a pilot continuous column on natural landfill leachate, **sum PFAS removal plateaued at ~60%**, with **>90% removal for individual long‑chain PFAS** and **<~30% for select short‑chain PFAS** [R01, R09].  
- Authors reported negligible differences between enriching vs stripping modes and between continuous vs batch operation under the tested regime, and concluded that continuous FF is “highly applicable” for PFAS‑contaminated water [R01, R09].  
- This evidence is limited to a single leachate and operating window, but it is the strongest pilot‑scale dataset on operational robustness.

**Commercial / full‑scale SAFF® systems**

- At Tveta landfill, a three‑stage SAFF40 system treats **330 m³/day** of leachate; over the reported period it processed ~**80,000 m³** with no exceedances of target PFOS/PFOA/PFHxS criteria [R03, R12–E03]. Final effluent concentrations were ≤2.34 ng/L PFOS, ≤1.28 ng/L PFOA, and ≤1.00 ng/L PFHxS, corresponding to **≥98.7–99.7% removal** for these long‑chain sulfonates and carboxylates [R03].  
- When non‑criterion short‑chain PFHxA, PFBS, PFPeA, and PFBA are included, **summed PFAS removal falls to ~55%**, because these short‑chains are “less amenable to removal” [R03, R12].  
- A related SAFF poster shows multi‑stage enrichment: a second “dry‑foam” stage increased PFAS concentration by **up to ~1,500‑fold**, leading to a **~10,000‑fold** total concentration increase after two stages [R13, R14]. The underlying matrix is PFAS‑impacted water, not specified further for this example.

**Assessment**

For landfill leachate, FF is demonstrably useful as:

- A **robust long‑chain PFAS remover** (field data with ≥98–100% removal for long‑chains [R03, R13]).  
- A **strong concentrator**, with demonstrated enrichment from tens‑fold (lab and pilot) to order‑of‑magnitude **10³–10⁴‑fold** when multi‑stage configurations and downstream concentration (e.g., Superloading) are used [R05, R06, R13, R04].  
- A process that leaves substantial **short‑chain PFAS in the treated effluent**, limiting summed removal when short‑chains are regulatory drivers [R01, R02–R13].

### 2.2 AFFF‑impacted groundwater and drinking‑water trains

The clearest drinking‑water case is the **NF + FF** treatment train for AFFF‑contaminated groundwater [R05]:

- The **NF pilot** alone removed **98% of total PFAS**, giving a permeate with **1.4 ng/L total PFAS** [R05, R15].  
- The **NF concentrate** was then treated by FF:
  - Without co‑surfactant: **90% total PFAS removal from the concentrate** [R02, R15].  
  - With cationic co‑surfactant: total PFAS removal increased to **94%** [R02, R15].  
  - The foamate volume was **~2% of NF feedwater volume**, with PFAS concentration **41‑fold higher than in the NF feed** (>3000 ng/L) [R02, R15].

Thus, in this train FF is **not the primary drinking‑water barrier** (NF is), but a **highly effective secondary concentrator** that collapses the NF reject into a ~2% side stream at substantially elevated PFAS concentrations [R02]. This is a strong and well‑quantified demonstration of FF as a concentration step for a relatively clean groundwater matrix once pre‑concentrated by NF.

### 2.3 Mixed environmental waters (groundwater, leachate, WWTP effluent)

Laboratory/bench studies show that FF can function as a broadly applicable concentration step:

- In Chemosphere experiments on both **laboratory‑spiked and environmental waters** (landfill leachate, AFFF‑contaminated groundwater, WWTP effluent), **removals were above ~70% for most monitored PFAS**, except the **shortest alkyl‑chains**, which had lower removal [R13, R16, E03].  
- Across the same work, PFAS concentrations in collected foam were “**up to over 30×**” higher than the original sample [R06, R13, R16], and the authors explicitly note the production of “low‑volume, highly concentrated PFAS solutions” [R13, R16].

These are aggregated results without species‑resolved or matrix‑specific conditions, so they should be interpreted as **qualitative benchmarks**, but they reinforce that FF can routinely yield **tens‑fold enrichment** from diverse environmental matrices.

### 2.4 Foamate from soil washing and other upstream treatments

For soil systems, FF is not described in the quantitative table, but several studies treat foamate downstream:

- A Frontiers study investigates **electrochemical oxidation of PFAS‑enriched foamate generated by soil washing + FF** [R07, R17–E03]. The FF step is not quantified here, but its role is to concentrate PFAS into a foamate suitable for electrochemical treatment.  
- Comfort et al. used FF to generate **PFAS‑enriched foamate** from distilled and field waters containing PFOA and mixtures of PFAS, which they then treated electrochemically [R08, R10–E03]. Again, FF is explicitly framed as a concentration step, though enrichment factors are not reported.

## 3. Short‑chain PFAS recovery

Across scales and matrices, the **most consistent limitation** of FF is low recovery of short‑chain PFAS.

### 3.1 Quantitative evidence

- **Pilot landfill leachate (Smith)** – select short‑chain PFAS showed **removals <~30%**, while long‑chains exceeded 90% [R01, R09].  
- **Commercial SAFF – landfill leachate** – when short‑chain PFHxA, PFBS, PFPeA, and PFBA are included, summed PFAS removal drops to **~55%**, explicitly because these species are “less amenable to removal” [R03, R12].  
- **SAFF site table (PFAS‑impacted water)** – long‑chain PFOS, PFOA, 6:2 FTS, PFDA, PFNA have **100% reported removal**, and PFHxS 97–99%; by contrast, short‑chain PFHxA, PFPeA, PFBA, PFBS show a **0–62% removal range** [R08 (R14), R14].  
- **Environmental waters (Chemosphere)** – shortest alkyl‑chain PFAS had **lower removals** than other monitored compounds, although exact values are not reported [R13, R16].  
- **NF + FF with co‑surfactant (AFFF‑groundwater)** – adding a cationic co‑surfactant during FF of NF concentrate increased removal of specific short‑chains in the FF step by **37% (PFPeA), 9% (PFHxA), and 34% (PFBS)** and yielded overall NF+FF removals of **59% (PFPeA), 99% (PFHxA), and 96% (PFBS)** [R06, R15]. This is one of the few examples where short‑chain removal can approach near‑complete levels for some analytes, but only within a **pre‑concentrated NF reject** and with **chemical modification** of the FF step.

### 3.2 Qualitative patterns and implications

- **Consistent chain‑length trend**: In every study that distinguishes chain length, **long‑chain PFAS enrichment and removal are much higher** than for the shortest chains [R01, R02–R13, R08].  
- **Co‑surfactant strategies**: The NF‑concentrate FF work shows that **cationic co‑surfactants can measurably boost short‑chain removal** [R06], but this introduces additional chemicals to be managed in the foamate and may not be acceptable in drinking‑water plants without careful downstream control.  
- **Treatment‑goal framing**: One full‑scale landfill system explicitly sets a goal that **short‑chains contribute <~45% of PFAS mass** [R04, R18], acknowledging imperfect short‑chain capture and aiming instead to control their mass fraction; the abstract does not show whether this goal is achieved.

**Regulatory implication:** FF alone is generally **insufficient to meet stringent short‑chain PFAS criteria** for drinking water or sensitive discharges. It is best viewed as a pre‑treatment that:

- Greatly reduces long‑chain PFAS load;  
- Moderately reduces some mid‑chain species;  
- Leaves substantial residual short‑chains in the treated water, unless coupled with additional barriers (e.g., NF upstream, co‑surfactant‑aided FF, or downstream sorption).

## 4. Achievable volume reduction and enrichment

Where quantified, FF produces substantial volume reduction and PFAS enrichment:

- **NF concentrate FF (AFFF groundwater)** – foamate volume ≈ **2% of NF feedwater**, with PFAS concentration **41× higher** than NF feed [R02, R15].  
- **Environmental waters & spiked samples** – Chemosphere experiments reported **up to >30×** PFAS concentration in final collected foam vs original water [R06, R13, R16].  
- **Multi‑stage SAFF** – second‑stage dry foam increased PFAS concentration by **~1,500‑fold**, yielding an overall **~10,000‑fold** increase after two SAFF stages [R13, R14].  
- **Landfill FF + Superloading (abstract)** – the combined system (FF + adsorptive media “Superloading”) achieved an overall landfill waste concentration factor of **~1000–2000‑fold**, with the Superloading step alone giving up to an **additional ~500‑fold** concentration of PFAS into solid media [R04, R18].  
- **Ozone FF** – foamate wettability minimized to ~**4%** [R02, R11], indicating strong volume reduction, though explicit enrichment factors are not reported.

These figures demonstrate that **order‑of‑magnitude (10×–40×) enrichment is common in single‑stage FF**, while **multi‑stage or hybrid trains can reach 10³–10⁴‑fold overall concentration factors**. However, only a subset of these data are species‑resolved, and enrichment specifically for short‑chains is seldom quantified.

## 5. Operational stability

Evidence for stability is mostly qualitative but spans lab, pilot, and commercial scales:

- **Pilot landfill FF (Smith)** – in a continuous column, differences between enriching vs stripping mode and continuous vs batch operation were **negligible for treatment efficiency**, and the process was deemed “highly applicable” [R01, R09]. The study does not provide long‑term uptime or fouling statistics.  
- **Commercial SAFF (Tveta)** – over ~**80,000 m³** treated in a multi‑year trial, the poster reports **no exceedances of PFOS/PFOA/PFHxS criteria** and no need for complex pre‑treatment [R03, R12]. Detailed downtime or maintenance data are not shown.  
- **SAFF technology description** – SAFF is described as unaffected by metals, organic substances, particles, pH, nutrients or other water‑chemistry variants [R13, R14], implying tolerance of complex matrices such as landfill leachate; quantitative verifications are not provided.  
- **Landfill FF + Superloading** – 1.5 years of full‑scale operational data are mentioned, with “stable foam fractionation performance through long‑term chemical dosing strategies” [R04, R18]. No numerical stability metrics (e.g., run length between cleaning) are given.  

Overall, **pilot and commercial evidence suggests that FF can be operated stably over long periods** in leachate and site waters, even with high co‑contaminant loads, but **formal reliability statistics are lacking**.

## 6. Energy demand

Direct energy data for FF are mostly absent; the evidence is qualitative:

- The pilot landfill study describes FF as **cost‑ and energy‑efficient** and suggests combining it with “energy‑intensive degradative technology” on the foamate [R01, R09]. No kWh/m³ numbers are provided.  
- SAFF is characterized as using **only air and electricity and no filter materials** [R13, R14], which removes media regeneration/disposal but does not quantify power consumption.  
- The NF + FF study notes that **energy demand and life‑cycle impacts of the combined train are only qualitatively discussed** [R05, R02].  
- The ozone FF study explicitly notes that **energy consumption of ozone generation and the process is not fully quantified** [R02].  
- For landfill FF + Superloading, **no energy or media replacement data** are available in the abstract [R04].  

Thus, while FF clearly **avoids the pumping and backwash demands of deep‑bed adsorption**, and technologies like SAFF emphasize low consumables, current evidence **does not allow robust comparison of FF energy intensity** to alternatives (e.g., GAC, ion exchange, RO) on a kWh/m³ or kWh/kg‑PFAS basis.

## 7. Management and downstream treatment of PFAS‑rich foamate

A key boundary condition is that **FF does not destroy PFAS**; success depends on how the foamate is handled. The evidence shows several approaches.

### 7.1 Proposed or generic destructive treatment of liquid foamate

- **Pilot landfill FF (Smith)** – authors propose that FF pretreatment could be coupled to an **energy‑intensive degradative technology** for the concentrated foam as a promising on‑site remediation strategy [R01, R09]. No specific technology is tested.  
- **Commercial SAFF (Tveta)** – the end product after three fractionation stages is a **highly concentrated liquid PFAS waste**, described as amenable to **supercritical water oxidation, plasma, electrochemical oxidation, reductive defluorination, or high‑temperature incineration**, but **no particular destruction step is implemented or evaluated** in the poster [R03, R12].

FF is advantageous here when:

- Regulatory drivers focus on **mass load reduction to the environment**, and  
- Site owners are prepared to invest in **separate high‑energy destructive units** or **off‑site incineration** for a relatively small, highly contaminated liquid stream.

### 7.2 Electrochemical oxidation of foamate

Two peer‑reviewed studies couple FF foamate to **boron‑doped diamond (BDD) electrochemical oxidation**:

- **Electrochemical treatment of soil‑washing foamate** – a Frontiers paper evaluates BDD electro‑oxidation of foamate generated by simultaneous soil washing and FF [R07, R17–E04]. The study investigates how **current density, flow rate, initial PFAS concentration, organic matter content, and recirculation/mass transfer** affect the **efficiency of PFAS degradation** [R07]. Quantitative removal or defluorination figures are not provided in the excerpts, but the work is clearly framed as degradation of PFAS in foamate, not merely further separation.  
- **Comfort et al. (Environments)** – bench‑scale work uses FF to concentrate PFAS (including 14C‑PFOA) into a ~**200 mL** foamate batch, then applies BDD electrochemical oxidation under stirred batch conditions [R08, R10–E02]. Separate records note:
  - For distilled water spiked with 14C‑PFOA, FF creates a radiolabeled foamate fraction for electrochemical treatment [R08 (R10)].  
  - For field‑collected water with PFAS mixtures, FF again serves as a concentration step, and **long‑chain PFAS were degraded more efficiently than short‑chains** during electrochemical oxidation [R08 (R10), R10].  
  - These studies track radiolabel distribution and fate but available excerpts do not report quantitative PFAS removal, transformation product speciation, or inorganic fluoride release.

**Implications:**

- The **volume and concentration levels** produced by FF (tens‑ to thousands‑fold enrichment; %‑level or higher foamate volumes) are well aligned with **small‑volume electrochemical cells**, making EO technically attractive.  
- However, **robust mass‑balance evidence** (parent loss vs. defluorination vs. byproduct formation) and **energy per kg PFAS degraded** are **still missing** from this body of work.  
- Chain‑length dependence persists: **short‑chain PFAS are less efficiently degraded** than long‑chains in EO of foamate [R08], paralleling the FF selectivity issues.

### 7.3 Adsorptive “Superloading” and conversion to solid waste

The landfill leachate system described by Newman [R04] represents a different foamate‑management philosophy:

- FF is applied at **~100 gal/min** (lead‑lag Foam X‑3000 units) to landfill leachate [R04, R18].  
- The resulting **foamate** is treated in **successive adsorptive media beds**. The treated foamate is then **re‑blended into the FF influent**, while the **PFAS‑loaded solid media are returned to the landfill**, converting the initial liquid PFAS waste into a **solid waste** [R04, R18, E04].  
- The abstract reports **up to ~500‑fold additional concentration** of PFAS into the solid media (Superloading) and an overall waste concentration factor of **~1000–2000‑fold** for the landfill [R04, R18].  
- Treatment goals include removing **75% of PFAS mass** and maintaining **<~45% of PFAS mass as short‑chains** [R04, R18], but it is not clear from the abstract whether these goals are consistently met.  
- No information is provided on **long‑term chemical stability or potential leaching** of PFAS from the returned media.

**Assessment:**

Under conditions where:

- The landfill has secure, engineered containment;  
- Regulatory frameworks permit **returning PFAS‑loaded solids** to the landfill; and  
- Goals center on **minimizing liquid PFAS discharge** (to surface water or WWTP),

this approach can be advantageous by **minimizing off‑site waste and transport risks**. It does **not constitute PFAS destruction**, and its long‑term risk reduction depends entirely on landfill performance.

### 7.4 Discharge to WWTP after FF

The SAFF poster for Tveta landfill indicates that full‑scale SAFF treatment of leachate is used to meet a **PFOS discharge limit of 50 ng/L** to a receiving WWTP [R19, R14]. However:

- The poster does **not** report achieved effluent PFOS concentrations for that application, nor foamate generation rate or foamate treatment/disposal [R19].  
- The SAFF system is described as a **physical separation step prior to biological/other treatment at the WWTP** [R19].

This scenario can be advantageous when:

- The receiving WWTP can manage residual PFAS mass (e.g., via sorption to sludge), and  
- Regulatory compliance is primarily tied to **influent concentrations** to the WWTP (e.g., PFOS <50 ng/L).

It shifts PFAS from raw leachate into **two residual streams**: concentrated foamate and WWTP biosolids or effluent, highlighting the importance of **system‑wide mass balance** rather than endpoint concentrations alone.

## 8. When is foam fractionation an advantageous part of the treatment train?

Synthesizing across matrices and scales, FF is most advantageous as a concentration step when:

1. **Feeds are PFAS‑rich and surfactant‑bearing**  
   - Landfill leachate, AFFF‑contaminated groundwater, and other waters with significant PFAS and organics provide enough surface activity to generate stable foams.  
   - SAFF marketing claims tolerance to metals, organics, particulates, and variable pH [R13, R14], which is supported qualitatively by successful operation on complex leachates [R03, R19].

2. **The primary objective is mass load reduction of long‑chain PFAS**  
   - Strong, repeatable evidence shows ≥90–100% removal of long‑chain PFAS in both pilot and full‑scale systems [R01, R02, R03, R13].  
   - Residual short‑chains mean FF alone seldom meets ambitious total PFAS or short‑chain standards, but it can substantially reduce the **mass requiring downstream high‑energy treatment**.

3. **There is a viable downstream option for the concentrated stream**  
   - On‑site destruction (SCWO, plasma, EO, etc.) or off‑site incineration for liquid foamate [R01, R03].  
   - Laboratory demonstrations of BDD electrochemical oxidation of foamate, which show that FF‑generated volumes are appropriate for EO even though full mass‑balance and energy metrics are still needed [R07, R08].  
   - Adsorptive “Superloading” systems that convert foamate to a PFAS‑loaded solid waste returned to the landfill [R04], appropriate where destruction is not currently feasible and the landfill provides secure containment.

4. **Volume reduction or waste‑minimization is critical**  
   - NF + FF: 2% foamate volume at 41× concentration [R02].  
   - Single‑stage lab/pilot FF: up to >30× enrichment [R06, R13].  
   - Multi‑stage SAFF and Superloading: ~10³–10⁴× overall concentration [R13, R04].  
   - These reductions make **expensive downstream destruction or handling more tractable**, but must be weighed against incomplete short‑chain capture.

5. **Continuous, long‑term operation is required**  
   - Multi‑year SAFF operation with substantial treated volumes and no reported exceedances of key long‑chain PFAS [R03].  
   - 1.5 years of full‑scale data with stable performance for landfill FF + Superloading [R04].  
   - Pilot FF indicating that continuous operation can match batch efficiency [R01].

## 9. Key trade‑offs, contradictions, and evidence gaps

### 9.1 Trade‑offs

- **Long‑chain vs short‑chain control**  
  - Long‑chains: FF is highly effective at all scales.  
  - Short‑chains: performance is poor to moderate (0–~60% removal in many field cases), unless additional measures (NF pre‑concentration, co‑surfactants) are used [R01, R02–R13].  
  - Treatment trains must therefore plan **separate controls** for residual short‑chains (e.g., downstream sorption).

- **Liquid vs solid residuals**  
  - Some systems deliberately retain foamate as a **liquid for destruction** [R01, R03, R07, R08].  
  - Others convert foamate into **solid adsorbents returned to the landfill** [R04].  
  - These strategies differ in risk profile: destruction is more expensive but permanently reduces PFAS mass; solidification relies on long‑term containment.

- **Energy vs consumables**  
  - FF itself uses only air and electricity and no media [R13]; energy is presumed lower than many high‑pressure or thermal processes but unquantified.  
  - Electrochemical destruction and SCWO are acknowledged as energy‑intensive [R01].  
  - Superloading sacrifices consumable adsorbent media to avoid liquid waste export [R04].

### 9.2 Contradictions and limitations

- **Perceived broad matrix tolerance vs limited datasets**  
  - Technology posters claim SAFF is “unaffected” by co‑contaminants [R13], yet most detailed data come from a small number of sites and matrices (e.g., Tveta landfill), with little systematic exploration of extremes (e.g., emulsified oils, very high DOC).  
- **Discharge‑limit framing vs mass‑balance reality**  
  - The Tveta example highlights compliance with a **PFOS concentration limit to a WWTP** [R19], but offers no data on foamate fate or WWTP sludge PFAS, making it difficult to assess actual risk reduction.

### 9.3 Evidence gaps and research needs

To deploy FF responsibly as a concentration step, several gaps should be addressed:

1. **Quantitative energy and life‑cycle assessment**  
   - kWh/m³ and kWh/kg‑PFAS for FF units (including ozone‑based systems) across scales.  
   - Comparative LCAs for “FF + destruction” vs “no FF + direct destruction” pathways.

2. **Systematic short‑chain PFAS performance**  
   - Species‑resolved short‑chain data (including ultra‑shorts like TFA where relevant) under varied FF configurations (air vs ozone bubbles, co‑surfactants, multi‑stage vs single).  
   - Integration of FF with downstream short‑chain‑focused barriers (e.g., ion exchange, NF) and rigorous summation of their combined performance.

3. **Foamate characterization and byproducts**  
   - Detailed foamate composition (PFAS speciation, co‑contaminants), including aerosolized fractions, for field systems.  
   - For electrochemical and other destructive treatments of foamate, measurement of **transformation products, inorganic fluoride, and TOF** to distinguish **parent loss** from **true defluorination**.

4. **Scalability of electrochemical oxidation of foamate**  
   - Pilot‑scale EO on real foamate (from leachate, groundwater, soil washing), with energy use, electrode life, and full mass balances.  
   - Effect of foamate organics and salts on EO kinetics and fouling.

5. **Long‑term behavior of PFAS‑loaded solids**  
   - Leaching tests and long‑term monitoring of Superloading media or other solid residuals returned to landfills [R04].  
   - Comparison with off‑site destruction in terms of risk and cost.

## 10. Practical conclusions

Based on the structured evidence:

- **Foam fractionation is best used as an intermediate concentration step**, not as a stand‑alone treatment, particularly for matrices with substantial PFAS loads (landfill leachate, AFFF‑groundwater, certain wastewaters).  
- It is **highly effective at removing and concentrating long‑chain PFAS**, often achieving ≥90–100% removal for these species at pilot and full scale [R01, R02, R03, R13].  
- **Short‑chain PFAS are consistently under‑captured**, limiting total PFAS removal and requiring additional barriers or acceptance of residual short‑chain mass in effluents.  
- **Volume reduction and enrichment are strong**, with single‑stage enrichments of tens‑fold and multi‑stage or hybrid systems achieving 10³–10⁴‑fold concentration, making FF an attractive front‑end to energy‑intensive destruction or compact adsorption systems [R05–R06, R13, R04].  
- The **overall treatment train is advantageous** when:
  - The main goal is to reduce long‑chain PFAS mass discharged,  
  - There is a clear, feasible plan for **foamate management** (destruction, secure solidification, or regulated discharge paths), and  
  - Stakeholders acknowledge and manage residual short‑chains via additional treatment or risk‑based criteria.

Without an explicit, credible foamate disposition step, FF merely **redistributes PFAS** and should not be regarded as a complete remediation solution.
