### 1. Scope of the evidence and membrane process types

Across the verified studies and project descriptions, the following PFAS‑relevant membrane processes are represented:

- **Pressure‑driven nanofiltration (NF)**  
  - Laboratory and pilot NF for AFFF-related PFAS mixtures [WEB03; WEB06].  
  - Pilot NF on AFFF‑impacted groundwater and in an NF+UV‑sulfite train [WEB02].

- **Pressure‑driven reverse osmosis (RO)**  
  - Directly compared with NF on the same groundwater and PFAS mixture [WEB02].

- **Adsorptive, amphiphilic “functionalized” membranes**  
  - γ‑AlOOH ceramic filters coated with amphiphilic silanes, operated as dynamic filtration devices removing Method 537.1 PFAS mostly by adsorption rather than sieving [WEB01].

- **Thermal membrane distillation (DCMD)**  
  - Direct contact MD with hydrophobic PTFE and PVDF flat‑sheet membranes concentrating and removing PFOA from model groundwater [WEB04].

- **Electrically assisted nanofiltration (NF + reactive anode)**  
  - Electric‑field‑assisted NF for PFOA, combining nanofiltration with electrochemical destruction near a reactive anode [WEB05].

- **Hybrid NF + UV‑sulfite**  
  - NF as a separation and concentration step followed by UV‑sulfite treatment to reduce PFAS mass in NF reject [WEB02].

All of these are **separation or concentration steps**, except the UV‑sulfite and electrochemical components, which have **parent‑compound loss (destruction)** as measured endpoints. None of the membrane steps report defluorination or mineralization metrics; PFAS are transferred to a concentrate, adsorbed, or (for electrically assisted NF) partly destroyed at the anode, but fluorine fate is not quantified.

## 2. Separation mechanisms and implications for process selection

### 2.1 NF and RO: size and charge exclusion

For the AFFF‑impacted groundwater:

- **NF**  
  - In a pilot train, NF operated in semi‑batch mode to **90% recovery** with a 10% reject stream. For most of 12 detected PFAS, rejection was **≥95%** over a 30‑day trial [WEB02‑E01, WEB02‑E02].  
  - Rejection of **short‑chain PFSAs** declined when recovery was increased from **90 to 97%**, illustrating sensitivity of the smallest, most mobile PFAS to concentration polarization and back‑diffusion at high recovery [WEB02‑E02].

- **RO (vs NF)**  
  - In controlled experiments, both NF and RO were run at the **same permeate flux (18.8 LMH) and reject flow (6.2 L/min)** [WEB02‑E04], with groundwater spiked with PFHxA, PFOA, PFBS, PFHxS, PFOS at **0.5 µg/L each** [WEB02‑E03].  
  - Under these **matched hydraulics and matrix**, RO rejection of all five PFAS was **>99% at all tested recoveries**, and was explicitly described as “superior rejection compared to NF” [WEB02‑E03, WEB02‑E04].

Mechanistically, high NF and RO rejection in this groundwater was attributed to:

- **Electrostatic repulsion** between negatively charged NF/RO membranes and anionic PFAS at pH ~7.3.  
- **Size exclusion**, particularly for longer‑chain and sulfonate PFAS [WEB02‑E07].

These are **classical sieving plus Donnan exclusion mechanisms**, not adsorption‑dominated processes.

The conference abstract corroborates that **NF can deliver >90% rejection of “common PFAS” in AFFF‑related matrices** [WEB03‑E04], and notes that NF is known to have **lower energy cost than RO** while still generating a concentrated PFAS stream [WEB03‑E01]. However, it does not provide flux, pressure or recovery, so these data are qualitative only.

### 2.2 Amphiphilic functionalized ceramic membranes: adsorption‑driven removal

The amphiphilic γ‑AlOOH filters coated with F16‑4PEG and H16‑4PEG silanes remove PFAS primarily by **fluorophilic and hydrophobic adsorption**, not size exclusion [WEB01‑E05]. Key conditions:

- **Matrix and PFAS mixture:** DI water spiked with 18 PFAS (EPA 537.1, including PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS, PFHxA, PFBS, chlorinated ether acids, etc.) at **100 ppt each** [WEB01‑E01].
- At this concentration, **>99.5% removal** (“complete removal”) was achieved for **10 of 18 PFAS** under dynamic filtration [WEB01‑E02].
- At a higher feed of **750 ppt per PFAS**, the F16‑4PEG filter showed **>95% removal for all PFAS except HFPODA**, with especially high removal for **long‑chain PFCAs and PFSAs** [WEB01‑E03].

Breakthrough tests at **~1 µg/L combined PFOA+PFOS and 1223 LMH flux** showed **30–80‑fold higher PFOA breakthrough volume vs Filtrasorb 300 GAC** under identical conditions [WEB01‑E04], indicating much **higher dynamic sorption capacity** at that flux.

Mechanism:

- Removal is associated with **C–F…F–C fluorophilic interactions** and hydrophobic interactions of PFAS tails with the silane layer [WEB01‑E05].  
- The endpoint is **removal from the aqueous phase via adsorption to the coating**, not destruction; no transformation products or inorganic fluoride are reported in the cited passages.

These behave more like **immobilized adsorbents in a membrane geometry** than like conventional NF/RO sieving membranes.

### 2.3 Direct contact membrane distillation (DCMD): phase change and volatility screening

DCMD with hydrophobic PTFE and PVDF membranes uses **vapor transport across a hydrophobic porous barrier**:

- Only volatile species (here, mainly water) traverse the pores as vapor; non‑volatile PFAS, including **PFOA at 10 mg/L**, remain in the feed [WEB04‑E01, WEB04‑E02].  
- The membranes (PTFE and PVDF, both ~0.20 µm pores) are hydrophobic; PFAS removal is achieved by **retention in the feed and concentration** as water evaporates and condenses [WEB04‑E01, WEB04‑E05].

The study explicitly focuses on **concentrating and removing PFOA from contaminated groundwater** by DCMD [WEB04‑E01]. Detailed rejection figures are not given in the excerpts, but PFAS removal is linked to membrane surface chemistry and PFAS–polymer interactions [WEB04‑E05]. Over 120 h at **60 °C with 10 mg/L PFOA**, flux decline was **about 15% for PVDF vs 43% for PTFE** [WEB04‑E03], indicating different fouling/adsorption propensities (see Section 4).

DCMD is therefore a **thermal concentration step**, not a destructive process. Non‑volatile PFAS are accumulated in a reduced‑volume brine.

### 2.4 Electric‑field‑assisted nanofiltration: coupled separation and electrochemical destruction

The Purdue technology brief describes a configuration where a **nanofiltration membrane is placed near a reactive anode** so PFAS are:

1. Concentrated at/near the membrane, and  
2. **Destroyed at the anode** under an applied electric field [WEB05‑E01].

Reported performance:

- In a water sample containing PFOA (concentration not given), **97% PFOA loss** was achieved at a **flux of 68.4 L m⁻² h⁻¹ and 30 V applied voltage** [WEB05‑E02, WEB05‑E03].  
- Under similar hydraulic conditions but **without the electric field**, “filtration alone” (NF) achieved **about 45% removal** [WEB05‑E03].

The brief explicitly states that in the electric‑field configuration a **substantial fraction of the removed PFOA is destroyed near the anode** [WEB05‑E01, WEB05‑E02]. However:

- The only quantitative endpoint is **parent PFOA loss (97%)**;  
- There are **no separate numeric destruction vs concentration fractions**, and **no measurements of fluoride release or total organofluorine**.

Thus, from an evidence standpoint, this is **combined separation + destruction**, but the membrane’s pure sieving contribution cannot be isolated quantitatively.

### 2.5 Hybrid NF + UV‑sulfite: separation followed by advanced reduction

In the AFFF‑impacted groundwater train [WEB02]:

1. **NF** concentrates PFAS into a 10% reject stream at 90% recovery [WEB02‑E05], achieving **≥95% rejection** for most PFAS [WEB02‑E02].
2. The **NF reject (10% of feed)** is then subjected to UV‑sulfite at **10 mM sulfite, pH 11.2, up to 8 h** [WEB02‑E05].

Measured destruction performance in the NF reject:

- **>75% of detected PFAS mass** destroyed after **4 h**, increasing to **>90% after 8 h** [WEB02‑E06].  
- **Energy per order (EE/O)** for parent removal:
  - **PFCAs:** ≤13.1 kWh/m³  
  - **PFOS:** 14.1 kWh/m³  
  - Some **short‑chain PFSAs:** **>100 kWh/m³** [WEB02‑E06].

This is true **parent‑compound loss**, but the excerpt does **not** report inorganic fluoride, total organofluorine, or transformation product profiles, so **defluorination and mineralization remain unquantified**.

## 3. Effects of PFAS properties, membrane characteristics, recovery, and matrix

### 3.1 PFAS chain length and headgroup

**NF and RO (groundwater, AFFF‑impacted)**

- Short‑chain PFSAs exhibited **lower rejection at higher NF recovery (90 → 97%)**, while most other PFAS remained at ≥95% rejection at 90% recovery [WEB02‑E02].  
  - This indicates that **short‑chain, highly mobile PFAS** are most sensitive to rising concentration polarization and back‑diffusion as recovery increases.
- In contrast, RO maintained **>99% rejection** for PFHxA, PFOA, PFBS, PFHxS, and PFOS across all tested recoveries at 18.8 LMH [WEB02‑E03, WEB02‑E04].  
  - The tighter RO barrier mitigates the recovery‑related loss of rejection seen with NF.

**Amphiphilic adsorptive membranes (DI water, multi‑PFAS mix)**

- At **750 ppt per PFAS**, removal **>95%** was achieved for **all PFAS except HFPODA**, with “particularly high efficiencies” for **long‑chain PFCAs and PFSAs** [WEB01‑E03].  
- This is consistent with **stronger hydrophobic/fluorophilic interactions for longer chains**; HFPODA appears to be less compatible with the coating chemistry.

**DCMD**

- Only **PFOA** at high concentration (10 mg/L) was evaluated [WEB04‑E02]. No evidence is provided for behavior of shorter‑chain or ether PFAS.

**Electrically assisted NF**

- Only **PFOA** is reported [WEB05‑E02, WEB05‑E03]. Performance for other PFAS chemistries is explicitly noted as not described in the brief.

**Implication for selection**

- If a treatment objective prioritizes **short‑chain PFSA removal at high recovery** (e.g., groundwater with substantial PFBS/PFHxS), RO or NF operated at **moderate recoveries** (≤90%) is better supported by the evidence than high‑recovery NF alone [WEB02‑E02, WEB02‑E03].  
- Adsorptive amphiphilic membranes are promising for **long‑chain PFCA/PFSA polishing at ng/L levels in low‑ionic, low‑OM waters**, but HFPODA‑like species may be exceptions [WEB01‑E03].

---

### 3.2 Membrane material and configuration

**NF/RO flat‑sheet vs hollow‑fiber**

- The NF/RO comparison in [WEB02] used **spiral‑wound‑type modules** (flat sheets in housings), but specific materials and MWCOs are not given in the excerpts.  
- The KapillO‑PFAS project shows a **full‑scale hollow‑fiber NF module** operated as a pilot at four waterworks [WEB06‑E02]. This configuration:
  - Achieved PFAS‑20 and PFAS‑4 sums below German drinking‑water limits at all sites [WEB06‑E04].  
  - Also provided **higher retention of divalent ions and higher‑molecular organic substances**, improving overall water quality [WEB06‑E05].  
  - Was **stable without antiscalant**, though pH control was needed for CaCO₃ scaling in mineralized waters [WEB06‑E04].

Laboratory comparisons of hollow‑fiber NF with two **flat‑sheet high‑pressure membranes** focused on salt and PFAS retention benchmarking, but provide no numeric performance data in the project description [WEB06‑E03].

**Amphiphilic ceramics**

- The functionalized γ‑AlOOH supports differ fundamentally from polymeric NF/RO: they act as **porous ceramic filters with adsorptive coatings** [WEB01‑E01, WEB01‑E05]. Hydraulic behavior and fouling will not mirror thin‑film composite NF/RO, and they are best thought of as **high‑performance sorbent filters**.

**MD membranes**

- Both PTFE and PVDF flat‑sheet DCMD membranes had **0.20 µm pores** [WEB04‑E05].  
- Their differing flux decline behavior under identical conditions (Section 4.2) underscores that **hydrophobic polymer chemistry and fouling morphology** can dominate long‑term performance, even at the same nominal pore size [WEB04‑E03, WEB04‑E05].

**Electrically assisted NF**

- Membrane material and MWCO are not given [WEB05]. As a result, only **relative improvement with applied field** (97% vs ~45% PFOA removal) can be interpreted, not absolute selectivity.

### 3.3 Operating flux, recovery, and trade‑offs

**Matched NF vs RO (groundwater)**

- Both operated at **18.8 LMH** and **6.2 L/min reject flow** [WEB02‑E04], enabling a strict comparison of **rejection vs recovery**.  
- Under these conditions, **RO maintained >99% rejection** for all five spiked PFAS at all recoveries [WEB02‑E03], while NF exhibited **recovery‑sensitive rejection for short‑chain PFSAs** [WEB02‑E02].  
- This provides a clean basis for selecting RO over NF when **very high rejection must be maintained at elevated recovery**.

**NF in the NF + UV‑sulfite train**

- The semi‑batch NF step operated to **90% total recovery**, producing a **10% reject** for UV treatment [WEB02‑E05].  
- This reflects a deliberate **balance between permeate volume and reject concentration**: more concentrated reject improves destruction efficiency per unit of reactor volume but increases osmotic and scaling stresses.

**Amphiphilic ceramic membranes**

- Dynamic filtration tests were conducted at modest PFAS concentrations (100–750 ppt per PFAS) [WEB01‑E01, WEB01‑E03];  
- Breakthrough tests used a very high flux (**1223 LMH**) and showed large differences in **breakthrough volume vs GAC** for PFOA/PFOS [WEB01‑E04].  
- These data are excellent for comparing **relative dynamic sorption capacity** at a single high flux, but not directly for NF/RO design, because the driving mechanism is adsorption and the flux is far above typical NF/RO practice.

**DCMD**

- Long‑term PFOA removal tests used **60 °C feed temperature** and **120 h operation** [WEB04‑E03].  
- No permeate flux values are reported in the excerpts, only **flux decline percentages**.  
- DCMD energy use is dominated by heat, so **LMH values are not directly comparable to NF/RO** energy-wise [WEB04 source limitations].

**Electric‑field‑assisted NF**

- Reported at **68.4 L m⁻² h⁻¹** and **30 V** [WEB05‑E02, WEB05‑E03].  
- No hydraulic pressure, recovery, or residence time is given, so energy requirements and mass transfer regimes cannot be compared with NF/RO studies.

**Hollow‑fiber NF pilot**

- Operated **three months per site** at four waterworks [WEB06‑E02].  
- Conditions are described as “typical of large‑scale systems”, but without pressure, flux, or recovery details [WEB06‑E04].  
- Stability “without loss of permeability” implies that any fouling and scaling were manageable under these unspecified conditions [WEB06‑E04].

---

### 3.4 Ions, organic matter, and fouling/scaling

**NF/RO in AFFF‑impacted groundwater**

- The matrix is AFFF‑impacted groundwater pre‑treated with sediment and cation exchange (per source limitations), with 12 PFAS detected [WEB02‑E01].  
- The excerpts do not quantify fouling, but high recoveries up to 97% were tested, implying that **scaling and fouling were at least tolerable** under the specific conditions.

**Hollow‑fiber NF in groundwater**

- At four waterworks with differing groundwater compositions, the hollow‑fiber NF pilot:
  - Achieved PFAS‑20 and PFAS‑4 sums below limits across all operating settings [WEB06‑E04].  
  - Ran **stably without permeability loss and without antiscalant** [WEB06‑E04].  
  - Experienced **CaCO₃ scaling** in mineralized waters, controlled by **pH adjustment** [WEB06‑E04].  
  - Provided **higher retention of divalent ions and higher‑molecular organics, and particulate removal** [WEB06‑E05].

These findings argue that **for moderately mineralized groundwaters**, hollow‑fiber NF can deliver regulatory PFAS compliance with **manageable scaling through pH control** and potential side benefits (hardness and NOM removal), without chemical antiscalant.

**NF/RO vs MD in fouling sensitivity**

- The DCMD paper notes that **NF/RO can experience up to 60% flux decline** in **high‑salinity, high‑organic matrices**, whereas MD is **less sensitive to such fouling** [WEB04‑E04].  
- In the DCMD experiments themselves, with **10 mg/L PFOA in model/synthetic groundwater**, PVDF experienced **about 15% flux decline** vs **43% for PTFE** over 120 h [WEB04‑E03].  
  - The difference is attributed to membrane surface chemistry and fouling layer morphology [WEB04‑E05].

Thus, **for high‑salinity, high‑organic feedwaters**, MD may offer **better fouling robustness** than NF/RO, though at the cost of thermal energy input and PFAS ending up in a hot brine.

**Amphiphilic ceramic membranes**

- All dynamic filtration and breakthrough tests were in **DI water or simple model solutions** [WEB01‑E01, WEB01‑E04], so **no direct evidence** is provided on performance in the presence of natural organic matter or multivalent ions. Source‑level limitations explicitly state that natural organic matter and competing ions typical of full‑scale drinking water were not represented. Therefore, **real‑water fouling and competitive sorption remain a key uncertainty**.

**Electric‑field‑assisted NF**

- The brief notes **no data on long‑term operation, fouling, or electrode stability** [WEB05 source limitations; WEB05‑E04 reference to scalability is aspirational].  
- Without those data, fouling and scaling behavior cannot be compared to NF/RO, MD, or adsorptive membranes.

## 4. When performance metrics are comparable for process selection

Given the diversity of mechanisms and conditions, comparisons are only valid under specific constraints.

### 4.1 Conditions for directly comparable rejection data

Rejection data are **sufficiently comparable** for process selection when:

1. **Same PFAS or clearly overlapping PFAS set** is tested,
2. **Same or similar matrix** (e.g., same groundwater, same synthetic solution) is used,
3. **Flux and recovery are matched or explicitly reported**, and
4. **Mechanism is the same class** (e.g., size/charge exclusion vs size/charge exclusion).

This threshold is clearly met in:

- **NF vs RO in AFFF‑impacted groundwater** [WEB02‑E02–E04]:
  - Same PFAS: PFHxA, PFOA, PFBS, PFHxS, PFOS at 0.5 µg/L each [WEB02‑E03].
  - Same matrix and pretreatment [WEB02‑E01, WEB02‑E03].
  - Same permeate flux (18.8 LMH) and reject flow (6.2 L/min) [WEB02‑E04].
  - Same class of mechanism (pressure‑driven thin‑film composites; size + charge exclusion).  
  ⇒ On this basis, the evidence robustly supports **RO selection over NF** when **maximal rejection across all recoveries** is prioritized for that PFAS set.

- **Hollow‑fiber NF across four groundwater sites** [WEB06‑E02, WEB06‑E04]:
  - Same full‑scale hollow‑fiber module and general operating regime, differing only in raw water.  
  - Outcome measured as PFAS‑20 and PFAS‑4 sums vs regulatory limits [WEB06‑E01, WEB06‑E04].  
  ⇒ Allow **within‑technology selection** (hollow‑fiber NF is technically viable across variable groundwaters), but do **not** allow ranking against RO or flat‑sheet NF energetically or hydraulically.

### 4.2 When adsorptive removal and sieving can be compared

For **amphiphilic adsorptive membranes vs GAC**:

- At **100–750 ppt per PFAS in DI water** [WEB01‑E01, WEB01‑E03], reporting **percent removal** (>99.5% or >95% for many PFAS [WEB01‑E02, WEB01‑E03]) is **formally comparable to NF/RO percent rejection**, in the narrow sense of “what permeate concentration is achieved from a given influent concentration in a short test”.  
- However, because:
  - Mechanism is **adsorption with finite capacity**, and  
  - Breakthrough and **total treated bed volumes** determine practical performance,  
  these percent‑removal numbers alone **cannot be directly ranked** against NF/RO rejection for process selection.

The **PFOA breakthrough volume comparison** (30–80× higher volume vs Filtrasorb 300 at **~1 µg/L and 1223 LMH**) [WEB01‑E04] is only suitable for **relative capacity ranking between these two sorbents** at a single, very high flux. It is **not comparable** to NF/RO in terms of permeability or energy use.

Thus, for process selection:

- Adsorptive membranes can be considered when **very high removals at trace levels in low‑ionic, low‑OM waters** are required and **breakthrough capacity is sufficient**;  
- But their **percentage removal** should not be used to conclude they “outperform NF/RO” unless **capacity, breakthrough behavior, and regeneration/disposal constraints** are explicitly considered.

### 4.3 DCMD vs NF/RO

DCMD studies [WEB04] share **the same PFAS (PFOA) and concentration (10 mg/L)** and configuration across PTFE and PVDF, but are:

- At **much higher PFAS concentrations** than typical NF/RO groundwater (µg/L), and  
- Driven by **temperature/heat**, not pressure.

Within the DCMD study, **flux decline** is directly comparable between PTFE and PVDF under matched conditions (43% vs about 15% [WEB04‑E03]) and can guide **material selection within DCMD**.

However, because:

- Energy use is thermal and not reported in detail, and  
- Fluxes and recoveries are not given in the excerpts,

DCMD results are **not quantitatively comparable** to NF/RO on an energy or productivity basis. They instead support **use of DCMD where high salinity/organics would cripple NF/RO** [WEB04‑E04] and where a **concentrated brine** is acceptable or desired.

### 4.4 Electrically assisted NF vs conventional NF/RO

The electric‑field‑assisted NF work [WEB05] demonstrates:

- **97% PFOA parent loss** at 68.4 L m⁻² h⁻¹ and 30 V, versus **~45% removal** with filtration alone [WEB05‑E02, WEB05‑E03].

But it does **not** report:

- Membrane type/MWCO,  
- Hydraulic pressure, recovery, or residence time,  
- Separate destruction vs concentration fractions, or  
- Any byproducts/fluoride.

Therefore, these data allow only a **within‑system comparison** (electric‑field on vs off) and **cannot be used to rank** electrically assisted NF against conventional NF/RO, DCMD, or adsorptive membranes on a robust quantitative basis.

### 4.5 Hybrid NF + UV‑sulfite: separating the roles

In the NF + UV‑sulfite train [WEB02]:

- NF’s role is **separation and concentration**, with **≥95% rejection at 90% recovery** [WEB02‑E02] and production of a **10% reject** [WEB02‑E05].  
- UV‑sulfite’s role is **parent PFAS mass loss**, with **>75% and >90% mass destroyed at 4 and 8 h** respectively [WEB02‑E06], and **EE/O** values for different PFAS classes [WEB02‑E06].

When using these data for process selection, it is critical to:

- Treat **NF rejection** and **UV‑sulfite destruction** as **distinct endpoints**;  
- Use NF results to size the membrane system and determine reject composition;  
- Use UV‑sulfite EE/O values to assess whether concentrate destruction is **energetically feasible**, especially noting that **short‑chain PFSAs require >100 kWh/m³ per order** [WEB02‑E06], much higher than PFCAs and PFOS.

## 5. Practical selection guidance by objective and context

Below are evidence‑based considerations for selecting among the membrane processes in the ledger, given a specified PFAS mixture, matrix, and objective. All statements are conditioned on the specific PFAS, matrices, and operating regimes reported.

### 5.1 Groundwater with mixed PFAS (including AFFF signatures)

**Objective:** Achieve regulatory compliance (PFAS‑20/PFAS‑4 sums, or µg/L‑level targets) with robust operation and manageable residuals.

- **Hollow‑fiber NF**  
  - Demonstrated to reduce PFAS‑20 and PFAS‑4 sums **below German regulatory limits** across four waterworks with differing groundwater compositions [WEB06‑E01, WEB06‑E04].  
  - Showed **stable operation without permeability loss or antiscalant**, with **CaCO₃ scaling controlled by pH** [WEB06‑E04].  
  - Added benefit of **retaining divalent ions and higher‑molecular organics** and particulates [WEB06‑E05].  
  ⇒ For distributed or retrofit applications targeting **sum‑based regulations** in groundwater, hollow‑fiber NF is a well‑supported choice, especially where **antiscalant minimization** is desired and **concentrate management** can be handled separately.

- **Flat‑sheet NF vs RO**  
  - For AFFF‑impacted groundwater with PFHxA, PFOA, PFBS, PFHxS, PFOS at 0.5 µg/L each, RO at **18.8 LMH** and comparable reject flow achieved **>99% rejection at all recoveries**, outperforming NF, which lost rejection for short‑chain PFSAs at 97% recovery [WEB02‑E02–E04].  
  ⇒ If the objective is **maximal and recovery‑insensitive rejection**, especially of **short‑chain PFSAs**, RO is the more evidence‑supported choice.  
  ⇒ If the objective allows **slightly lower rejection of short‑chain PFAS** and seeks **lower energy** (per [WEB03‑E01] qualitative comparison), NF may be acceptable, but recovery should be limited to avoid excessive rejection decline.

- **Residuals and destruction**  
  - NF and RO both produce PFAS‑rich **reject streams**. In the NF + UV‑sulfite train, **>90% PFAS mass loss in NF reject** was achieved after 8 h at pH 11.2 and 10 mM sulfite, but with **EE/O >100 kWh/m³ for some short‑chain PFSAs** [WEB02‑E06].  
  ⇒ Concentrate destruction is **technically demonstrated** but **energy‑intensive for short‑chain PFSAs**, and transformation products/fluorine fate remain uncharacterized.

### 5.2 High‑salinity or high‑organic matrices

**Objective:** Remove/concentrate PFAS where NF/RO fouling would be severe.

- **DCMD with hydrophobic PTFE/PVDF**  
  - MD is cited as capable of operating at high salinity and TOC with **reduced fouling sensitivity** compared to NF/RO [WEB04‑E04].  
  - In 10 mg/L PFOA model and synthetic groundwater, **PVDF’s flux decline (~15%)** over 120 h was far less than **PTFE’s (43%)** [WEB04‑E03], under identical conditions.  
  ⇒ For **saline or organic‑rich feedwaters** where NF/RO would experience severe flux loss, DCMD is attractive as a **concentrating step**.  
  ⇒ Within DCMD, **PVDF** is better supported than PTFE for long‑term operation under the reported conditions; both retain PFOA in the feed [WEB04‑E01–E03].

- **Caveats**:  
  - Energy demand is thermal, not directly comparable numerically to NF/RO without full heat‑balance data.  
  - PFAS end up in a **reduced‑volume brine** requiring **downstream handling or destruction**.

### 5.3 Low‑ionic, low‑NOM waters with trace PFAS (polishing)

**Objective:** Achieve very low PFAS levels (tens of ppt) in relatively clean waters.

- **Amphiphilic adsorptive ceramic membranes**  
  - In DI water at **100 ppt per PFAS**, achieved **>99.5% removal of 10 PFAS** [WEB01‑E02].  
  - At **750 ppt**, delivered **>95% removal** of all PFAS except HFPODA, with especially strong performance for **long‑chain PFCAs/PFSAs** [WEB01‑E03].  
  - Exhibited **30–80× higher PFOA breakthrough volume** than GAC at ~1 µg/L PFOA+PFOS and 1223 LMH [WEB01‑E04].  
  ⇒ For **polishing applications on low‑ionic waters**, these adsorptive membranes could be competitive or superior to GAC in capacity for certain PFAS, with minimal pressure requirements.

- **Caveats**:  
  - All evidence is from **DI or simple model waters** [WEB01‑E01, WEB01‑E04]; impact of NOM and competing anions is unknown.  
  - Performance is **capacity‑limited**, so **breakthrough and regeneration/disposal** must be addressed in process selection.  
  - They **concentrate PFAS on a solid phase**; no destruction is reported [WEB01‑E02, WEB01‑E05].

### 5.4 Coupled separation and destruction within the membrane module

**Objective:** Minimize separate concentrate handling by partially destroying PFAS near the membrane.

- **Electric‑field‑assisted NF**  
  - Achieved **97% PFOA parent loss** at 68.4 L m⁻² h⁻¹ and 30 V, versus **~45% removal** with filtration alone [WEB05‑E02, WEB05‑E03].  
  - Claims “orders of magnitude less electrical energy” than alternative destruction technologies, but without a full energy balance [WEB05‑E02].  
  ⇒ The concept is promising for **integrated separation + destruction**, but currently:
    - Only **PFOA** is demonstrated,  
    - Conditions and membrane properties are under‑specified, and  
    - Byproducts and fluorine fate are unreported [WEB05‑E01–E03].  
  ⇒ As such, **it should not be chosen over more mature NF/RO/UV‑sulfite trains based solely on these data**, but can be flagged for **future R&D and pilot evaluation**.

## 6. Key evidence gaps and research needs

Based on the structured evidence:

1. **Membrane property reporting for NF/RO:**  
   - Many NF/RO studies and pilots do not specify MWCO, surface charge, or operating pressure in the excerpts [WEB02, WEB03, WEB06], limiting mechanistic generalization.  
   ⇒ Future work should report **full membrane characterization and operating points** so that permeability and energy can be compared across sites and technologies.

2. **Short‑chain PFAS behavior at high recovery:**  
   - Only one robust data set shows **short‑chain PFSAs losing rejection as NF recovery increases from 90 to 97%** [WEB02‑E02].  
   ⇒ More systematic studies across **multiple NF/RO products and matrices** are needed to define **recovery ceilings** that maintain short‑chain PFAS control.

3. **Natural‑water performance of adsorptive membranes:**  
   - Amphiphilic ceramic filters are tested only in **DI and simple model solutions** [WEB01‑E01, WEB01‑E04].  
   ⇒ Studies in **NOM‑containing surface waters and real groundwaters**, including sorption competition and fouling, are essential before large‑scale adoption.

4. **Broader PFAS scope in DCMD and electrically assisted NF:**  
   - DCMD work focuses on **PFOA at 10 mg/L** [WEB04‑E02]; electric NF on **PFOA only** [WEB05‑E02, WEB05‑E03].  
   ⇒ Experiments with **short‑chain PFAS, perfluoroether acids, and fluorotelomer sulfonates** at environmentally relevant concentrations are needed.

5. **Fluorine mass balance in destruction steps:**  
   - UV‑sulfite and electric‑field‑assisted NF report **parent loss** and EE/O [WEB02‑E06, WEB05‑E02], but not inorganic fluoride or total organofluorine.  
   ⇒ Future studies should quantify **defluorination and transformation products** to distinguish **partial degradation** from near‑complete mineralization.

6. **Concentrate management and residual streams:**  
   - NF, RO, and DCMD all **create PFAS‑rich streams** (reject or brine).  
   - Apart from the NF + UV‑sulfite example [WEB02‑E05–E06], downstream management options are not evaluated in detail.  
   ⇒ Comparative assessments of **thermal, electrochemical, and reductive destruction of concentrates**, including byproduct toxicity and energy use, are critical for realistic process selection.

---

### 7. Summary

- **NF and RO** are well‑supported for **PFAS separation by size and charge exclusion** in groundwater. RO offers **higher and recovery‑robust rejection**, especially for **short‑chain PFSAs**, whereas NF can be acceptable at **moderate recovery and lower energy** when small‑PFAS leakage is tolerable.  
- **Hollow‑fiber NF** has demonstrated **regulatory PFAS‑sum compliance** with stable, antiscalant‑free operation across multiple groundwater sites, and should be considered a strong candidate for **groundwater pump‑and‑treat** where concentrate management is feasible.  
- **Amphiphilic ceramic adsorptive membranes** provide **very high PFAS removal and capacity** in clean waters, particularly for long‑chain PFAS, but are capacity‑limited and untested in NOM‑rich matrices.  
- **DCMD** is suited to **high‑salinity/organic feeds**, with polymer chemistry strongly affecting fouling. It concentrates PFAS in a brine rather than destroying them, and its energy profile differs fundamentally from NF/RO.  
- **Electrically assisted NF** and **NF + UV‑sulfite** represent emerging options that couple separation with **destruction of concentrated PFAS**, but current evidence is limited in scale, PFAS scope, and fluorine‑fate characterization.

For any given PFAS mixture and matrix, **process selection should be based on**:

- The **PFAS profile** (chain length, headgroup, ether vs linear),  
- The **required recovery and residual stream strategy**,  
- The **matrix fouling/scaling propensity**, and  
- Whether the primary objective is **separation only** or **integrated separation + destruction**.

Reported removal, rejection, adsorption, permeability, and energy data are only comparable, and thus only suitable for ranking processes, when these contextual factors—and the underlying separation mechanisms—are aligned.
