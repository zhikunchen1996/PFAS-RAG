## Scope and decision context

The records collectively address regeneration and end‑of‑life (EoL) management of PFAS‑loaded ion exchange resins and granular activated carbon (GAC), including:

- Physical/chemical regeneration and reuse (brine/organic solvents, hot water, supercritical CO₂, microwave, hydrothermal alkaline treatment, thermal reactivation).
- Downstream destructive treatment of PFAS-rich regenerants (UV/sulfite, SCWO).
- Full‑scale thermal reactivation of PFAS‑laden GAC.
- Leaching from spent GAC and LCA of thermal destruction vs regeneration.

All quantitative and qualitative statements below are constrained to the verified table.

---

## 1. Comparative performance of regeneration strategies

### 1.1 Ion exchange resins

#### 1.1.1 Brine/organic solvent regeneration with on‑site destruction

Liu et al. combined ion exchange (PFAS‑specific and generic resins) with regeneration using 5 wt % NaCl and 60 % v/v methanol, distillation of the waste regenerant, and UV/sulfite treatment of the still bottoms for spiked ultrapure, surface, and groundwater (total PFAS 1000 ng/L; mixed chain lengths and functionalities) [R01]. The treatment train achieved:

- Near‑100 % overall PFAS removal from the water across matrices [R01].
- Eventual defluorination during UV/sulfite treatment of still bottoms, but without reported fluoride recovery or carbon balance [R01, R01].
- Regenerated resins with PFAS removal “comparable” to pristine resins over three adsorption–regeneration cycles; no quantitative capacity recovery is given [R01].

Key points:

- **Recovered capacity and cycling**: Qualitatively preserved over three cycles; no information beyond three cycles and no numeric loss per cycle.
- **Material deterioration**: Not characterized (no mechanical/chemical degradation data).
- **PFAS fate**: Adsorption and regeneration steps are separation only; destruction/defluorination occurs only in UV/sulfite treatment of the still‑bottoms concentrate.

Godiya et al. conceptually describe a similar train (ion exchange + cyclic regeneration with high‑salinity brine + UV/sulfite treatment), reporting complete PFOS removal by UV/sulfite from regenerant [R02]. As with Liu, the record indicates:

- **Removal**: Ion exchange resins can reduce PFOS in water to below regulatory limits [R02].
- **Destruction**: Complete PFOS removal from the regenerant via UV/sulfite is reported, but without defluorination or byproduct data [R02].

The repository description explicitly notes that **regeneration efficiency of PFAS IERs is typically low and requires high‑salinity brines that generate substantial secondary waste and limit reuse over multiple cycles** [R02]. The present work investigates approaches to overcome that, but quantitative regeneration and reuse metrics are not visible.

#### 1.1.2 Influence of regenerant formulation and PFAS class

Al‑Qaraghuli et al. systematically examined batch regeneration of three anion exchange resins (two strong‑base, one weak‑base) with aqueous and organic solvent regenerants (acetone, ethanol, methanol) across multiple cycles [R03, R03]. They report:

- Regeneration effectiveness (i.e., PFAS desorption) was higher for **short‑chain PFCAs than long‑chain PFCAs**, followed by **n:2 fluorotelomer sulfonates** and **PFSAs** [R03].
- **Organic solvent regenerants outperformed aqueous** solutions; higher organic fraction improved effectiveness. At low organic fractions, acetone performed better than ethanol and methanol [R03].

Implications:

- **Regeneration is strongly PFAS‑ and solvent‑dependent.** Strongly bound long‑chain PFSAs are hardest to desorb.
- **Evidence is qualitative**: no desorbed fractions or recovered capacities are quantified, so trade‑offs among solvent types cannot yet be normalized.

#### 1.1.3 Supercritical CO₂ regeneration of resins

In a preprint, supercritical CO₂ with ethanol and HCl regenerated PFOA‑loaded ion exchange resins with **99.7 ± 0.1 % regeneration efficiency** at 75 °C and 60 min contact time [R04]. The text states this occurred **“without compromising their structure or surface functional groups, thus enabling repeated adsorption–regeneration cycles”** [R04].

- **Recovered capacity / repeated cycling**: The data support near‑complete desorption for PFOA under one condition; repeated‑cycle performance is inferred from structural integrity but not quantified.
- **Material deterioration**: The extracted record reports no compromise of structure/functional groups at those conditions [R04]; longer‑term cycling is not documented.
- **PFAS fate**: At this stage PFAS is only transferred into a PFAS‑rich regenerant stream; no destruction at the regeneration step [R04, R04].

Downstream, the same study applies continuous‑flow supercritical water oxidation (SCWO) to the PFAS‑rich regenerant and states it is **“fully mineralized”** and that the regenerant’s chemical energy enables autogenic operation, “eliminating secondary waste streams” [R04]. However:

- The endpoint is labeled as destruction in the verified record, because **no carbon‑fate or fluoride‑yield data** are provided.
- The claim of eliminating secondary waste streams is based on author wording; the record notes that the absence of persistent PFAS residuals is partly inferential.

This combined scCO₂ + SCWO concept is therefore promising but evidentially limited:

- A single PFAS (PFOA) and a single resin/condition are documented.
- No energy balance, reagent use, or multi‑cycle resin performance data are extracted.

#### 1.1.4 Field‑scale in‑place regeneration with solvent/brine

Woodard et al. report a pilot‑scale comparison of ion exchange resin vs GAC for PFOS and PFOA removal from groundwater, both operated at 5‑min EBCT [R05]. Before breakthrough to USEPA health advisory levels:

- The resin treated ~8‑fold more bed volumes than GAC for PFOS and ~6‑fold more for PFOA [R05].

The resin was then regenerated **in place** with an organic solvent + brine solution, after PFOS/PFOA breakthrough, and the pilot:

- Demonstrated **successful in‑place regeneration to near‑virgin conditions**, as judged by the regenerated resin again treating groundwater up to the same breakthrough point [R05].

Key implications:

- **Recovered capacity**: At pilot scale, at least one regeneration cycle restored effective PFOS/PFOA removal to the original bed‑volume performance.
- **Repeated cycles and deterioration**: Neither the number of regeneration cycles nor long‑term resin deterioration are reported in the examined excerpts.
- **Regenerant management**: The process clearly produces a solvent/brine regenerant containing desorbed PFAS; the record infers a PFAS‑containing stream, but its PFAS concentration and downstream treatment are not described [R05].

### 1.2 Granular activated carbon – non‑thermal regeneration

#### 1.2.1 Organic/acidic regenerants (ethanol/HCl)

Ranjbar et al. regenerated PFOS‑saturated GAC using mixed organic/inorganic regenerants. Optimization identified **0.1 mol/L HCl in ethanol, 30 min contact** as the effective composition/time [R06]. The study reports:

- The method **“efficiently regenerate[s] the PFAS‑saturated GAC” even after the fifth usage cycle** [R06].
- BET analysis shows increased surface area and total pore volume in regenerated GAC relative to PFAS‑saturated GAC, consistent with restored capacity [R06].
- The regeneration mechanism is **physical displacement and solubilization** of PFAS into the regenerant solution [R06].

From a decision perspective:

- **Recovered capacity**: Qualitatively maintained through at least five cycles; exact percentages and breakthrough behaviors are missing.
- **Material change**: Increased pore volume/surface area suggests structural modification; whether this ultimately enhances or compromises long‑term durability is not resolved.
- **PFAS fate**: PFOS is transferred into an ethanol/HCl regenerant; no destruction is claimed at this stage.

#### 1.2.2 Hot water regeneration and aeration enrichment

Wang et al. studied hot‑water regeneration of PFOS‑saturated coal‑based GAC [R07]. Key findings:

- PFOS was desorbed quickly **within 30 min** by hot water [R07].
- At **92 °C** and a **solid–liquid ratio of 0.5**, **36.7 % PFOS desorption** was achieved in a 6‑h regeneration cycle [R07].
- Over six cycles at these conditions, **>90 % of PFOS on the spent AC was desorbed** [R07].
- Subsequent **aeration of the regeneration eluent** yielded enrichment factors of **50.6–62.0** for PFOS at initial eluent PFOS 10–100 µg/L [R07], and a final concentrated solution of **2.2 g/L PFOS** after sequential sorption, hot‑water regeneration, and aeration when the influent PFOS was 2.7 µg/L [R07].

Implications:

- **Recovered capacity**: Multi‑cycle desorption mass balance is given (>90 % desorbed after six cycles), but post‑regeneration adsorption performance is not explicitly reported.
- **Material deterioration**: Not evaluated in the abstract; repeated hot‑water exposure could affect GAC structure, but no data are provided.
- **PFAS fate**: PFOS is concentrated into a small‑volume aqueous stream (2.2 g/L), with no destructive treatment described.

#### 1.2.3 Supercritical CO₂ regeneration of GAC

A separate study on “Regeneration of PFAS‑laden GAC by modified supercritical CO₂ extraction” reports that PFOA‑loaded GAC subjected to modified supercritical fluid extraction achieved **>99 % PFOA desorption after 1 h** [R08].

- **Recovered capacity & cycling**: No adsorption‑after‑regeneration data or multi‑cycle tests are reported in the snippets.
- **Material deterioration**: GAC property changes were not described in the extracted text.
- **PFAS fate**: PFOA is transferred into a supercritical CO₂/co‑solvent phase; the fate and treatment of this regenerant is not characterized [R08].

#### 1.2.4 Microwave regeneration of PFOS/PFOA‑saturated GAC

Gagliano et al. examined microwave regeneration of PFOA‑ and PFOS‑saturated bituminous and lignite coal‑based GACs, at 125–500 W and 3–12 min [R09]. Performance metrics include:

- **Regeneration efficiency (RE)** based on restored adsorption capacity.
- **Weight loss percentage (WL)** to quantify GAC consumption.
- **Changes in texture (surface area, pore volume)** via N₂ adsorption [R09].

However, the abstract provides no numerical RE, WL, or textural changes, and no cycling count.

Interpretive points:

- **Recovered capacity and deterioration**: The existence of RE and WL metrics indicates that the method can recover capacity while consuming some GAC mass and altering structure; the degree and its evolution over cycles are unknown.
- **PFAS release vs destruction**: PFAS fate (volatilization vs destruction vs condensation) is **not reported**, so microwave “regeneration” here must be treated solely as a media‑condition endpoint, not as demonstrated PFAS destruction.

### 1.3 Granular activated carbon – thermal and hydrothermal treatment

#### 1.3.1 Full‑scale thermal reactivation with PFAS destruction

DiStefano et al. report **full‑scale reactivation of PFAS‑laden GAC in a multihearth furnace with off‑gas abatement**, concluding:

- Reactivation **volatilizes and destroys** adsorbed contaminants at high temperature, restoring GAC to a “near‑virgin” state [R10].
- Data from a multihearth furnace with appropriate abatement **demonstrate high levels of destruction of PFAS compounds** [R10].
- Targeted PFAS and fluoride were measured in carbon and gas phases (PFAS in ng/g; fluoride in µg/g and ppm(v) for gas) [R10], supporting defluorination as part of destruction, though numeric extents are not provided.

Key consequences for decisions:

- **Recovered capacity**: Qualitatively, reactivated GAC is reusable; exact capacity recovery and cycle life are not reported.
- **Destruction vs transfer**: This is one of the few records with explicit PFAS destruction and defluorination indications at industrial scale (though still without full mass balances).
- **Residuals**: Fluoride remains in carbon and off‑gas [R10]. Specific PFAS or fluorinated byproducts in the gas or ash are not described in the accessible text.

#### 1.3.2 Hydrothermal alkaline treatment (HALT) of spent GAC

Soker et al. apply **hydrothermal alkaline treatment (HALT)** to spent GAC from a long‑term PFAS treatment pilot [R11]:

- HALT uses subcritical water (e.g., 350 °C, 16.5 MPa) with NaOH to **destroy PFAS** on the GAC [R11].
- Analyses of reactor liquids, GAC mass loss, and NaOH neutralization provide evidence for **removal of co‑adsorbed non‑target organics and possible GAC surface renewal via carbon gasification**, analogous to thermal reactivation [R11].
- HALT‑treated GAC was tested in rapid small‑scale column tests (RSSCTs); **BV50 > 50 000** was reported for long‑chain PFAS (PFOS, PFHxS, PFNA) [R11]. This demonstrates high long‑chain PFAS removal capacity after HALT, though the direct linkage of BV50 to a particular regeneration success criterion is interpretive.

Implications:

- **PFAS destruction**: HALT is explicitly applied to destroy PFAS on the media; quantitative destruction or defluorination extents are not in the abstract.
- **Recovered capacity & cycling**: RSSCT results show high performance for long‑chain PFAS using HALT‑treated spent GAC [R11]; however, the number of HALT cycles a given GAC batch can undergo is not reported.
- **Material deterioration**: GAC mass loss and gasification are noted qualitatively [R11]; net impact on usable life is not quantified.

#### 1.3.3 Thermal reactivation of GAC from PFAS‑contaminated landfill leachate

Sun et al. show that GAC loaded with PFAS from raw and ozonated landfill leachate can be **thermally reactivated**, and that post‑reactivation adsorption performance was evaluated [R12, R12]. The available excerpts:

- Confirm sorptive removal of short‑ and long‑chain PFAS from landfill leachate using five commercial GACs [R12].
- Indicate thermal reactivation of PFAS‑contaminated GAC and qualitative assessment of adsorption performance after reactivation cycles [R12].

However, **reactivation conditions (temperature, atmosphere, duration), PFAS destruction vs volatilization, and recovered capacities are not quantified**.

#### 1.3.4 Life‑cycle assessment (LCA) of thermal destruction vs regeneration

Lin et al. performed an LCA of thermal oxidation and pyrolysis of PFOS‑loaded GAC, with and without calcium‑based additives, and compared to regeneration/reuse [R13, R13, R13].

Key insights relevant to choosing regeneration vs destruction:

- **Without additives**:
  - **Regeneration and reuse have overall lower environmental impact than thermal oxidation** in the LCA [R13].
  - The **afterburner required to treat volatile organic fluorine (VOF)** is identified as the largest environmental burden in thermal oxidation [R13].

- **With hydrated lime additive**:
  - Hydrated lime **decreases the temperature and time required for PFAS mineralization**, reducing environmental impact in all categories [R13].
  - This is attributed to **not needing post‑processes to handle VOF and HF** [R13].

The LCA is model‑based and specific to PFOS‑loaded GAC, using prior experimental mineralization data [R13]; it does not provide new destruction or regeneration efficiency measurements, but it clarifies that:

- **Air pollution control (APC) for gaseous fluorinated products can dominate impacts** of destructive options.
- Additives that suppress VOF/HF releases can change the relative favorability of destruction vs regeneration‑and‑reuse.

## 2. Residual streams and the distinction between transfer and destruction

### 2.1 Regeneration without concurrent destruction

Many regeneration approaches **only transfer PFAS** from media to a secondary stream:

- **Brine/organic regenerants for IX**:
  - High‑salinity brines and organic solvents clearly desorb PFAS from resins [R02, R03, R03].
  - In Woodard’s pilot, an organic solvent + brine stream is produced during in‑place regeneration; treatment/disposal is not described [R05].
- **Organic/ethanol–acid regenerants for GAC**:
  - PFOS is displaced from GAC into 0.1 M HCl in ethanol [R06, R06]. The record indicates a PFAS‑containing regenerant requiring subsequent management [R06; partially supported inference].
- **Hot water and aeration enrichment**:
  - PFOS is desorbed into hot water, then enriched to 2.2 g/L via aeration [R07, R07]. No destruction is attempted; the final concentrate is a highly PFAS‑rich liquid residual [R07].
- **Supercritical CO₂ (resin and GAC)**:
  - PFOA is nearly completely desorbed from resins (RE 99.7 %) [R04] and >99 % desorbed from GAC [R08]. In both cases, PFAS is transferred into a scCO₂/co‑solvent phase; its subsequent management is not characterized.

For all such processes:

- **Calling regeneration or desorption “PFAS destruction” would be incorrect** and is avoided here by definition. They **restore media capacity but create PFAS‑bearing liquid or supercritical residuals**.

### 2.2 Regeneration linked to downstream destruction

A subset of studies explicitly couples separation with destructive treatment of the regenerant or media:

- **UV/sulfite of regenerant or still bottoms**:
  - Liu et al. treat distillation still bottoms from NaCl/methanol regenerant with UV/sulfite, reporting eventual defluorination [R01, R01]. Without fluoride or carbon balances, this can only be taken as qualitative evidence of destruction/defluorination.
  - Godiya et al. report complete PFOS removal from brine regenerant by UV/sulfite [R02]; again, fluorine fate and byproducts are unreported.

- **SCWO of PFAS‑rich regenerant**:
  - The scCO₂ preprint integrates PFAS extraction from resins with downstream SCWO, where the PFAS‑rich regenerant is described as being “fully mineralized” and enabling autogenic reactor operation [R04]. In the verified record this is treated as a **destruction** claim; mineralization is not substantiated by carbon‑fate data.

- **HALT of spent GAC**:
  - HALT is designed to destroy PFAS directly on GAC [R11], with reactor liquid products and fluoride neutralization behavior used to infer PFAS destruction and carbon gasification [R11]. Quantitative destruction extents are not in the abstract.

- **Full‑scale thermal reactivation**:
  - DiStefano et al. provide field evidence of **high PFAS destruction** during thermal reactivation of PFAS‑laden GAC [R10], coupled with fluoride measurements in solids and gas [R10].

In all these cases, **only the UV/sulfite, SCWO, HALT, and thermal reactivation stages provide destruction**; upstream regeneration steps by themselves remain separation processes.

### 2.3 Residual streams and byproducts

Across the studies, residuals include:

- **Liquid residuals**:
  - Brine/solvent regenerants with desorbed PFAS (IX and GAC systems) [R02, R06, R05].
  - Hot‑water eluates and PFOS‑enriched concentrates (up to 2.2 g/L) [R07].
  - Reactor liquids from HALT that contain non‑target organics and PFAS destruction products [R11]; specific species are not described.
  - Leachates from spent GAC in standardized leaching tests: PFOA and PFOS were measured as leaching from spent GAC using several batch procedures [R14]. This highlights that **landfilling or stockpiling spent GAC without appropriate containment can mobilize PFAS**.

- **Gaseous residuals**:
  - Thermal oxidation and pyrolysis of PFOS‑GAC generate VOF and HF that require afterburners or other post‑processes in non‑additive scenarios, which dominate LCA impacts [R13].
  - With hydrated lime additive, the LCA attributes reduced impacts to **avoiding post‑processes for VOF and HF** [R13].
  - Multihearth GAC reactivation generates fluoride in off‑gas measured in ppm(v) [R10]; other gases are not specified in the inspected text.

- **Solid residuals**:
  - Reactivated GAC with some inorganic fluoride loading [R10].
  - HALT‑treated GAC with mass loss and surface renewal [R11].
  - Microwave‑treated GAC with weight loss (WL) and altered texture [R09].

These records underscore that **media regeneration or destruction does not eliminate residuals; it transforms them**:

- **Regeneration without destruction** concentrates PFAS into brines, solvents, or CO₂ phases that must be managed as hazardous residuals.
- **Destructive options** shift the burden toward:
  - Fluoride‑containing gases and solids (requiring APC and solid waste management),
  - High energy input (thermal, HALT, SCWO) and reactive chemicals (e.g., NaOH, oxidants, sulfite).

## 3. Media durability and recovered capacity over cycles

The evidence on repeated‑cycle performance and material deterioration is fragmentary:

- **IX resins**:
  - Liu et al.: comparable PFAS removal on regenerated vs pristine resins over three cycles [R01]; no data beyond three cycles.
  - Woodard et al.: at least one in‑field regeneration restored PFOS/PFOA removal to original bed‑volume performance [R05]; long‑term cycling not described.
  - Supercritical CO₂ regeneration: structural integrity and surface functional groups reportedly preserved at 75 °C, 60 min [R04], suggesting viability for multiple cycles, but no cycle‑by‑cycle performance data.

- **GAC – solvent/acid and hot water**:
  - Ranjbar et al.: regeneration “even after the fifth usage cycle” is described as efficient [R06]; no performance decay profile is reported.
  - Wang et al.: desorption >90 % over six hot‑water cycles [R07]; post‑regeneration adsorption performance is not explicitly given.

- **GAC – microwave**:
  - RE and WL are measured [R09], but not reported numerically; the trade‑off between recovered capacity and carbon consumption is unknown.

- **GAC – HALT and thermal reactivation**:
  - HALT: GAC mass loss and gasification are reported qualitatively [R11]; RSSCT data show very high BV50 for long‑chain PFAS after one HALT event [R11].
  - Full‑scale reactivation: GAC is restored to near‑virgin state [R10]; no explicit cycle‑life limit is given.

Collectively, the **number of practically achievable regeneration/destruction cycles, and the point at which media replacement becomes preferable, remain poorly quantified** in the accessible records.

## 4. Operational, energy, chemical, cost, and sustainability evidence needs

The user’s decision problem—choosing among regeneration, media replacement, and different EoL options—requires metrics that are largely **absent or only qualitative** in the records:

### 4.1 Operating conditions and energy

For most studies, **specific energy use per unit PFAS removed or per unit media regenerated is not reported**:

- UV/sulfite systems (Liu; Godiya) lack UV dose, reaction time, and energy metrics [R01, R02].
- SCWO (supercritical water oxidation) is only described conceptually; no temperature, residence time, or energy balances [R04].
- HALT reports example conditions (350 °C, 16.5 MPa) [R11], but no energy per mass GAC or per mass PFAS destroyed.
- Thermal reactivation processes (Sun; DiStefano) lack furnace temperature profiles, residence times, or energy intensities in the inspected text [R12, R10].
- Microwave regeneration provides power (125–500 W) and time (3–12 min) [R09], but not energy normalized to GAC mass or PFAS load.

Thus, **energy comparisons are not currently evidence‑based** in this dataset.

### 4.2 Chemical consumption

Only a few regenerant and additive compositions are quantified:

- **NaCl/methanol (5 wt % NaCl, 60 % v/v methanol)** for IX resin regeneration [R01].
- **0.1 M HCl in ethanol** for PFOS‑GAC regeneration [R06].
- **High‑salinity brines** for IX regeneration (PFAS in general) [R02].
- **NaOH** for HALT [R11].
- **Hydrated lime** as an additive in thermal oxidation/pyrolysis LCA [R13].

However, volumes and doses per unit PFAS or per unit media are not provided, so:

- **Reagent intensity** (kg chemical per kg PFAS removed/destroyed) cannot be calculated.
- **Solvent recovery and reuse** (e.g., distillation of methanol, recovery of organic solvent in Woodard’s pilot) are acknowledged only qualitatively or not at all.

Given the LCA finding that APC units (afterburners) can dominate impacts [R13], **accurate accounting of oxidants, bases, solvents, and additives is essential** for any sustainability assessment.

### 4.3 Cost and life‑cycle impacts

Except for the LCA by Lin et al. [R13], **no study in the table provides explicit cost estimates**. The LCA reveals that:

- Regeneration and reuse of PFOS‑GAC (with no additive) have **lower overall environmental impact** than thermal oxidation, driven by the avoided VOF afterburner [R13].
- Hydrated lime additive for thermal treatment reduces temperature/time requirements for mineralization and avoids VOF/HF post‑processes, leading to **lower environmental impacts across all categories** compared with non‑additive cases [R13].

This provides an important directional insight but **is not a full techno‑economic analysis**. Site‑specific decisions also require:

- Opex and capex for regeneration vs media replacement and off‑site reactivation.
- Transportation distances and modes for shipping spent media to reactors or reactivation facilities.
- Regulatory costs associated with handling PFAS‑rich residuals (liquid, gaseous, solid).

### 4.4 Sustainability considerations and the review constraints

Under the task constraints, **an option cannot be labeled “sustainable” based solely on one regeneration cycle** or in the absence of energy, chemical, emissions, and secondary waste data. The current evidence base:

- Supports that **certain regeneration approaches (e.g., solvent/brine, hot water, supercritical CO₂) can restore media capacity for several cycles**.
- Demonstrates **destruction capability** for UV/sulfite, thermal reactivation, HALT, and possibly SCWO at lab/full scale.
- **Does not provide integrated energy–chemical–emissions accounting** except LCA modeling for a limited set of thermal processes with/without additives [R13].

Therefore, **no technology can be definitively labeled as “sustainable” here**. Instead, the data outline which parameters a site‑specific evaluation must obtain.

## 5. Evidence needed for site‑specific decisions

To choose among regeneration, replacement, and EoL destruction for PFAS‑loaded media at a given site, decision‑makers will need:

### 5.1 Media performance metrics

For each candidate approach and medium (IX resin, GAC, others):

1. **PFAS loading capacity and breakthrough** in the site‑specific water matrix (including co‑contaminants and NOM).
2. **Regeneration efficiency per cycle**:
   - Fraction of PFAS desorbed from media (for separation‑only regeneration).
   - Restoration of adsorption capacity, expressed as capacity ratio vs virgin media or as restored bed volumes to breakthrough (comparable to Woodard’s metric [R05]).
3. **Cycle life**:
   - Number of regeneration cycles before capacity or mechanical integrity decline make replacement or destructive EoL preferable.
   - For HALT and thermal reactivation, how many full destruction/reactivation cycles GAC can withstand.

Most existing records provide **only partial answers**:
- Woodard gives a single regeneration episode with restored bed volume treatment [R05];
- Ranjbar demonstrates qualitative effectiveness through five cycles [R06];
- Wang provides desorption mass balance but not breakthrough [R07].

### 5.2 Fate of PFAS and fluorine

To avoid mischaracterizing transfer as destruction, and to comply with emerging regulatory expectations, sites need:

- **Mass balances for PFAS and fluoride** across the entire regeneration + destruction train:
  - For UV/sulfite, SCWO, HALT, and thermal reactivation.
  - Quantification of inorganic fluoride formation (as in DiStefano et al. [R10]).
- **Byproduct speciation**:
  - Identification of organic transformation products in effluents from UV/sulfite, SCWO, HALT.
  - Speciation and capture efficiency of VOF and HF in thermal processes, per the LCA emphasis on APC [R13, R13].

Only qualitative destruction and defluorination statements are available today for most processes in this table.

### 5.3 Residual management strategies

A robust decision analysis must consider:

- For **liquid regenerants and concentrates**:
  - Whether on‑site destructive treatment (UV/sulfite, SCWO, HALT) is feasible and effective.
  - Storage, transport, and external disposal options for PFAS‑rich brines, solvents, or concentrates (e.g., 2.2 g/L PFOS solution from hot‐water/aeration [R07]).
- For **gases**:
  - Nature and volume of gas‑phase PFAS, VOF, and HF, and required APC options.
- For **solids**:
  - Disposal of ashes or reactivated GAC containing inorganic fluoride or residual PFAS.
  - Potential PFAS leaching from any GAC that is landfilled, guided by leaching behavior from spent GAC [R14, R14].

### 5.4 Energy and resource use

Site‑specific data needed include:

- **Thermal processes**:
  - Fuel or electricity demand per kg GAC or per kg PFAS destroyed.
  - Effect of additives (e.g., hydrated lime) on required temperature/time and energy [R13].
- **Chemical‑intensive regenerants**:
  - Solvent, salt, acid/base usage per regeneration cycle.
  - Solvent recovery efficiencies (e.g., methanol in Liu’s treatment train [R01]) and brine disposal options.
- **Hydrothermal/HALT/SCWO**:
  - High‑pressure, high‑temperature energy demand.
  - Chemical requirements (NaOH, oxidants, sulfite).

Only LCA modeling for specific thermal options provides any system‑level insight [R13]; **no experimental study here quantifies energy intensity**.

### 5.5 Cost and regulatory alignment

Finally, for a site‑specific choice, operators need:

- **Capital cost** estimates for reactors, UV/sulfite systems, SCWO units, HALT reactors, or thermal furnaces.
- **Operating costs**, including labor, utilities, chemicals, and disposal of residuals.
- **Regulatory constraints** on:
  - Off‑site shipment of PFAS‑laden media or regenerants.
  - Stack emission limits for PFAS, VOF, HF.
  - Landfill acceptance criteria for spent or reactivated GAC, informed by leaching studies [R14].

None of these are supplied quantitatively in the experimental records considered.

## 6. Evidence gaps and future research needs

From this structured evidence, several clear gaps emerge:

1. **Quantitative multi‑cycle performance**  
   Systematic data on capacity recovery vs cycle number (for both IX and GAC) under realistic field waters are scarce. Future work should report:
   - Capacity (or bed volumes to breakthrough) across 10+ regeneration cycles.
   - Structural/mechanical degradation indicators (crushing strength, attrition, pore collapse).

2. **Integrated mass balances and fluorine fate**  
   UV/sulfite, SCWO, HALT, and thermal reactivation should be evaluated with:
   - Closed mass balances on PFAS and fluoride.
   - Coherent accounting of aqueous, gaseous, and solid phases.

3. **Energy and chemical intensity benchmarks**  
   For each technology class:
   - kWh and kg reagent per kg PFAS removed/destroyed and per kg media processed.
   - Comparisons at equivalent PFAS loads and removal/destruction targets.

4. **Residual stream characterization and management options**  
   PFAS composition and treatability of:
   - Solvent/brine regenerants.
   - Hot‑water and aeration concentrates.
   - Supercritical CO₂/co‑solvent phases.
   - Off‑gases and ashes from thermal and HALT processes.

5. **Coupled experimental–LCA–cost analyses**  
   Extending beyond PFOS‑GAC to mixed PFAS and different media, and explicitly linking experimental performance to:
   - LCA categories (climate, toxicity, resource use).
   - Cost of ownership over media life (new media vs multiple regenerations vs off‑site reactivation).

6. **Disposal and leaching behavior of spent or partially regenerated media**  
   Building on leaching studies for PFOA/PFOS [R14], more work is needed on:
   - Short‑chain PFAS leaching.
   - Leaching after partial regeneration vs fully spent GAC.
   - Effects of landfill conditions (pH, ionic strength, DOC).

## 7. Practical synthesis for decision‑makers

Within the constraints of the current evidence:

- **If rapid deployment with minimal infrastructure is needed**, and PFAS concentrations are moderate:
  - IX resins with on‑site regeneration using brine/organics, coupled with off‑site or on‑site destructive treatment of regenerants (e.g., UV/sulfite), are supported by lab studies and a pilot demonstration [R01, R02, R05].  
  - However, secondary brine/solvent waste streams are unavoidable, and long‑term resin durability is not yet quantified.

- **If a high‑temperature facility or reactivation service is accessible**:
  - Full‑scale multihearth reactivation of PFAS‑GAC has documented high PFAS destruction and GAC restoration [R10], aligning with destructive EoL plus reuse.
  - LCA modeling suggests that, for PFOS‑GAC, **regeneration/reuse is environmentally preferable to thermal oxidation without additives**, largely by avoiding VOF afterburners; hydrated lime additives can mitigate impacts for thermal destruction [R13, R13].

- **If on‑site destructive regeneration of GAC is prioritized** (minimizing transport and off‑site liabilities):
  - HALT offers a bench‑scale proof‑of‑concept for **simultaneous PFAS destruction and GAC surface renewal** with high subsequent PFAS removal capacity [R11, R11, R11].
  - Energy and chemical intensities, and engineering feasibility at scale, are still to be demonstrated.

- **If destruction capacity is not yet available**, and operators must rely on separation:
  - Solvent/acid or hot‑water regeneration (Ranjbar, Wang) [R06, R07] and supercritical CO₂ extraction [R08] can recover PFAS into smaller‑volume liquid or supercritical streams and extend media life.  
  - In these cases, **robust interim storage and eventual destructive treatment of PFAS‑rich regenerants are critical**, as leaching from mismanaged GAC can re‑release PFAS [R14, R14].

Until the missing quantitative performance, energy, and cost data are filled in, **site‑specific decisions will require supplementary engineering estimates and, ideally, pilot testing under local conditions**, using this literature primarily to frame which regeneration–destruction pathways are technically plausible and where their major trade‑offs lie.
