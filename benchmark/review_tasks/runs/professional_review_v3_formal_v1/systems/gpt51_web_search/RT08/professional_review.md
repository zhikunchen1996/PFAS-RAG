## 1. Overview and scope

This review synthesizes bench‑scale evidence on three destructive technologies for PFAS‑containing liquids:

- Electrochemical oxidation (EO), mainly at boron‑doped diamond (BDD) and boron‑doped graphene sponge anodes.
- Non‑thermal plasma (NTP), including atmospheric and gas–liquid flowing‑film reactors.
- Ultrasonication (sonolysis), in synthetic and complex environmental matrices.

The focus is fit‑for‑purpose matching to **PFAS‑rich** streams (e.g., landfill leachate, concentrates, AFFF/cooling waters, saline brines). Parent‑compound loss is treated as *removal or transformation*, not as complete destruction or mineralization unless supported by fluorine and carbon fate data.

---

## 2. Reaction environments, feed requirements, and PFAS scope

### 2.1 Electrochemical oxidation

**Reaction environment and reactor types**

Across the EO studies, PFAS destruction is driven by high‑potential anodes (BDD films, plates, or graphene‑based sponges) operated in:

- **Flow‑through BDD reactors** treating spiked water and municipal wastewater [WEB01‑E02].
- **Recirculating BDD cells** for landfill leachate [WEB02‑E02].
- **Hydrothermal BDD reactors**, where temperature is elevated relative to conventional EO [WEB04‑E01, WEB04‑E02].
- **One‑pass flow‑through cells with boron‑doped graphene sponge anodes** [WEB05‑E01].
- **Recirculation cells with plate/mesh BDD electrodes in NOM‑amended waters** [WEB06‑E01].

These are all experimental‑scale systems; explicit scale is not always stated, but configurations and data are clearly bench‑type.

**Matrices and feed requirements**

EO has been evaluated on:

- **Synthetic PFAS solutions**: PFOA alone [WEB04‑E01], PFOA and PFOS [WEB02‑E01], and C4–C8 PFAS mixtures [WEB05‑E01].
- **Municipal wastewater containing multiple PFAS** [WEB01‑E02, WEB01‑E03].
- **Real landfill leachates**, described as PFAS‑rich, saline, and organic‑laden [WEB02‑E02; WEB03‑E01].
- **Simulated environmental waters with NOM/DOC** [WEB06‑E01].

These feeds range from simple, clean synthetic water to high‑strength leachate and NOM‑rich matrices.

**PFAS structures addressed**

- Long‑chain **PFOA and PFOS** are the most intensively studied [WEB01‑E01; WEB02‑E01; WEB03‑E01; WEB04‑E01; WEB10‑E01].
- Mixtures of **C4–C8 PFAS** were treated with the graphene sponge anode [WEB05‑E01].
- Real wastewater and leachates contained **multiple perfluoroalkyl acids (PFAAs)**, though individual species are not fully resolved in the excerpts [WEB01‑E03; WEB02‑E02].
- In NOM‑containing waters, PFAS are treated generically as a mixture; specific identities are not given [WEB06‑E01].

Overall, EO evidence spans long‑chain model PFAS and mixed PFAS profiles in realistic wastewaters.

---

### 2.2 Non‑thermal plasma

**Reaction environment and reactor types**

NTP studies use discharges in contact with PFAS‑containing water, emphasizing the gas–liquid interface:

- **Atmospheric AC and DC non‑thermal plasma reactors**, focused on PFOA degradation mechanisms and gas–liquid interface design [WEB08‑E01, WEB08‑E02].
- A **gas–liquid flowing‑film reactor** with nanosecond pulsed power in argon; plasma channels propagate along the gas–liquid interface to exploit PFAS surface activity [WEB09‑E01, WEB09‑E03].
- Other **non‑thermal plasma reactors with varying configurations and operating conditions**, compared for PFAS removal efficiency and byproduct speciation [WEB07‑E01, WEB07‑E02].

**Matrices and feed requirements**

NTP evidence is mostly for **relatively clean water**:

- Water contaminated with PFOA, PFOS, and shorter‑chain PFCAs/PFSAs [WEB07‑E01].
- Water containing only PFOA as a model PFAS [WEB08‑E01].
- Liquid water with trace‑level PFOA and PFOS (sub‑µg/L influent) in the gas–liquid flowing‑film reactor [WEB09‑E01, WEB09‑E02].

No study in this evidence set treats PFAS‑rich leachate or concentrates with NTP; the feeds are dilute and low in reported co‑contaminants.

**PFAS structures addressed**

- **PFOA and PFOS**: studied as parents [WEB07‑E01; WEB08‑E01; WEB09‑E01].
- **Shorter‑chain PFCAs and PFSAs**: both as target PFAS and as transformation products, including observed PFHxA, PFBA, PFHpA, PFHxS and others [WEB07‑E01].
  
NTP thus has been evaluated both for long‑chain legacy PFAS and for shorter homologues, but largely at low concentrations.

---

### 2.3 Sonolysis (ultrasonication)

**Reaction environment and reactor types**

Sonolysis studies employ ultrasonic treatment to generate cavitation in PFAS solutions:

- Ultrasonication of PFOA in the presence of surfactants [WEB11‑E01].
- Ultrasonication in synthetic seawater and cooling water spiked with PFAS and PFAS‑containing formulations [WEB10‑E01].
- Ultrasound treatment of PFAS mixtures in three complex matrices: groundwater, still‑bottom concentrate, and AFFF [WEB12‑E01].

Frequency, power, and reactor geometries are not visible in the excerpts, so only qualitative assessment is possible.

**Matrices and feed requirements**

Sonolysis spans a wide matrix range:

- **Synthetic seawater and cooling water** spiked with PFOA, PFOS, 6:2 FTS, AFFF, and foam fractionate [WEB10‑E01].
- **Groundwater, still‑bottom concentrate, and AFFF**, all containing PFAS mixtures and co‑contaminants such as chloride [WEB12‑E01].
- **PFOA solutions with added surfactants** (cationic, anionic, nonionic) at various fractions of their CMCs [WEB11‑E01].

These include saline systems, surfactant‑rich formulations, and PFAS‑rich concentrates, although the reported endpoints are mostly qualitative.

**PFAS structures addressed**

- PFOA and PFOS, as individual compounds and in formulations [WEB10‑E01; WEB11‑E01].
- 6:2 fluorotelomer sulfonate (6:2 FTS) [WEB10‑E01].
- Complex PFAS mixtures in AFFF, foam fractionate, groundwater, and still‑bottom concentrate; individual PFAS are not listed [WEB10‑E01; WEB12‑E01].

Sonolysis therefore has been probed across both single PFAS and complex mixtures in realistic chemical environments.

## 3. Effects of concentration, salinity, co‑contaminants, and reactor operation

### 3.1 PFAS concentration and matrix strength

**Electrochemical oxidation**

- In **synthetic PFAS‑contaminated water**, BDD EO achieved up to **99.5% PFOA removal** with about **50% inorganic fluoride release**, indicating substantial but incomplete defluorination [WEB01‑E01].
- Applying the same optimized conditions to **real municipal wastewater**, only **up to 56% of total PFAS were degraded**, explicitly highlighting matrix interferences and incomplete mineralization [WEB01‑E03].
- Hydrothermally enhanced EO targets **“high‑concentration refractory PFOA wastewater”**, where elevated temperature improves degradation kinetics and defluorination relative to conventional EO [WEB04‑E01, WEB04‑E02]. This suggests utility for very concentrated PFOA streams, though energy and kinetics are not quantified.
- The graphene‑sponge system was evaluated for C4–C8 PFAS at some concentration (not specified here). Removal efficiencies of **17–67% in one‑pass flow‑through at 230 A m⁻²** imply that single‑pass performance is moderate under those conditions [WEB05‑E01].

Taken together, EO performance declines markedly as the matrix becomes more complex and PFAS move from clean, synthetic waters to real wastewaters, even at the same nominal operating regime [WEB01‑E03]. For very high PFOA concentrations, elevating temperature can improve kinetics and defluorination, but the concentration regime is extreme and not representative of typical environmental levels [WEB04‑E01].

**Non‑thermal plasma**

- The gas–liquid flowing‑film reactor treated **sub‑µg/L influent concentrations of PFOA and PFOS** and achieved about **90% and 96% parent‑compound loss**, respectively, at 2 kHz pulse repetition frequency [WEB09‑E01, WEB09‑E02]. This indicates strong apparent degradation of trace PFAS in relatively clean water.
- Other NTP studies also used **water matrices** without reported elevated PFAS loadings [WEB07‑E01; WEB08‑E01].

Thus, current NTP evidence supports effectiveness at **trace‑level PFAS** in clean or mildly contaminated waters; there is no documented performance in PFAS‑rich leachates or concentrates in this evidence set.

**Sonolysis**

- Awoyemi et al. examined ultrasonication in **synthetic seawater and cooling water** spiked with PFAS and PFAS‑containing formulations [WEB10‑E01], and Luo et al. tested ultrasound on **PFAS mixtures in groundwater, still‑bottom concentrate, and AFFF** [WEB12‑E01]. Both highlight PFAS in strong matrices, but do not provide concentration or removal metrics in the excerpts.
- Lin et al. studied PFOA degradation efficiency as a function of surfactant addition under ultrasonics [WEB11‑E01], suggesting that PFAS concentration at bubble interfaces is modulated by co‑surfactants rather than bulk concentration alone.

Because removal extents and kinetics are not quantified, sonolysis appears promising for **PFAS‑rich or formulation‑rich streams**, but quantitative effectiveness at high loadings remains unclear in this dataset.

---

### 3.2 Salinity and ionic composition

**Electrochemical oxidation**

- Landfill leachate treated in BDD flow‑through and recirculating reactors is explicitly described as **PFAS‑rich, saline, and organic‑laden** [WEB02‑E02]. The study evaluated how composition influenced EO performance, but the accessible text does not provide quantified effects [WEB02‑E02].
- In BDD EO of municipal wastewater, **matrix interferences** substantially reduced PFAS degradation relative to synthetic solutions [WEB01‑E03]; while salinity is not singled out, this suggests sensitivity to the broader ionic and organic background.
- For the graphene‑sponge anode, **negligible chlorate and perchlorate formation** was observed even in **brackish solutions**, which is advantageous for treating chloride‑containing, saline brines or ion‑exchange regenerates while limiting oxychlorine byproducts [WEB05‑E03].

**Non‑thermal plasma**

- Mahyar et al. evaluated different NTP reactors in water and tracked byproducts like PFHxA, PFBA, PFHpA, PFHxS [WEB07‑E01]; the abstract does not discuss salinity.
- The atmospheric AC/DC systems and the flowing‑film reactor are not explicitly associated with saline feeds [WEB08‑E01; WEB09‑E01]. Argon gas is used in the flowing‑film reactor [WEB09‑E01], but water composition beyond PFAS is not specified.

Hence, the current NTP evidence is largely for **low‑salinity, clean waters**. Its robustness to seawater‑like salinity or PFAS‑rich saline brines is not documented here.

**Sonolysis**

- Awoyemi et al. explicitly targeted **synthetic seawater and cooling water** to evaluate ultrasonication performance “under realistic ionic compositions” [WEB10‑E01]. Although quantitative outcomes are not visible, the design directly addresses high ionic strength and cooling‑water chemistry.
- Luo et al. monitored transformation of **co‑contaminants such as chloride** in groundwater, still‑bottom concentrate, and AFFF during ultrasound treatment [WEB12‑E01], again reflecting attention to ionic co‑contaminants.

Thus, sonolysis has been deliberately explored for **high‑salinity and ionically complex matrices**, but the strength of evidence is qualitative (no energy or removal metrics in the excerpts).

---

### 3.3 Co‑contaminants: organics, NOM, surfactants, and AFFF

**Electrochemical oxidation**

- In municipal wastewater, matrix interferences (from organic loading and other constituents) limit PFAS degradation to about 56% of total PFAS under conditions that performed much better in synthetic solutions [WEB01‑E03].
- Energy consumption in BDD EO **increases with organic loading** and current density [WEB01‑E04], underscoring competition between PFAS and co‑contaminants for oxidative capacity.
- In NOM‑amended waters, carbon and fluorine molar balances indicated that **“missing intermediates did not contain fluorine” and that non‑fluorinated organics formed** [WEB06‑E02]. This shows that in the presence of NOM, PFAS are not simply mineralized but yield non‑fluorinated organic byproducts; the specific identities and toxicity of those products are not detailed.
- Landfill leachate EO studies acknowledge that **realistic leachate composition and electrode material govern scalability, energy use, and by‑product formation** [WEB03‑E02], signaling that co‑contaminants strongly modulate performance and residuals.

**Non‑thermal plasma**

- Mahyar et al. tracked structure‑dependent byproduct speciation during NTP treatment, including formation of PFHxA, PFBA, PFHpA, PFHxS and other PFCA/PFSA byproducts from parent PFAS [WEB07‑E01, WEB07‑E02]. This directly documents **shorter‑chain PFAS formation**, not full mineralization.
- Biondo et al. focused on PFOA in water and how **reactive species at the gas–liquid interface** affect degradation [WEB08‑E02], rather than on complex co‑contaminant suites.
- Gallan et al. worked with **relatively clean water**, with no co‑contaminant description; PFAS surface activity is exploited to concentrate PFAS at the gas–liquid interface [WEB09‑E03].

Thus, NTP studies provide more detail on **PFAS transformation pathways (shorter‑chain PFCAs/PFSAs)** than on interference by organic and inorganic co‑contaminants.

**Sonolysis**

- Lin et al. deliberately varied **surfactant type (cationic, anionic, nonionic) and concentration relative to CMC** to see how these modify “PFOA degradation efficiency” under ultrasonics [WEB11‑E01]. While endpoints are not quantified here, the design recognizes strong coupling between surfactants (e.g., AFFF components) and PFAS sonolysis.
- Awoyemi et al. tested **PFAS‑containing formulations (AFFF, foam fractionate)** in synthetic seawater and cooling water [WEB10‑E01], directly targeting co‑contaminant‑rich solutions typical of firefighting and process streams.
- Luo et al. treated **PFAS mixtures in groundwater, still‑bottom concentrate, and AFFF**, tracking both degradation efficiency and **transformation of co‑contaminants including chloride** [WEB12‑E01].

Collectively, sonolysis evidence directly addresses **PFAS‑plus‑co‑formulant systems** (surfactants, AFFF, chloride), albeit without quantitative performance metrics in the provided text.

### 3.4 Reactor operation, kinetics, and energy demand

**Electrochemical oxidation**

- In BDD EO, **current density, electrolyte conductivity, and supporting electrolyte composition** were systematically varied [WEB01‑E02]. Higher current densities improved PFAS removal but **increased specific energy consumption (kWh m⁻³)** [WEB01‑E04].
- For municipal wastewater, best‑performing conditions identified in synthetic solutions were applied directly, but PFAS degradation still plateaued at about 56% due to matrix interferences [WEB01‑E03], suggesting that simply increasing current density is not sufficient in complex matrices.
- The graphene‑sponge anode achieved **17–67% PFAS removal** for a C4–C8 mixture in one‑pass flow‑through at **230 A m⁻²**, with **specific energy consumption of about 10 kWh m⁻³** [WEB05‑E01, WEB05‑E02]. Removal was partitioned between **electrosorption and electrooxidation** via fluorine mass balance [WEB05‑E02], meaning not all removal corresponds to chemical transformation.
- In hydrothermal EO, **elevated temperature** improved PFOA degradation kinetics and defluorination versus conventional EO [WEB04‑E02], indicating that thermal conditions can be a strong lever on kinetics and possibly energy efficiency per unit destruction, though energy inputs are not quantified.
- Landfill leachate studies with low‑ and high‑boron BDD anodes [WEB03‑E01] and with recirculating flow‑through cells [WEB02‑E02] underscore that **electrode material and realistic leachate composition govern energy use and scalability** [WEB03‑E02], but specific rate constants or kWh m⁻³ are not visible.

Overall, EO offers the **richest energy data** in this set (quantified for one system [WEB05]), and explicit evidence that energy demand escalates with current density and organic loading [WEB01‑E04]. However, full kinetic models and energy‑normalized mineralization are not consistently reported.

**Non‑thermal plasma**

- The flowing‑film reactor showed strong sensitivity to **pulse repetition frequency**: maximum PFOA and PFOS parent loss (~90% and ~96%) occurred at **2 kHz** [WEB09‑E02]. Other frequencies were investigated but not detailed here [WEB09‑E02].
- Reactor design explicitly exploits the **PFAS surface activity** and gas–liquid interface (plasma channels along the interface) [WEB09‑E03], rather than relying on bulk phase reaction.
- Mahyar et al. compared **different NTP configurations and operating conditions** in terms of PFAS removal efficiency and byproduct speciation [WEB07‑E02], but energy use and kinetics are not visible.
- Biondo et al. focused on **reactor configurations to maximize useful reactive species at the gas–liquid interface** [WEB08‑E02], again emphasizing design rather than implementation metrics.

For NTP, **operational knobs are well described (pulse frequency, discharge mode, interface design)**, but this evidence set provides **no explicit kWh m⁻³ or rate constants**; thus, energy‑based comparisons to EO and sonolysis cannot be made.

**Sonolysis**

- Lin et al. varied **surfactant type and concentration relative to CMC** to assess effects on PFOA ultrasonic degradation efficiency [WEB11‑E01], capturing reactor‑matrix interactions at the bubble interface. Power, frequency, and kinetics are not reported here.
- Awoyemi et al. used two setups for **synthetic seawater and cooling water** spiked with PFAS and formulations to evaluate ultrasonication performance [WEB10‑E01], but no operating parameters or energy data are provided.
- Luo et al. evaluated ultrasound treatment of PFAS mixtures in three complex matrices [WEB12‑E01]; again, no kinetic or energy metrics are present in the excerpt.

Sonolysis thus has **no energy consumption or rate data** in this table; reactor operation insights are limited to matrix composition (e.g., surfactants, ions) rather than electrical/mechanical inputs.

## 4. Translating evidence into conditional technology‑selection criteria

Given the heterogeneous and mostly bench‑scale evidence, technology choice must remain **matrix‑, PFAS‑profile‑, endpoint‑, and scale‑specific**. Below, I outline conditional guidance for several representative PFAS‑rich liquid streams.

### 4.1 PFAS‑rich landfill leachate

**Evidence base**

- Recirculating and flow‑through BDD reactors have been applied to **PFAS‑rich, saline, organic‑laden landfill leachates** [WEB02‑E02].
- PFOA and PFOS were decomposed on **low‑ and highly boron‑doped BDD anodes** in real landfill leachate [WEB03‑E01].
- Authors of the landfill‑focused EO study explicitly note that **electrode material, realistic leachate composition, and reactor configuration govern scalability, energy use, and byproduct formation** [WEB03‑E02].

**Implications**

- **Electrochemical oxidation (BDD)** is the only technology in this evidence set with **direct data on real landfill leachate**. It can achieve parent‑compound loss for PFOA/PFOS in that matrix [WEB03‑E01], but the extent and energy cost are not quantified here.
- EO performance is expected to be constrained by **high organic loading and salinity**, analogous to the performance drop from synthetic PFOA solutions to municipal wastewater [WEB01‑E03].
- Because **short‑chain PFAS and organofluorine residues** may persist after EO (e.g., incomplete fluoride release and incomplete mineralization in other matrices [WEB01‑E01, WEB01‑E03; WEB06‑E02]), EO should not be assumed to fully destroy all PFAS in leachate without fluorine balance data.

**Technology selection**

- For **landfill leachate**, EO with robust anodes (high‑boron BDD) is currently the **best‑documented destructive option**, but:
  - Design should prioritize **electrode selection and hydrodynamics** to handle high salinity and organics [WEB03‑E02].
  - Treatment goals should be framed as **substantial parent‑compound loss with partial defluorination**, not complete mineralization, unless verified.
- NTP and sonolysis have **no direct leachate data** in this corpus, so their application to this matrix would be extrapolative.

### 4.2 Municipal wastewater and similar complex aqueous effluents

**Evidence base**

- BDD EO in **municipal wastewater** achieved up to **56% total PFAS degradation** under conditions optimized in synthetic solutions [WEB01‑E03].
- In synthetic water, **99.5% PFOA removal with ~50% fluoride release** was attainable [WEB01‑E01], emphasizing the gap between simple and complex matrices.
- EO with NOM/DOC‑amended simulated environmental water showed that **missing intermediates did not contain fluorine**, implying conversion to non‑fluorinated organics [WEB06‑E02].

**Implications**

- EO can significantly reduce PFAS concentrations in wastewater, but:
  - **Matrix interferences reduce achievable degradation** versus clean solutions [WEB01‑E03].
  - **Energy demand increases** with organic loading and current density [WEB01‑E04].
  - Transformation leaves **non‑fluorinated organics** whose risks remain to be evaluated [WEB06‑E02].

**Technology selection**

- For **secondary or tertiary treatment** of municipal or industrial effluents with moderate PFAS levels:
  - **EO is suitable as a polishing step** to substantially reduce long‑chain PFAS, particularly when combined with upstream concentration (e.g., adsorptive removal, membrane) to lower volumes processed by EO.
  - **NTP** has demonstrated strong parent‑compound loss at **trace levels in clean water** [WEB09‑E02], so it is potentially attractive as a **post‑polishing step** on already clarified, low‑TOC effluents. However, no data here confirm its behavior in typical municipal effluent.
  - **Sonolysis** in this evidence set has not been applied specifically to conventional municipal effluent; its main role appears in saline, surfactant‑rich, or concentrate matrices (below).

### 4.3 High‑salinity PFAS‑rich brines and ion‑exchange regenerates

**Relevant evidence**

- The graphene‑sponge EO system maintained **negligible chlorate and perchlorate formation even in brackish solutions**, suggesting a **low oxychlorine byproduct risk** when treating chloride‑rich brines [WEB05‑E03].
- Energy consumption for this system was about **10 kWh m⁻³** for one‑pass PFAS treatment with 17–67% removal of C4–C8 PFAS [WEB05‑E01, WEB05‑E02].
- Hydrothermal EO addressed **high‑concentration refractory PFOA wastewater** [WEB04‑E01]; elevated temperature improved degradation and defluorination [WEB04‑E02], which may be relevant for concentrated brine volumes.
- Sonolysis was tested in **synthetic seawater and cooling water spiked with PFAS and PFAS‑containing formulations** [WEB10‑E01], and ultrasound was applied to PFAS‑rich **still‑bottom concentrate** and AFFF [WEB12‑E01].
- NTP evidence does not explicitly address saline brines; the Mahyar and Gallan studies treated water without reported high salinity [WEB07‑E01; WEB09‑E01].

**Technology selection**

- For **chloride‑rich, PFAS‑concentrated brines**:
  - **EO with graphene‑sponge anodes** looks promising where **oxychlorine byproducts are a key constraint**, given negligible chlorate/perchlorate formation in brackish solutions [WEB05‑E03]. However, one‑pass removal (17–67%) at ~10 kWh m⁻³ [WEB05‑E01, WEB05‑E02] suggests:
    - Multiple passes or longer residence times would be needed for deep reduction.
    - Energy can be significant, and not all removal is destructive (electrosorption contributes [WEB05‑E02]).
  - **Hydrothermal EO** may be targeted at small‑volume, high‑concentration streams where the incremental thermal energy can be justified [WEB04‑E01, WEB04‑E02]. The absence of quantitative energy data precludes direct benchmarking.
  - **Sonolysis** has been evaluated in seawater‑like matrices [WEB10‑E01], but without energy or quantitative performance data. It could be considered where:
    - High salinity or cooling‑water chemistry makes some EO configurations unfavourable.
    - Cavitation can be efficiently generated at the relevant ionic strengths.
    - However, the lack of quantitative benchmarks and scale‑up discussion is a significant limitation.

There is **no NTP evidence** here for PFAS‑rich brines; applying plasma would require new matrix‑specific work.

### 4.4 AFFF, foam fractionate, and surfactant‑rich waters

**Relevant evidence**

- Ultrasonication in **synthetic seawater and cooling water** spiked with PFOA, PFOS, 6:2 FTS, AFFF, and foam fractionate evaluated “ultrasonication performance under realistic ionic compositions” [WEB10‑E01].
- Ultrasound was also evaluated on **PFAS mixtures in AFFF**, with co‑contaminant transformation (including chloride) monitored [WEB12‑E01].
- For PFOA with added surfactants, ultrasound **degradation efficiency was studied as a function of surfactant type and concentration relative to CMC** [WEB11‑E01], showing that surfactant chemistry strongly conditions sonolysis behavior.
- NTP and EO studies in this evidence set do not explicitly treat AFFF or foam fractionates; PFAS‑rich leachate and wastewater are covered mainly by EO [WEB01; WEB02; WEB03].

**Technology selection**

- For **AFFF, foam fractionate, and surfactant‑rich washwaters**:
  - **Sonolysis is the only technology here with explicit evidence** for these matrices [WEB10‑E01; WEB12‑E01; WEB11‑E01]. It directly addresses:
    - High surfactant loads (AFFF, cooling water).
    - High salinity (seawater).
    - Complex co‑contaminants (chloride and others) [WEB12‑E01].
  - However, lack of quantitative removal and energy data means:
    - Sonolysis should currently be viewed as **experimentally promising but not yet design‑ready** for full‑scale AFFF/FF destruction.
    - Integration with upstream separation (e.g., foam fractionation) and downstream polishing may be necessary.
  - **EO** could be applied to AFFF‑derived concentrates, but this is extrapolation; the closest analogs are NOM‑containing waters and landfill leachate [WEB01; WEB02; WEB03; WEB06], where organic load increases energy demand and leaves non‑fluorinated organics [WEB01‑E04; WEB06‑E02].

NTP has not been demonstrated on AFFF in the provided evidence, and its performance with high surfactant loads is unknown here.

### 4.5 Groundwater and dilute complex matrices

**Relevant evidence**

- Ultrasound was evaluated for PFAS mixtures in **groundwater**, with co‑contaminant transformation (e.g., chloride) tracked [WEB12‑E01].
- NTP in a flowing‑film reactor obtained about **90% PFOA and 96% PFOS parent‑compound loss at sub‑µg/L** influent concentrations in relatively clean water [WEB09‑E01, WEB09‑E02].
- Non‑thermal plasma also treated PFOA, PFOS and shorter‑chain PFCAs/PFSAs in water, tracking formation of specific shorter‑chain byproducts [WEB07‑E01].
- EO with BDD in synthetic and municipal waters achieved high PFOA removal and partial defluorination [WEB01‑E01, WEB01‑E03], and graphene‑sponge EO was tested in aqueous electrolytes (including brackish solutions) at bench scale [WEB05‑E01, WEB05‑E03].

**Technology selection**

- For **relatively clean groundwater with trace PFAS**, after conventional treatment:
  - **NTP** in gas–liquid flowing‑film reactors is highly promising for **polishing**:
    - High parent‑compound removal at sub‑µg/L in clean water [WEB09‑E02].
    - Reactor design that concentrates PFAS at the interface [WEB09‑E03].
    - But transformation products and defluorination are uncharacterized in the excerpt; observed degradation is parent‑loss only [WEB09‑E01, WEB09‑E02].
  - **EO** (BDD or graphene‑sponge) also works well on relatively clean waters, achieving high PFOA removal and substantial defluorination in synthetic matrices [WEB01‑E01] or moderate removal with known energy demand [WEB05‑E01, WEB05‑E02]. It has the advantage of:
    - Some fluorine‑balance data (partial defluorination) [WEB01‑E01].
    - But higher energy and more complex reactors than some NTP options may be required, depending on design.
  - **Sonolysis** has groundwater data [WEB12‑E01], but without quantitative performance or energy metrics, and may be better reserved for complex concentrates or surfactant‑rich cases where NTP/EO are less tested.

In dilute, clean matrices, both **NTP and EO** are plausible polishing options; selection will hinge on site‑specific energy, infrastructure, and byproduct considerations, rather than on unequivocal performance differences in this evidence set.

## 5. Cross‑technology trade‑offs, residuals, and evidence gaps

### 5.1 Parent loss vs defluorination and mineralization

Across all three technologies:

- **Parent‑compound loss is often high**, but:
  - BDD EO on PFOA achieved **99.5% removal yet only ~50% fluoride release** [WEB01‑E01], directly demonstrating significant residual organofluorine (intermediates or residual PFAS).
  - In NOM‑containing waters, carbon and fluorine balances showed that **missing intermediates were non‑fluorinated**, implying cleavage of C–F bonds but also formation of **non‑fluorinated organic byproducts** [WEB06‑E02]. While authors interpret this as “partial mineralization”, the data do not explicitly resolve inorganic carbon fate.
  - NTP studies observed **formation of shorter‑chain PFAS** (e.g., PFHxA, PFBA, PFHpA, PFHxS) [WEB07‑E01], indicating stepwise transformation rather than full mineralization.
  - NTP flowing‑film and atmospheric plasma studies, as well as sonolysis studies in this corpus, do **not report fluoride release or total organofluorine changes**, so destruction beyond parent‑loss cannot be confirmed [WEB08‑E01; WEB09‑E01, WEB09‑E02; WEB10‑E01; WEB11‑E01; WEB12‑E01].

Accordingly, none of the technologies should be treated as achieving **complete defluorination or mineralization by default**. Technology selection must recognize that **residual organofluorine and transformation products are expected**, and monitoring must be designed accordingly.

### 5.2 Energy and scale‑up constraints

- **EO**:
  - Provides the **only quantitative energy data** in this set: ~**10 kWh m⁻³** for 17–67% PFAS removal in a graphene‑sponge system [WEB05‑E02].
  - Explicitly shows **energy increasing with current density and organic loading** [WEB01‑E04].
  - Landfill leachate studies stress that **electrode material and realistic composition govern scalability and energy use** [WEB03‑E02].
- **NTP**:
  - No explicit kWh m⁻³ values are available here. Optimization around **pulse repetition frequency** (e.g., 2 kHz) is reported [WEB09‑E02] but without an energy basis.
  - Reactor designs are still laboratory configurations focused on gas–liquid interface optimization [WEB08‑E02; WEB09‑E03].
- **Sonolysis**:
  - No energy or rate data are visible in these records.
  - Scale is not specified for most ultrasound studies; descriptions point to experimental setups, not engineered full‑scale reactors [WEB10‑E01; WEB11‑E01; WEB12‑E01].

Because energy is poorly quantified for NTP and sonolysis, **no technology can be declared globally superior on an energy basis**. EO is the only option for which energy demand can be roughly gauged, and even there, values are limited to specific bench‑scale conditions.

### 5.3 Residual streams and byproducts

- **EO**:
  - Incomplete fluoride release and statements of incomplete mineralization in BDD reactors [WEB01‑E01, WEB01‑E03] imply accumulation of PFAS intermediates and non‑fluorinated organics.
  - NOM‑containing waters produce **non‑fluorinated organic byproducts** [WEB06‑E02].
  - Landfill leachate studies note that **byproduct formation is governed by electrode material and composition** [WEB03‑E02], but do not enumerate specific products here.
  - Graphene‑sponge EO shows **negligible chlorate and perchlorate formation**, reducing concern about oxychlorine residuals in chloride‑rich matrices [WEB05‑E03].
- **NTP**:
  - Mahyar et al. documented **shorter‑chain PFCAs/PFSAs as byproducts** [WEB07‑E01, WEB07‑E02], including PFHxA, PFBA, PFHpA, PFHxS.
  - Gallan et al. and Biondo et al. do not report fluorine balances or byproduct suites in the excerpts; residual PFAS and organofluorine likely remain [WEB08‑E01, WEB08‑E02; WEB09‑E01, WEB09‑E02].
- **Sonolysis**:
  - Luo et al. monitored **transformation of co‑contaminants such as chloride** in complex matrices [WEB12‑E01], but PFAS byproducts are not detailed.
  - Lin et al. and Awoyemi et al. do not report fluorine or byproduct data in the accessible text [WEB10‑E01; WEB11‑E01].

Thus, **all three technologies generate residual streams** whose composition is incompletely characterized. For PFAS‑rich applications, any deployment should be coupled with:

- Organofluorine mass balances.
- Targeted analysis of short‑chain PFAS and key co‑contaminant derivatives.
- Assessment of non‑fluorinated organics for toxicity.

### 5.4 Key evidence gaps and research needs

Across technologies:

- **Comparable energy metrics** (kWh m⁻³ or kWh per log‑removal) in realistic matrices are largely absent, especially for NTP and sonolysis.
- **Defluorination and carbon fate** are rarely quantified for NTP and sonolysis; even for EO they are incomplete.
- **Complex PFAS mixtures and rich matrices** (AFFF, still‑bottom concentrate, PFAS‑rich brines) have been probed by sonolysis and EO, but almost exclusively with **qualitative endpoints**.
- **Reactor scale and long‑term stability** (electrode fouling, plasma source durability, ultrasonic transducer performance) are not reported in the excerpts, limiting practical implementation guidance.

Priority research directions include:

1. **Head‑to‑head comparisons** of EO, NTP, and sonolysis on the **same PFAS‑rich matrices** (e.g., landfill leachate, AFFF concentrate, ion‑exchange regenerate), with:
   - Standardized endpoints (parent loss, organofluorine, fluoride, key byproducts).
   - Energy‑normalized performance.
2. **Detailed fluorine and carbon mass balances** for NTP and sonolysis comparable to those used in EO studies [WEB01‑E01, WEB06‑E02].
3. **Matrix‑resolved NTP and sonolysis work** in high‑salinity, surfactant‑rich streams (extending beyond the existing qualitative sonolysis and NTP water studies) [WEB07‑E01; WEB08‑E01; WEB10‑E01; WEB12‑E01].
4. **Scale‑up demonstrations** (pilot level) for at least one technology per matrix class, with multi‑month stability and fouling assessment.

## 6. Summary: Matching technologies to PFAS‑rich liquids

Within the limitations of the available evidence:

- **Electrochemical oxidation** is best supported for:
  - **PFAS‑rich landfill leachate** and **municipal/industrial wastewaters**, where BDD anodes can achieve significant parent PFAS loss but are constrained by organic loading, salinity, and energy demand [WEB01; WEB02; WEB03].
  - **Brackish, chloride‑rich brines**, where graphene‑sponge EO offers PFAS removal with negligible oxychlorine byproducts [WEB05‑E01, WEB05‑E03].
  - Streams where at least **partial fluorine balance and organofluorine fate** must be characterized.

- **Non‑thermal plasma** is most compelling for:
  - **Trace PFAS in relatively clean groundwater or treated effluents**, where flowing‑film reactors have achieved high parent‑compound loss at sub‑µg/L concentrations [WEB09‑E02].
  - Situations where **shorter‑chain PFAS formation and structure‑dependent pathways** are of research interest [WEB07‑E01, WEB07‑E02].
  - At present, there is no direct evidence here for its performance on PFAS‑rich leachates, brines, or AFFF.

- **Sonolysis** appears particularly relevant for:
  - **Saline and surfactant‑rich matrices** (synthetic seawater, cooling water) and **PFAS‑rich formulations** (AFFF, foam fractionate, still‑bottom concentrates) [WEB10‑E01; WEB12‑E01].
  - Systems where **surfactant chemistry** and **ionic composition** dominate PFAS interfacial behavior [WEB11‑E01].
  - However, its quantitative performance and energy cost in these matrices remain largely undefined in this evidence set.

No technology emerges as **universally superior**; instead, selection should be conditioned on:

- **Matrix complexity and salinity** (EO and sonolysis have direct evidence in complex high‑strength matrices; NTP mainly in clean waters).
- **PFAS speciation and concentration** (NTP and EO are more established at trace to moderate levels; hydrothermal EO and sonolysis target higher concentrations).
- **Energy constraints and residual‑stream management** (EO offers some energy benchmarks and partial fluorine balances; NTP and sonolysis need more data).

Designers and regulators should therefore treat EO, NTP, and sonolysis as **complementary tools** within PFAS treatment trains, rather than competing stand‑alone solutions, and explicitly tailor technology choice to the PFAS‑rich stream characteristics documented above.
