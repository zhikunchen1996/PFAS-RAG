### 1. Scope and framing

Evidence across the table consistently shows that high-pressure membranes (NF/RO and CCRO-type systems) are very effective PFAS separators, not PFAS solutions. They:

- Concentrate PFAS into small-volume liquid streams (brines, still bottoms) or onto solids (filters, sorbents, and the membranes themselves), and  
- Require **downstream management**—concentration, destruction, recycle, or disposal—to determine the true outcome of a treatment train.

Below, I focus on:

1. How recovery, concentration factor, PFAS composition, and co‑contaminants define the membrane-derived retained stream.
2. How downstream options (concentration, destruction, recycle, disposal, hybrid trains) compare, given **residual volume, composition, endpoints, and energy/cost information actually available**.
3. How these residual-management choices change the net energy, cost, environmental burden, and practical value of membrane separation.

## 2. Characterizing membrane-derived PFAS concentrates

### 2.1. High-recovery NF/RO and CCRO concentrates

**Closed-circuit membrane filtration (CCMF) with NF/RO**

A pilot-scale closed-circuit NF/RO system treating water spiked with nine PFAAs demonstrates how recovery and membrane tightness shape the retained stream [WEB03]:

- Water recovery was pushed up to **97%** [WEB03], so only ~3% of influent volume emerges as feed–retentate.
- Overall PFAA rejection at 97% recovery exceeded **98.3%** for NF90, CR100, and SW30 membranes [WEB03]. This indicates that nearly all PFAA mass is routed into the retentate, not destroyed.
- PFOS in the feed–retentate increased from **about 0.132 mg/L at 90% recovery** to **about 0.382 mg/L at 97% recovery** [WEB03]. This illustrates progressive enrichment of long‑chain PFAAs as water is removed.
- Specific energy at 97% recovery ranged from **0.14 kWh/m³ (loose NF)** to **0.57 kWh/m³ (tight RO)** [WEB03], directly tying the concentration factor to energy demand.

A closed-circuit NF/RO groundwater pilot treating aqueous film‑forming foam (AFFF)-impacted groundwater (total PFAS around **14.3 µg/L** in the feed) achieved:

- **97.9–99% NF rejection** and  
- **>99% RO rejection** across PFAS, with recoveries of 80–90% [WEB05].

Here again, PFAS are efficiently transferred into a smaller volume concentrate, but the accessible text provides **no quantitative concentrate composition or volume**, so the degree of enrichment must not be inferred.

**Closed-circuit high-pressure membranes and concentration factor**

An AMTA fact sheet on a full-scale closed-circuit NF/RO desalination pilot reports:

- Operation at **97% recovery** [WEB04].
- Tight NF and RO membranes concentrated “PFAS streams by a factor of **33** at 97% recovery” while maintaining very low permeate PFAA concentrations [WEB04].

Here, “concentration factor 33” is explicitly tied to high recovery and tight membranes, but feed and concentrate compositions, co‑contaminants, and pressures are not quantified [WEB04]. That omission limits energy and risk assessment but clearly demonstrates that **recovery and membrane tightness jointly define the retained stream volume and concentration**.

**Takeaway:** For liquid concentrates from NF/RO/CCRO:

- **Recovery** determines **residual volume** (e.g., 3% of influent at 97% recovery).
- **Concentration factor** (e.g., ~33× [WEB04] or ~30,000× for IX still bottoms, see below) determines PFAS intensity.
- **PFAS composition** (e.g., PFOS enrichment in the retentate [WEB03]) influences downstream treatability and toxicity.
- **Co‑contaminants** and ionic strength (inferred in high-recovery pilots [WEB03]) shape both downstream process feasibility and energy demand.

### 2.2. Ion-exchange still bottoms as “super-concentrates”

While not membrane-derived per se, IX still bottoms provide a useful extreme case of PFAS enrichment relevant to membrane trains that use IX polishing:

- After IX regeneration and distillation, **45 L** of still bottom contained **~640 mg/L total PFAS** [WEB11].
- This corresponded to an **overall concentration factor of about 30,000** relative to the original water [WEB11].

This is much higher than the factor of 33 reported for closed-circuit NF/RO at 97% recovery [WEB04]. It illustrates that **coupling membranes with regeneration/distillation steps can push PFAS into ultra-concentrated liquid streams**, which are attractive for destruction (lower volume) but extremely challenging environmentally if mishandled.

### 2.3. RO concentrates in potable reuse and bioelectrochemical treatment

A U.S. Bureau of Reclamation pilot evaluating CCRO and FO-based alternatives for RO concentrate management in potable reuse:

- Sampled **RO concentrate and treated streams** and analyzed PFAS by EPA 537.1 together with ethylene glycol, semi-volatile organics, and priority pollutants [WEB06].
- Evaluated “different recovery conditions” [WEB06].

Although no PFAS concentrations or concentration factors are provided, this study is important in showing that **PFAS in RO concentrate are being treated explicitly as a co‑contaminant suite alongside other organics** [WEB06]. It reinforces that **concentrate composition is multi-contaminant**, not PFAS-only, which is essential for downstream technology selection.

A pilot microbial fuel cell (MFC) treating RO concentrate from a UF+CCRO water reuse train shows:

- RO concentrate with PFOS **31.7 ng/L**, PFOA **4.6 ng/L**, and PFHxA **12.3 ng/L**; other PFAS were below detection [WEB02].
- The MFC achieved **partial PFAS removal** while generating electrical power from organics [WEB02].

Here, the retained stream has **low ng/L PFAS**, but meaningful organic content. PFAS removal is qualitative (no % removal given) and the PFAS fate (sorption vs transformation) is not resolved [WEB02], so the MFC must be viewed as a **brine-conditioning and power-recovery step**, not a quantified PFAS solution.

### 2.4. Co-contaminants in RO concentrates

The UV-advanced reduction study treating RO concentrate (ROC) reports a matrix with:

- **High dissolved organic carbon, nitrate, and carbonate** [WEB01].

The ROC was **spiked with mg/L PFOS, PFBS, PFOA, PFBA** to test defluorination under UV-ARP [WEB01]. This underscores that:

- The same concentrate that carries PFAS also carries **organics and inorganics that can compete for radicals, inhibit treatment, or change process design.**

The Reclamation report similarly analyzed ethylene glycol, semi-volatile organics, and priority pollutants alongside PFAS [WEB06], reinforcing that any downstream step must be specified for a **multi-component brine**, not PFAS in isolation.

## 3. PFAS retained on membranes and other solids

### 3.1. PFAS leaching and inventory in RO membranes

Ekande et al. characterize PFAS embedded in commercial RO membranes and their potential to leach [WEB10]:

- Bench leaching of 40 membrane fragments (1 cm² each) in 35 mL Milli‑Q water for 48 h sonication yielded:
  - Several PFAS, including PFOS and PFOA, with **surface-normalized loads of about 17–38 pg/cm²** [WEB10].
- A **direct TOPA** assay on membrane pieces yielded **up to 16× higher PFOS mass per cm²** compared with simple leaching [WEB10], indicating substantial oxidizable or non-leachable PFAS within the polymer.
- Scenario extrapolation to a **40 m² RO element** estimated **potential ΣPFAS release on the order of tens of milligrams per element over its lifetime** [WEB10].

Critically:

- These values reflect **PFAS stored in the membrane polymer**, not PFAS from the water.
- TOPA represents a **worst-case oxidizable PFAS inventory**, not in‑situ operating release [WEB10].

From a residual-management perspective:

- **New or spent RO elements are PFAS-containing solid wastes.** Even if leached PFAS masses per cm² are low, the element-scale inventory (tens of mg ΣPFAS per element) is non‑trivial [WEB10].
- Routine leaching and oxidative cleaning could mobilize part of this inventory toward permeate or brine, or leave PFAS locked into the polymer until end‑of‑life.

### 3.2. Sorption of PFAS to filters and other treatment materials

In the AFFF-impaired groundwater pilot [WEB05], PFAS sorption to solid materials in the treatment train was quantified:

- **PFOS represented 60–85% of total PFAS sorbed** across evaluated treatment materials [WEB05].
- Solid-phase PFAS concentration on **0.2 µm filters was roughly ten times higher** than on any other media [WEB05].

These results show that:

- Prefilter cartridges (0.2 µm) and similar materials can act as **significant PFAS sinks**, especially for PFOS.
- Such solids must be included in the PFAS **mass balance** and treated as residuals requiring some form of management, not as negligible byproducts.

### 3.3. Spent sorbents and SCWO destruction

A Chemosphere study applies SCWO to **spent GAC and anion exchange (AIX) media with sorbed PFAS** [WEB08]:

- SCWO is used “to destroy both the spent media and the PFAS adsorbed onto it” [WEB08].
- The abstract confirms application to PFAS captured on GAC and AIX from prior treatment [WEB08], but **does not provide destruction efficiencies, fluorine balance, or byproducts** in the accessible text.

This study does not involve membrane concentrates directly, but it demonstrates:

- SCWO can be considered for **solid residuals** from membrane-based trains (e.g., downstream GAC or AIX, or potentially PFAS‑containing prefilters).

## 4. Downstream options for membrane-derived concentrates and solids

Here I compare the main downstream management strategies in the evidence, being explicit about:

- **Residual volume/composition**.
- **Endpoints** (separation vs defluorination vs destruction vs sorption).
- **Energy/cost and environmental implications** where supported.

### 4.1. Further concentration and hybrid membrane trains

**Closed-circuit high-pressure NF/RO (liquid concentration)**

- Closed-circuit membranes at 97% recovery produce high concentration factors (up to **33×** for PFAS [WEB04]) with specific energy **0.14–0.57 kWh/m³** depending on membrane tightness [WEB03].
- High recoveries further **lower the volume** of PFAS-rich concentrate but proportionally **increase PFAS concentrations** (PFOS ~0.382 mg/L at 97% recovery vs ~0.132 mg/L at 90% [WEB03]).

Energy trade-offs:

- Within the NF/RO class, **looser NF** achieves lower specific energy per m³ product (0.14 kWh/m³) than tight RO (0.57 kWh/m³) at the same 97% recovery [WEB03], but retention of smaller or more polar PFAS may differ (species detail not fully captured here).
- Between NF/RO and IX-distillation: IX still bottoms can achieve concentration factors of **∼30,000×** [WEB11], far exceeding the 33× of CCRO membranes [WEB04]. This dramatically reduces destruction volume but shifts the burden to an energy-intensive regeneration/distillation step (energy not quantified in the record).

Implications:

- **Concentration-only strategies** (closed-circuit NF/RO, IX+distillation) *improve the leverage* of expensive destruction technologies by shrinking volume and raising PFAS concentration.
- However, they also produce **higher-hazard residuals** that demand robust containment and destruction, not disposal-in-kind.

**Bioelectrochemical polishing (MFCs)**

- The MFC pilot treats RO concentrate with PFOS 31.7 ng/L, PFOA 4.6 ng/L, PFHxA 12.3 ng/L, achieving **partial PFAS removal** while recovering electric power from organics [WEB02].
- No quantitative PFAS reduction or PFAS mass fate is reported, and energy recovery metrics are unavailable in the accessible text [WEB02].

Role in treatment trains:

- With current evidence, MFCs are best considered as **supplementary brine treatment** that can reduce organic load and generate some power, but they **cannot be counted as a terminal PFAS solution** without destruction or mass-balance data.

**CCRO/FO alternatives in potable reuse**

- The Reclamation CCRO/FO pilot characterized PFAS and other contaminants in RO concentrate and treated streams under varying recovery [WEB06].
- No performance or energy numbers are available, so FO and CCRO can only be described qualitatively as **alternative concentration and water-recovery steps**.

**Summary of concentration-stage trade-offs**

- **End point**: All these options (CCMF, CCRO, FO, IX+distillation, MFC) primarily change **where PFAS resides** and at what concentration; none provide quantified, terminal destruction in the evidence set.
- **Energy**: Among membranes, high recovery drives up specific energy [WEB03]. Additional steps (distillation, FO) will introduce further energy burdens not quantified here.
- **Environmental burden**: Higher concentration factors (e.g., 30,000× for IX still bottoms) reduce destruction footprint but **raise accidental-release consequences** if containment fails.

### 4.2. Chemical/photochemical treatment of concentrates

**UV/sulfite advanced reduction on ROC**

Fennell et al. treated ROC spiked with mg/L PFOS, PFBS, PFOA, PFBA using:

- Hardness removal + UV/persulfate preoxidation, followed by **24 h UV/sulfite**, yielding **>90% PFAS defluorination** [WEB01].

Key points:

- The measured endpoint is **defluorination**, not full mineralization. No carbon-fate or complete mass-balance data are reported in the abstract [WEB01].  
- The ROC contained high DOC, nitrate, and carbonate [WEB01], indicating treatment was performed on a *realistic, co-contaminant-rich* concentrate but under **lab-scale conditions** (scale not reported; any “likely bench” inference is not supported).
- Energy and reagent consumption are not reported.

Implications for treatment trains:

- UV-ARP can effect substantial **chemical transformation of PFAS in ROC** with a measured indication (defluorination).  
- Without mineralization data or byproduct characterization, this should be viewed as **destructive transformation of parent compounds**, but not yet as demonstrated full destruction/mineralization.
- Given the high ROC organic and inorganic content [WEB01], similar brines from NF/RO systems likely require **pretreatment (e.g., hardness removal, preoxidation)**, adding complexity and cost.

### 4.3. Thermal/SCWO destruction of concentrates

**SCWO on concentrated PFAS waste (matrix-agnostic)**

McDonough et al. field-validated **SCWO on a concentrated waste stream containing 12 perfluoroalkyl acids**, with:

- Liquid and gaseous effluents characterized per EPA 537.1 and OTM‑45 [WEB07].
- No quantitative destruction efficiencies in the accessible text.

Importantly, the discussion states:

- “An appropriate treatment strategy for PFAS-impacted waste includes multiple technologies in series to concentrate PFAS into the smallest possible volume for energy-intensive destruction” [WEB07].

This is conceptually aligned with using **membranes or IX/distillation upstream** of SCWO, even though the specific concentrate origin was not identified as membrane-derived [WEB07].

**Field deployment: RO + SCWO at Peterson Space Force Base**

A Journal of Environmental Engineering paper reports:

- SCWO treating PFAS-impacted aqueous streams, with **RO used upstream to concentrate PFAS and increase SCWO efficiency** [WEB12].
- The study notes that concentrating PFAS by RO prior to SCWO **reduces overall energy use and reactor volume requirements** [WEB12].
- No numerical recovery, concentration factors, or destruction efficiencies are reported.

This is direct evidence that, in practice:

- **RO is used as a PFAS concentrator feeding SCWO**, and
- **Concentrate volume reduction translates into lower SCWO energy and capital (reactor volume)**, even if the magnitude cannot be quantified from the abstract.

**SCWO for spent media and model solutions**

- The Chemosphere study on GAC/AIX [WEB08] and a Chemical Engineering Journal study on PFOS solutions [WEB09] both present SCWO as an **effective destruction technology for PFOS and other PFAS**, but accessible text lacks quantitative operating or performance metrics [WEB08, WEB09].

Taken together:

- SCWO is consistently treated as a **terminal destruction step** for liquid or solid PFAS-rich residuals.
- Membrane concentration (RO, CCRO) is explicitly positioned as a **pre‑treatment to reduce SCWO volume and energy** [WEB12], in line with the conceptual recommendation to concentrate PFAS to “the smallest possible volume” before energy-intensive destruction [WEB07].

### 4.4. Plasma-based destruction with RO polishing

The plasma vortex study on IX still bottoms with RO polishing demonstrates a different hybrid approach [WEB11]:

- A distilled still bottom (**45 L, ~640 mg/L total PFAS**) represents an **overall ~30,000× concentration factor** [WEB11].
- The still bottom is subjected to plasma vortex “destruction” (extent not quantified in the abstract) [WEB11].
- The **treated still bottom is further polished by RO**, and **RO retentate is recycled back to the plasma unit**, forming a **closed loop with no liquid discharge** [WEB11].

This architecture shows:

- How **membrane polishing can be integrated within a destructive loop** to ensure that any PFAS surviving destruction is recaptured and re-treated.
- The **liquid discharge endpoint is permeate from RO applied to the already-treated brine**, while PFAS-enriched streams are internally recycled.

Energy and cost:

- No energy-use or cost data are reported for either plasma or RO [WEB11].
- Conceptually, repeated recirculation should increase energy demand but reduce environmental risk of PFAS discharge, at the expense of complex operations.

### 4.5. Management of PFAS-containing membranes and solids

**SCWO of sorptive solids**

As noted, SCWO can destroy **both spent sorbents (GAC, AIX) and sorbed PFAS** [WEB08], offering a single-step route for solid residuals that may sit downstream of membrane trains.

**Membranes as PFAS residuals**

Based on Ekande et al. [WEB10]:

- Commercial RO elements inherently contain PFOS, PFOA, and other PFAS, with **leachable loads of ~17–38 pg/cm²** and much larger oxidizable inventories (up to 16× leaching) [WEB10].
- Lifetime ΣPFAS release from a 40 m² element is **modeled at tens of mg** [WEB10].

Thus:

- Used RO elements should be treated as **PFAS-bearing residuals** requiring controlled disposal or destruction (e.g., high-temperature or SCWO, where feasible), not as innocuous plastic waste.

**Filters and ancillary materials**

- 0.2 µm filters in the AFFF groundwater pilot accumulated PFAS at **~10× higher solid-phase concentrations** than other media, with PFOS constituting 60–85% of sorbed PFAS [WEB05].
- Such filters are **small but PFAS-rich solid residuals**, likely requiring disposal routes comparable to spent sorbents.

## 5. How residual management modifies energy, cost, and environmental value of membrane separation

Drawing on the above, we can now address the third required component explicitly.

### 5.1. Energy and cost implications

**Membrane stage**

- High-recovery CCMF at 97% recovery uses **0.14–0.57 kWh/m³** depending on membrane tightness [WEB03], but yields only separation—not destruction—of PFAS.
- A 97%‑recovery closed-circuit NF/RO system with a PFAS concentration factor of **33** [WEB04] reduces downstream destruction volume by that same factor relative to the influent, but **does not eliminate the need for destruction**.

**Destruction stage**

- For UV/sulfite ARP applied to ROC, the abstract shows **>90% PFAS defluorination in 24 h** [WEB01], but gives no energy or chemical consumption. Given the long reaction time (24 h) and the need for pretreatment (hardness removal, UV/persulfate) [WEB01], this route likely carries a substantial energy and reagent cost compared with purely physical separation—but without numbers it cannot be quantitatively ranked.

- SCWO is described as **energy-intensive** [WEB07, WEB12], but:

  - When preceded by RO concentration, SCWO **requires less reactor volume and less energy overall** for a given PFAS mass [WEB12].
  - The conceptual guidance to concentrate PFAS into “the smallest possible volume for energy-intensive destruction” [WEB07] directly supports using membrane trains (and/or IX-distillation) upstream of SCWO.

- Plasma vortex + RO polishing is a similar energy-concentrating concept: PFAS mass is first concentrated to ~30,000× [WEB11], then cycled between plasma and RO retentate in a closed loop [WEB11]. The energy implications are potentially large but not quantified; however, the design clearly **prioritizes containment and destruction over energy minimization**.

**Net effect on the economics of membranes**

From an engineering standpoint:

- Membranes **shift energy use from continuous, high-volume treatment (e.g., SCWO on raw water) to lower-volume, higher-intensity destruction** (e.g., SCWO or plasma on concentrated streams).
- The **incremental energy and cost of high-recovery NF/RO** (0.14–0.57 kWh/m³ [WEB03]) must be weighed against:
  - The **avoided energy and capital** of treating the full influent volume with destruction technologies [WEB07, WEB12], and
  - The need for **regeneration/distillation** in IX-based trains that achieve even higher concentration factors (~30,000× [WEB11]).

Given the data gaps, the evidence supports a **qualitative** conclusion:  
Using NF/RO as a concentration step **can lower destruction-stage energy per unit volume** at the expense of membrane-stage energy and capital. Whether this yields net cost savings is site- and technology-specific and **not quantifiable** from the current table.

### 5.2. Environmental and risk implications

**Residual-volume reduction vs residual-hazard increase**

- High concentration factors (33× at 97% recovery [WEB04]; ~30,000× for IX still bottoms [WEB11]) **shrink the liquid volume requiring destruction** but create **small volumes of highly hazardous waste.**
- If downstream destruction (UV-ARP, SCWO, plasma) is reliable and properly controlled, this **reduces overall environmental burden**:
  - Smaller reactors,
  - Reduced fuel or electricity per unit influent water,
  - Potentially smaller emission footprints.

- However, high-concentration residuals **elevate risk** in cases of:
  - Tank failures,
  - Improper handling/transport,
  - Incomplete destruction, or
  - Accidental release during regeneration or brine management.

**Fate of PFAS and byproducts**

- UV-ARP on ROC achieves **>90% defluorination** [WEB01] but lacks data on carbon fate or transformation products. Thus it likely **reduces parent PFAS** and releases fluoride, but may yield persistent organofluorinated intermediates if not fully oxidized.
- SCWO is conceptually positioned as a **terminal destruction technology**, with effluents analyzed for PFAS in both liquid and gas phases [WEB07]; however, **quantitative destruction or defluorination is not reported** in the accessible excerpts.

Without comprehensive byproduct and defluorination data:

- **Membrane + destruction trains should be evaluated cautiously**, with explicit attention to:
  - Fluoride and inorganic byproducts,
  - Low-volatility organics in effluents,
  - Possible PFAS in off-gas (SCWO) [WEB07].

**Solid residuals**

- PFAS-rich solids (spent GAC/AIX [WEB08], PFAS-loaded filters [WEB05], RO elements with embedded PFAS [WEB10]) represent **long-term liability** if landfilled or poorly incinerated.
- SCWO of GAC/AIX [WEB08] offers a path to avoid PFAS persistence in solid wastes, but scale and cost remain unquantified in the current evidence.
- RO elements should be treated as **controlled PFAS residuals** (tens of mg ΣPFAS per element modeled over lifetime [WEB10]); end‑of‑life decisions should not ignore this inventory.

### 5.3. Practical value of membrane separation in complete treatment trains

When viewed strictly as separation steps, NF/RO/CCRO achieve:

- Very high PFAS rejection (>98.3–>99%) across a wide range of PFAS [WEB03, WEB05],
- High recoveries (80–97%) that minimize brine volumes [WEB03, WEB04, WEB05],
- Reasonable specific energies for desalination-scale operations (0.14–0.57 kWh/m³ at 97% recovery [WEB03]).

However, **the practical value of membrane PFAS treatment depends on how their residuals are handled**:

- **Best use**:
  - As **front-end concentrators** that feed well-designed destruction units (SCWO, plasma, or validated UV-ARP) [WEB01, WEB07, WEB11, WEB12].
  - Integrated into loops (e.g., plasma + RO recirculation) that prevent PFAS-laden retentate from being discharged [WEB11].
  - Combined with explicit management of PFAS-laden solids (GAC/AIX [WEB08], filters [WEB05], membranes [WEB10]).

- **Limited value or “false solutions”**:
  - Membrane trains operated without a **quantified, enforceable end-of-life pathway** for their concentrates and solids.
  - Systems that treat high-recovery concentrate or IX still bottoms as a disposal end point (e.g., deep well injection or poorly monitored landfilling), which is **not supported** as effective PFAS management by any record here.

## 6. Priority gaps and research needs

The evidence base highlights several critical gaps for rigorous design and regulation of membrane-based PFAS treatment trains:

1. **Quantitative destruction performance on real concentrates**

   - UV-ARP: need full-scale ROC tests with **energy-normalized destruction metrics**, transformation product identification, and fluorine balance beyond the >90% defluorination headline [WEB01].
   - SCWO and plasma: require **PFAS-specific destruction and defluorination efficiencies** on actual NF/RO/CCRO concentrates with known co‑contaminants, not just model solutions or unspecified concentrated wastes [WEB07, WEB09, WEB11, WEB12].

2. **Integrated energy and cost assessment**

   - Joint accounting of:
     - Membrane-stage energy (e.g., 0.14–0.57 kWh/m³ at 97% recovery [WEB03]),
     - Regeneration/distillation (for IX systems with ~30,000× concentration [WEB11]),
     - Destruction-stage energy (SCWO, plasma, UV-ARP),
     - And avoided infrastructure when destruction targets only concentrate rather than full flow.

3. **Comprehensive mass-balances and lifecycle PFAS accounting**

   - Full mass-balances across:
     - Permeate,
     - Concentrate,
     - Gases,
     - Solids (filters, GAC, AIX, membranes),
     using methods like EPA 537.1, TOPA, and total organofluorine.
   - Lifecycle-oriented analyses of **membrane elements as PFAS reservoirs** (building on leaching/TOPA work [WEB10]) and of PFAS loading on auxiliary solids [WEB05].

4. **Scale-up and operability of hybrid trains**

   - Pilot/field scale demonstrations combining:
     - High-recovery NF/RO or CCRO [WEB03, WEB04, WEB05, WEB06],
     - Plus SCWO [WEB12], plasma [WEB11], or advanced photochemical treatments [WEB01],
     with documented:
     - Reliability,
     - Maintenance needs,
     - Residual handling (e.g., brine tanks, off-gas, spent elements),
     - Occupational and environmental safety.

5. **Regulatory-relevant endpoints**

   - Translation of **parent-compound removal**, **defluorination**, and **sorption** endpoints into:
     - Drinking water compliance,
     - Residual classification (e.g., hazardous waste thresholds),
     - Long-term liability for landfilled or stockpiled PFAS-containing membranes and media.

---

### 7. Concluding synthesis

Across the records, the consistent pattern is:

- **Membranes are powerful PFAS concentrators**, not stand‑alone solutions.
- Characterization of the retained stream—its **volume (determined by recovery), concentration factor, PFAS composition, and co‑contaminants**—is essential for choosing downstream options.
- Downstream options split into:
  - **Further concentration and recycle** (CCRO, FO, IX+distillation, MFC),
  - **Destructive treatments** (UV-ARP, SCWO, plasma),
  - **Solid residual management** (SCWO of GAC/AIX, controlled disposal or destruction of membranes and filters).

The environmental and practical value of membrane separation emerges only when these residuals are:

- Accurately characterized,
- Coupled to robust and energy-conscious destruction or long-term containment,
- And managed in a way that **prevents re‑release of PFAS to the environment** rather than displacing it into smaller, less visible waste streams.
