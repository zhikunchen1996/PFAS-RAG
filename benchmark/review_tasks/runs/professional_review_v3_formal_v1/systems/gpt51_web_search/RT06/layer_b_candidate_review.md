### 1. Scope and conceptual frame

Across the available studies, foam-based PFAS treatment is consistently a **physical separation and concentration** process: surface‑active PFAS partition to gas–water interfaces, are transported in rising bubbles, and are skimmed or collapsed as a PFAS‑enriched foamate, while the bulk water is depleted but not chemically detoxified [R01; R02; R03; R04]. This review interprets how:

- PFAS molecular properties,
- water chemistry and co‑constituents, and
- reactor operation and hydrodynamics

jointly control performance, keeping **removal, recovery, enrichment, mass balance, and volume reduction** clearly distinct.

---

## 2. PFAS molecular properties: chain length and functional group

### 2.1 Adsorption coefficient as primary molecular control

The full‑scale SAFF field trial treating hard groundwater at the Oakey Army Aviation Centre directly linked separation performance to a **PFAS adsorption coefficient at the gas–liquid interface** [R01]. When the reported adsorption coefficient exceeded **approximately 1.0×10⁻⁶ m**, “practically all” of those PFAS (including PFOS, PFHxS, PFOA and other longer‑chain species) were removed by SAFF [R01‑E03]. Shorter‑chain PFAS with lower adsorption coefficients were correspondingly harder to remove [R01‑E03].

This is the clearest mechanistic evidence that, for foam fractionation:

- The **gas–liquid interfacial adsorption coefficient** is a more direct predictor of removal than chain length alone.
- Chain length matters because it controls the adsorption coefficient; longer chains (and sulfonates) tend to have higher coefficients and thus higher removal under otherwise similar conditions [R01‑E03].

### 2.2 Long‑ vs short‑chain PFAS in controlled columns

Bench‑scale column experiments with synthetic groundwater explicitly tested both **long‑chain PFOA/PFOS** and **short‑chain PFBA/PFBS** under a single, well‑defined hydrodynamic regime (60 cm column, 5 cm ID, 0.47 L·min⁻¹ air, 0.5 μm diffuser) [R02‑E02, R02‑R01]. The study was designed to examine how initial concentrations affect both removal and enrichment of these four PFAS, but the excerpt does not report compound‑specific removal or enrichment values [R02‑R01]. What is clear is:

- Both **carboxylate (PFBA, PFOA)** and **sulfonate (PFBS, PFOS)** groups were treated in the same reactor.
- Short‑chain PFBA/PFBS were deliberately included as challenging test compounds, recognizing their weaker surface activity relative to PFOA/PFOS [R02‑E03].

Combined with the Oakey field correlation, this supports a cautious generalization: **long‑chain PFAS—whether acids or sulfonates—are more readily stripped into foam than short‑chain analogues under the same interfacial conditions**, provided adsorption coefficients are high [R01; R02].

### 2.3 Functional group class (PFCAs vs PFSAs) and other PFAS

Several pilot and bench studies include **broad PFAS class coverage**:

- Landfill‑leachate and industrial‑water systems removed a **wide range of perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids (PFCAs) and sulfonates (PFSAs)** [R05‑E04; R06‑E02, E03].
- Groundwater experiments included a ladder of PFCAs (C4–C9) and PFSAs plus 6:2 FTS [R02‑E05].

These studies qualitatively report **high removal of many long‑chain PFAS** from the water phase [R06‑E03], but the excerpts do not provide class‑resolved efficiencies. The only structurally explicit, quantitative pattern is again the adsorption‑coefficient relationship [R01‑E03]. Thus, while PFSAs are commonly more surface‑active than PFCAs of the same chain length, the table does not provide enough resolved data to rank PFSAs vs PFCAs at fixed carbon number beyond this general expectation.

### 2.4 PFOS/PFOA behaviour under metal‑activated flotation

In dilute water, a foam‑flotation variant with **Fe(III) activation** achieved >99% removal of **PFOS and PFOA** within 5 min at 11.5 mM Fe(III) [R03‑E01]. Here, PFAS molecules likely form Fe–PFAS complexes and/or are swept into foam through Fe‑induced hydrophobic flocs, but the verified record only supports the practical outcome:

- Long‑chain PFOS and PFOA can be **almost completely removed from simple aqueous solutions** by metal‑assisted foam flotation under strongly acidic, high‑Fe conditions [R03].

This does not directly speak to short‑chain performance, since short‑chain PFAS were not tested.

## 3. Water chemistry and co‑constituents

### 3.1 Ionic strength, hardness, and salts

The Oakey SAFF trial explicitly notes that **hardness and dissolved electrolytes** are believed to enhance adsorption coefficients and thus foam‑fractionation performance [R01‑E04]. The authors infer that SAFF is likely more effective in **brackish, saline, or RO‑reject waters** than in low‑ionic‑strength matrices [R01‑E04]. This aligns with:

- Groundwaters from two sites (AFB and MI) spanning modest but distinct **conductivities (0.168 vs 0.109 mS·cm⁻¹) and salinities (0.02 vs 0.05 ppt)** [R02‑E06], used explicitly to probe **pH and salinity effects** on PFAS removal by foam fractionation [R02‑R02].
- Landfill leachate described as a **complex, saline matrix** [R05‑E04], where a pilot continuous system removed a wide range of PFCAs and PFSAs [R05‑E04].

However, the table does not include direct quantitative comparisons of removal as a function of conductivity or salinity. The supported statements are:

- **Higher ionic strength/hardness is plausibly beneficial** for PFAS adsorption and thus foam fractionation [R01‑E04].
- Foam fractionation can operate in **saline and moderately saline matrices** (landfill leachate, industrial water, AFB and MI groundwaters) [R01; R05; R06; R02].

The specific sensitivity of different PFAS or performance metrics to ionic strength remains under‑quantified.

### 3.2 pH and metal activators

The most explicit pH effect is in the Fe(III)‑activated dilute‑water system:

- **Maximum PFOS/PFOA removal occurred at pH 2.3**, and removal increased as pH decreased over the studied range [R03‑E02].
- **No significant removal was observed at pH ≥ 6**, attributed to OH⁻ competing with PFOS/PFOA for Fe³⁺ and forming ferric hydroxo species that impede surfactant removal [R03‑E02].

Here, pH is controlling the **speciation and complexation of the Fe activator**, not just PFAS ionization. This is a **foam flotation with metallic activators**, distinct from the air‑only foam fractionation used in SAFF and the Pennell column [R01; R02]. Generalizing this strong low‑pH dependence to air‑only systems would be inappropriate; those studies do not report pH‑dependent performance.

### 3.3 Natural organic matter and co‑surfactants (inherent in matrices)

Matrices like **landfill leachate** and **industrial water** are described as complex and saline [R05‑E04; R06‑E02]. Although dissolved organic matter is not quantified, the studies show:

- Foam fractionation can still achieve **high apparent removal of many long‑chain PFAS from the water phase** in these complex matrices [R06‑E03].
- However, **mass balance closure is incomplete**, with PFAS accumulating in an **upper water layer** within the column and some mass unaccounted for [R06‑E03]. This indicates that co‑surfactants and organic matter can alter internal phase partitioning and potentially promote aerosol or wall losses.

These findings underscore that in high‑organic matrices:

- **Measured “removal” from influent to effluent does not necessarily equal capture in foamate**.
- Co‑surfactants can promote foam stability and PFAS partitioning into internal water layers rather than the collected foam [R06].

### 3.4 Intentional co‑surfactants (organic modifiers)

The Pennell group systematically injected **cationic co‑surfactants (CTAB, DTAB, Polyquaternium‑10)** into their bench‑scale column at controlled mass flow rates [R02‑E07; R02‑R03]. For PFAS‑containing groundwater (synthetic and/or site water within the same study context), they report:

- **Sauter mean bubble diameter** and **foam stability** were measured over time to assess co‑surfactant effects on column hydrodynamics [R02‑E07].
- Co‑surfactant choice affected **foamate volume, volume reduction factor (VRF), and PFAS enrichment** qualitatively; specific VRF/enrichment values are not given in the excerpt [R02‑E07; R02‑R03].

Thus, co‑surfactants here act as **engineered hydrodynamic and interfacial modifiers**:

- Cationic surfactants can **reduce bubble size**, increase foam stability, and thereby change the **residence time of PFAS at interfaces**, all of which promote higher enrichment and potentially higher removal at fixed column geometry and flow [R02‑E07].
- However, they also introduce new organic constituents whose fate is **not tracked or destroyed** in the available data [R02‑R03], creating a secondary residual‑management problem.

### 3.5 Competing inorganic constituents and surfactant order

In the Fe(III)‑assisted flotation system, the relative performance of metallic activators for PFOS removal followed:

- **Fe(III) ≈ La(III) > Al(III) > Ca(II) > Fe(II) > K(I)** [R03‑E01].

This ordering shows that:

- **Multivalent cations**, particularly Fe(III) and La(III), most strongly enhance PFOS removal, likely through stronger complexation or more effective floc/collod formation [R03‑E01].
- At high pH, **OH⁻ is a competing ligand** that suppresses PFAS removal by forming ferric hydroxo species [R03‑E02].

These effects are specific to **metal‑assisted flotation in dilute water** and should not be generalized to air‑only foam fractionation in groundwater or leachate without explicit evidence.

## 4. Reactor operation and hydrodynamics

### 4.1 Gas flow rate and bubble production

Direct, quantitative operating conditions are only available for the Pennell bench‑scale column:

- **Air flow rate: 0.47 L·min⁻¹**, diffusion stone pore size 0.5 μm, with specified column height and fill height [R02‑E02].

Burns et al. emphasize more generally that in foam fractionation:

- **Gas flow rate and bubble size dictate the hydrodynamic condition of the foam**, and the **method of bubble production** is a key design variable [R01‑E06].

In the co‑surfactant experiments, Sauter mean bubble diameter was explicitly measured under different co‑surfactant injections [R02‑E07], indicating that:

- **Bubble size is actively engineered** in at least one study and demonstrably affects foam volume and PFAS enrichment [R02‑E07].

The landfill‑leachate and industrial‑water systems also rely on air sparging, but the excerpts do not report gas rates or bubble‑size distributions [R05; R06]. SAFF at Oakey is described at a process level, not with explicit operating values [R01].

Consequently, across the corpus:

- **Directionally**, smaller bubbles and optimized gas flux are highlighted as important for foam hydrodynamics and thus performance [R01‑E06; R02‑E07].
- **Quantitatively**, there is insufficient comparable data to identify an optimal gas‑rate or bubble‑size regime across matrices.

### 4.2 Residence time and operation mode (batch vs continuous)

The only explicit temporal parameter is in the Fe(III) system: **5 min contact time** with Fe(III) at 11.5 mM gave >99% PFOS/PFOA removal [R03‑E01]. This very short hydrodynamic contact time underscores the intensity of that system but is not directly transferable to air‑only columns.

For column systems:

- The landfill‑leachate pilot is **continuous**, with air flow, contact time, and foam fraction systematically varied [R05‑E02]. However, specific values for contact time are not reported in the excerpt.
- The same system was also operated in **batch mode**, and the batch mass balance closed only to about half, versus ~66–104% for continuous runs [R05‑E03; R05‑R02]. This implies:
  - Batch operation, at least as implemented here, can allow more PFAS accumulation inside the column or aerosol loss.
  - **Operation mode affects system closure and interpretability**, even if nominal removal from the sampled streams could be high.

AFB/MI and synthetic‑groundwater experiments in the Pennell column appear to be **batch‑type column runs** (feed volume treated until completion) [R02‑E02–E04], but detailed residence‑time distributions are not provided.

### 4.3 Reactor configuration: columns vs SAFF vs sparge trenches

Three primary reactor configurations appear:

1. **Vertical foam columns (bench‑ and pilot‑scale)**  
   - 60 cm × 5 cm lab column with controlled air and vacuum [R02‑E02].  
   - Pilot columns for landfill leachate and industrial water [R05; R06].  
   These systems allow access to distinct phases (influent, foamate, effluent, upper water layer) for detailed mass balances [R06‑E02, E03].

2. **SAFF systems**  
   - Oakey’s SAFF unit is a **commercial field system** leading a train with anion‑exchange polishing [R01‑E01, E02].  
   - Geometry and bubble‑generation details are not specified, but SAFF achieved ≥99.5% aggregate removal of PFOS+PFHxS+PFOA from hard groundwater, primarily via separation and concentration into foamate [R01‑E01].

3. **In situ air sparge trench**  
   - A field‑scale **sparge trench** intercepting a shallow plume uses sparged air to form foam at the groundwater surface, which is then removed by vacuum and/or skimming [R04‑E02].  
   - This configuration is inherently **passive and distributed**, prioritizing low operational cost and in situ deployment over tightly controlled hydrodynamics.

These configurations differ critically in:

- **Phase accessibility**: Columns and SAFF plants can more easily collect separate foamate and effluent streams; the in situ trench yields a skimmed, reconstituted foam but has less control of internal volumes.
- **Hydrodynamic control**: Bench columns have fixed geometry and well‑specified flows [R02‑E02]; full‑scale SAFF and trenches prioritize robustness but have more complex flow paths [R01; R04].
- **Foam collection mechanism**:
  - Columns: foam overflows or is collapsed to foamate [R02; R06].  
  - SAFF: commercialized foam‑collection and collapse systems (not specified) [R01].  
  - Trench: **vacuum/skimming** to recover foam at the ground surface [R04‑E02].

No excerpt provides data on recycle loops, and thus recycling effects cannot be evaluated.

## 5. Performance metrics: definitions and evidence

It is essential to distinguish:

- **Removal** – PFAS **lost from the bulk water phase** (influent to effluent).  
- **Separation / enrichment** – the **degree to which removed PFAS are captured and concentrated in foamate**.  
- **Recovery** – the fraction of **initial PFAS that can be recovered from foamate or concentrate** for further handling.  
- **Mass balance closure** – how well the **sum of PFAS in all measured phases matches influent mass**, indicating completeness of accounting (not performance per se).  
- **Volume reduction (VRF)** – reduction in aqueous volume requiring downstream destruction; often expressed as a **groundwater:foam volume ratio**.

Treating these as interchangeable would misrepresent process performance.

### 5.1 Removal (influent–effluent losses from water)

Evidence:

- **SAFF Oakey**: ≥99.5% removal of aggregate PFOS+PFHxS+PFOA from hard groundwater over 12 months, consistently meeting and exceeding regulatory criteria for these PFAS by a factor of 3.3 [R01‑E01, E02]. This is **removal from bulk groundwater**, primarily via foam‑phase separation [R01‑E01, E03].
- **Fe(III) flotation in dilute water**: >99% removal of PFOS and PFOA within 5 min at 11.5 mM Fe(III) [R03‑E01].
- **Industrial water and landfill leachate columns**: the studies report **high removal efficiencies from the water phase for many long‑chain PFAS** qualitatively [R06‑E03; R05‑E04], but the excerpts do not give numeric values.

Removal is therefore demonstrated at high levels for long‑chain PFAS in **both simple and complex matrices**, but the **completeness of capture in foamate and the fate of short‑chain PFAS are less certain**.

### 5.2 Separation and enrichment in foamate

Evidence of separation endpoints:

- Oakey SAFF: SAFF “removes PFAS from bulk groundwater by adsorption at the gas–liquid interface and transfer into a foamate phase enriched in PFAS” [R01‑R01]. The foamate is qualitatively described as enriched in amphiphiles; **no numeric enrichment factor** is reported [R01‑E05].
- Bench column (synthetic and natural groundwater): PFAS enrichment in foamate and VRF are explicitly listed as experimental objectives [R02‑R01, R02], but specific values are not provided.
- Co‑surfactant experiments: co‑surfactant type affects foamate volume, VRF, and PFAS enrichment qualitatively [R02‑E07; R02‑R03].
- In situ sparge trench: PFAS **concentration factors** in reconstituted foam are “typically in the range of 1 L foam to 5000 L groundwater,” i.e., a roughly 5000:1 volume reduction factor, though the 5000 is explicitly described as part of a **typical range** rather than an exact universal value [R04‑E03].

These findings show that:

- **High removal does not guarantee high enrichment**; VRF and PFAS partitioning between foam and internal water layers must be measured separately.
- The **air‑sparge trench exemplifies extreme volume reduction**, potentially minimizing the volume that needs destructive treatment [R04‑E03], but without reported influent–effluent PFAS data, its true removal efficiency is unknown.

### 5.3 Recovery (mass recoverable from foamate/concentrate)

Only the Fe(III) flotation study explicitly measures **recovery** from the concentrated phase:

- By adjusting the **pH of the concentrate scum to 7.0**, approximately **84–91% of PFOS and PFOA could be recovered** [R03‑E03].

This is distinct from the >99% removal from the initial dilute solution [R03‑E01]:

- **Removal (>99%)**: PFAS transferred out of the bulk dilute solution.  
- **Recovery (84–91%)**: fraction of the initial PFAS that can actually be **regained from the concentrate**.

The shortfall illustrates that **some mass remains unrecovered in the scum or is otherwise lost**, even when apparent aqueous‑phase removal is near‑complete. Column studies and SAFF plants do not report analogous recovery fractions; they focus on removal and sometimes enrichment.

### 5.4 Mass balance closure

Mass balance is evaluated most rigorously in the landfill‑leachate and industrial‑water studies:

- **Continuous landfill‑leachate runs**: PFAS mass balance closure ranged from about **66% to 104%**, indicating moderate to good closure but also suggesting **PFAS accumulation within the foam column and/or aerosol losses** [R05‑E03].
- **Batch landfill‑leachate run**: mass balance closed only to **about 50%**, with ~half of the PFAS mass unaccounted for when considering only bottom or top effluent samples [R05‑E03; R05‑R02].
- **Industrial water column**: PFAS distribution among influent, foamate, effluent, and an upper water layer was quantified, and **incomplete closure** again indicated PFAS accumulation in internal water layers and possible unmeasured sinks [R06‑E02, E03].

These results underscore two key points:

1. **High apparent removal (%) from water does not guarantee full mass capture** in foamate or measured phases.
2. **Mass‑balance closure is a separate metric** from removal, enrichment, or recovery; poor closure can reflect:
   - Internal accumulation (e.g., upper water layer),
   - Aerosol or off‑gas carryover,
   - Adhesion to internal surfaces, or
   - Analytical or sampling limitations.

Burns et al. and the Pennell groundwater column experiments do not provide closed mass balances in the excerpts [R01; R02], limiting interpretation of where PFAS ultimately reside after treatment.

### 5.5 Volume reduction and concentration factor

Volume reduction is explicitly quantified only for the **in situ sparge trench**:

- PFAS concentration factors are **typically** in the range of 1 L reconstituted foam per 5000 L groundwater, i.e., **VRF ~5000:1**, recognizing this number as part of a typical range rather than a single exact factor [R04‑E03].

Column and SAFF studies conceptually seek high VRFs but do not report numeric VRF values in the excerpts [R01; R06; R02]. Co‑surfactors clearly influence VRF in the Pennell column [R02‑E07], yet those numeric effects are not available here.

Importantly:

- **VRF is distinct from removal**: A system can achieve high removal but low volume reduction (large foam volumes), or modest removal but extreme VRF. The two must be optimized jointly for practical design.
- VRF is also distinct from **recovery**: one can concentrate PFAS into a small volume yet still lose a significant fraction in internal layers or aerosols.

## 6. Integrated interpretation and practical trade‑offs

### 6.1 Structural and matrix controls

Within tested conditions:

- **Long‑chain PFAS with high gas–liquid adsorption coefficients** are consistently easier to strip and concentrate via foam fractionation than short‑chain PFAS [R01‑E03; R02‑E03]. This holds in hard groundwater [R01], synthetic groundwater [R02], and complex industrial/landfill matrices [R06; R05‑E04].
- **Matrix ionic strength and hardness** appear to facilitate PFAS adsorption and removal [R01‑E04; R02‑E06], consistent with the effective application of foam fractionation to saline landfill leachate and industrial water [R05‑E04; R06‑E02].
- **Co‑surfactants and organic matter** are double‑edged:
  - They can stabilize foam and increase enrichment [R02‑E07].
  - They can also create internal reservoirs (upper water layers) and promote mass‑balance deficits [R06‑E03; R05‑E03].

The explicit Fe(III) case demonstrates that **targeted inorganic additives** can give near‑complete removal and high recovery in simple matrices [R03], but these conditions (low pH, high Fe) are unlikely to be acceptable for large‑scale drinking‑water or in situ groundwater systems without further treatment.

### 6.2 Operational levers

Across systems, several operational levers consistently appear as important, though not yet fully quantified:

- **Bubble generation**: diffuser pore size and co‑surfactant addition are practical tools to control bubble size and foam stability [R02‑E02, E07].
- **Gas flow and contact time**: recognized as key determinants of hydrodynamics [R01‑E06; R05‑E02], but only sparsely parameterized.
- **Reactor configuration**:
  - Controlled columns and SAFF plants are suitable where infrastructure and energy are available, and where foamate can be managed ex situ [R01; R05; R06; R02].
  - Passive sparge trenches trade some control and accounting for low capital and passive interception of plumes, with very high volume reduction [R04‑E02, E03].

### 6.3 Residual streams and downstream treatment

None of the foam‑based systems in the table **destroy PFAS**:

- SAFF and column systems generate **PFAS‑enriched foamate** intended for downstream polishing or destructive treatment [R01; R06; R02].
- The in situ sparge trench explicitly envisions treating the recovered PFAS concentrate via **incineration, electrochemical oxidation, or enhanced plasma** [R04‑E03].
- Co‑surfactant‑modified systems introduce additional **organic residuals** whose treatment or environmental fate is not discussed [R02‑R03].
- Mass‑balance analyses reveal non‑negligible **unaccounted PFAS fractions**, implying potential aerosol release or retention in internal water layers [R05‑E03; R06‑E03].

Thus, from a remediation perspective, **foam fractionation is best viewed as a front‑end concentration technology**, whose environmental performance depends critically on:

- The efficiency and robustness of the downstream destruction step, and
- Minimizing unaccounted PFAS pathways (e.g., aerosols).

## 7. Evidence gaps and research needs

Given the current evidence base, key gaps include:

1. **Quantitative, structure‑resolved performance**  
   - Systematic data linking **PFAS chain length, functional group, and adsorption coefficient** to removal, enrichment, and recovery under **controlled hydrodynamics and matrix compositions** are limited.  
   - Short‑chain PFAS performance remains under‑quantified, particularly in complex waters.

2. **Explicit roles of DOC/co‑surfactants in real waters**  
   - Landfill leachate and industrial water clearly affect internal accumulation and mass balance [R05; R06], but DOC and co‑surfactant profiles are not explicitly linked to performance metrics.  
   - There is little evidence on whether **natural organic matter behaves like intentionally added co‑surfactants** in the Pennell column (bubble size, VRF, enrichment) [R02‑E07].

3. **Hydrodynamic optimization across scales**  
   - Only one study reports detailed column geometry and gas conditions [R02‑E02]; SAFF and field trenches lack such detail [R01; R04].  
   - Without systematic variation and reporting of **gas rate, bubble size, and residence times** at multiple scales, it is difficult to generalize design rules.

4. **Mass balance and aerosol fate**  
   - Repeated observation of incomplete mass balance in column systems [R05‑E03; R06‑E03] points to **aerosol and internal‑layer reservoirs** that are currently poorly characterized.  
   - Future work should include **off‑gas sampling and internal phase mapping** to ensure that PFAS are not being redistributed rather than controlled.

5. **Recovery and VRF as explicit design metrics**  
   - Only a single study quantifies **recovery from foamate** [R03], and only the sparge trench reports a **typical volume reduction factor** [R04‑E03].  
   - For treatment trains, **removal, recovery, VRF, and mass balance** all need to be reported to evaluate cost, risk, and downstream treatment requirements.

6. **Co‑surfactant and metal‑additive sustainability**  
   - Cationic surfactants and metal activators can improve hydrodynamics and removal/recovery [R02‑E07; R03], but their **own environmental impacts** and treatment needs are not quantified.  
   - Research should clarify whether these additives net‑reduce risk, once their full life‑cycle and residual management are included.

---

## 8. Concluding remarks

Within the boundaries of the verified evidence:

- **PFAS interfacial adsorption coefficient (strongly linked to chain length and sometimes functional group)**, **matrix ionic strength/hardness**, and **bubble‑scale hydrodynamics** are the primary determinants of foam‑fractionation performance.
- Co‑surfactants and metals provide powerful but potentially problematic levers for improving removal, enrichment, and perhaps recovery, particularly in simple matrices.
- Distinct performance metrics—**removal, enrichment, recovery, mass balance, and volume reduction**—must be reported and interpreted separately. The available studies demonstrate that high removal from water can coexist with incomplete recovery, poor mass balance, or modest volume reduction.
- Foam‑based technologies, from bench columns to SAFF plants and in situ sparge trenches, are best understood as **PFAS concentration steps that create small, high‑strength residual streams** for subsequent destruction, rather than standalone “remedies.”

Robust evaluation and scale‑up will require that future work systematically couples **PFAS molecular properties, controlled hydrodynamics, and detailed mass‑balance accounting** across all phases and residual streams.
