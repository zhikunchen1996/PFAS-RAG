### 1. Mechanisms and material properties relevant to PFAS structure

#### 1.1 Chain length and functional group: consistent trends across media

Across almost all adsorbent classes in the evidence base, two structural features dominate sorption behaviour:

- **Perfluorinated chain length**  
- **Head-group chemistry (PFSA vs PFCA vs precursors)**  

**Activated carbons and biochars**

- Bench-scale batch and isotherm studies with pinewood and date-seed biochars show much stronger sorption (lower equilibrium aqueous concentrations, Ce) for long‑chain PFAS (PFOA, PFOS, PFDA, PFTeDA) than for short‑chain PFHxA and GenX in simple aqueous solutions, even when Ce spans 0–9000 µg/L [R01]. Long‑chain compounds give lower Ce on both biochars; PFOS often has the lowest Ce, consistent with high affinity [R01].  
- Similar chain‑length dependence is seen for modified coal fly ash (CFA‑B): with pure PFAS solutions, the maximum capacity is higher for longer‑chain PFAS than shorter‑chain ones [R02].  
- Ball‑milled colloidal activated carbon (CAC BM) removes PFOS and PFOA more effectively (89% and 73% removal in 1 h) than PFBS and PFBA (55% and 30%) at 1 mg/L in synthetic water, again demonstrating higher affinity for longer‑chain and PFSA species [R03].  
- In multi‑matrix tests, activated carbon and a wood‑derived biochar maintain substantial equilibrium capacities for PFOA, PFOS and GenX (7.7, 16.1, 4.8 mg/g respectively) across Milli‑Q, groundwater and wastewater, despite reduced performance in wastewater [R04]. PFOS > PFOA > GenX is consistent with greater hydrophobicity and sulfonate vs carboxylate head [R04].

**Ion exchange resins (IX/AERs)**

- Strong‑base anion exchange resins with quaternary amines generally show **higher selectivity for PFSAs over PFCAs and for longer over shorter chains**. For example, selectivity ranking across PFAS on AERs was PFOS > PFHxS > PFBS > PFOA > PFHpA > PFHxA > PFPeA > PFBA [R05].  
- In batch IX with NOM, the resin (quaternary amine, chloride form) shows higher affinity for PFOS than PFOA, consistent with higher charge density and acidity of PFSAs [R06].  
- In mixed PFAS/short-chain PFAA groups, IX outperforms GAC for short‑chain PFAA removal in several waters, attributed primarily to **electrostatic attraction** between the anionic PFAS and cationic functional groups; this is particularly important for short‑chain compounds with low hydrophobicity [R07].

**Specialty sorbents and green media**

- Mixed‐media CPS and ZIPGEM columns treating canal water show adsorption preference PFOS > PFOA > PFHxS > PFHpA > PFHxA > PFPeA > PFBS > PFBA for both media [R08]. Long‑chain PFSAs and PFCAs are retained better than short‑chain analogues.  
- BIPGEM component tests show long‑chain PFAS adsorb strongly to biochar, clay and perlite; short‑chain PFAS and GenX adsorb to biochar and clay but poorly to sand and ZVI, with PFBA exhibiting negative distribution coefficients indicating net desorption tendency [R09].  
- IFGEM‑7 shows markedly higher column capacity for PFOS (q0 = 2.59 ng/g) than PFOA (0.007 ng/g), with life‑expectancy calculations predicting orders‑of‑magnitude longer operation for PFOS than PFOA at 100 ng/L influent in a filter scenario [R10].

**Colloidal activated carbon (in situ barriers)**

- For engineered CAC in simulated groundwater, inorganic anions and DOM suppress adsorption of short‑chain PFAA (<7 perfluorinated carbons) more than long‑chain PFAA (≥7 carbons); short‑chain PFAA therefore show lower retention and shorter modeled barrier lifetimes [R11].

**Implication for engineers**

- **Long‑chain PFSAs (e.g., PFOS, PFHxS)** are consistently the most “adsorbable” species across carbons, IX, and specialty media under the examined conditions.  
- **Long‑chain PFCAs (e.g., PFOA, PFNA, PFDA, PFTeDA)** are next most favourable.  
- **Short‑chain PFCAs and PFSAs (PFBA, PFPeA, PFHxA, PFBS, PFBA)** are systematically harder to remove and more sensitive to competition and matrix effects.  
- Ether‑based alternatives (e.g., GenX) frequently show **lower adsorption selectivity** on AERs and carbons than legacy PFOS/PFOA [R05, R01, R03, R08].  

For a target profile dominated by legacy long‑chain PFAS at µg/L levels, multiple media classes can perform well; for mixtures dominated by short‑chain PFAS at ng/L levels, media choice and water conditioning become critical.

#### 1.2 Material properties that tune PFAS affinity

**Carbonaceous sorbents (GAC, PAC, CAC, biochar, CFA, specialty media)**

Key properties:

- **Microporosity / mesoporosity and surface area**  
  - Pinewood biochar’s mesoporous structure is associated with stronger uptake (lower Ce) for long‑chain PFAS than date‑seed biochar despite similar long‑chain preference on both [R01].  
  - CFA‑B’s higher BET surface area and oxide content (SiO₂, Al₂O₃, Fe₂O₃, CaO) contribute to improved PFAS sorption versus other CFA variants [R02].  
  - BIPGEM’s modest BET surface area (1.35 m²/g) but appreciable porosity (30%) are largely attributable to biochar and perlite; PFAS uptake is dominated by those components rather than by sand or ZVI [R09].
- **Surface chemistry and charge (pH_PZC)**  
  - Biochar in BIPGEM has PZC ≈10.7; at experimental pH it is positively charged, enhancing electrostatic attraction to anionic PFAS heads [R09].  
  - ZVI and sand have PZCs 11.5 and 1.2: ZVI acquires positive charge in DI water but low porosity limits PFAS removal; sand’s negative surface charge and low porosity yield poor performance [R09].  
  - ZIPGEM has higher PZC (9.2) than CPS (5.6), yielding positive surface charge over a broader pH range and contributing to stronger PFAS adsorption [R08].  
- **Engineered colloidal carbon**  
  - CAC (Intraplex) is thermally defunctionalized to optimize surface chemistry for anionic PFAA adsorption and is delivered in an anionic polymer (CMC) [R11]. Its dual hydrophobic and electrostatic interaction capacity is central to chain‑length‑dependent uptake and sensitivity to groundwater solutes [R11].

**Ion exchange resins (IX and polystyrene AERs)**

Key features:

- **Quaternary amine functional groups**: permanent positive charge provides strong electrostatic attraction to anionic PFAS.  
- **Alkyl chain length and symmetry on amines**: longer and unequal alkyl chains increase hydrophobic interaction with PFAS tails and improve selectivity; gel‑type quaternary amine AERs with long alkyl chains show highest PFAS selectivity in competitive matrices [R05].  
- **Matrix and structure (gel vs macroporous)**: gel‑type structures with narrower pores better match PFAS size and enhance selective adsorption vs macroporous analogues [R05].  

In mixed PFAS and anion systems, **selectivity coefficients** quantify PFAS preference over chloride, nitrate and sulfate. Median K_PFAS/Cl ≈ 595 vs K_SO4/Cl ≈ 189 across AER–PFAS combinations [R05], showing PFAS outcompete chloride and often nitrate, but practical competition from sulfate can be significant due to higher sulfate concentrations [R05].

**Green/alternative media (IFGEM‑7, CPS, ZIPGEM, CFA)**

- IFGEM‑7 combines iron‑based components with other media; PFOS capacity (2.59 ng/g) vs much lower PFOA capacity shows strong functional‑group and hydrophobicity dependence [R10].  
- CPS (clay–perlite–sand) and ZIPGEM (adding ZVI) differ in BET surface area, porosity and PZC; ZIPGEM’s higher surface area and PZC support higher removal of some short‑chain PFAS (e.g., PFBS) compared with CPS at 2 h [R08].  
- Modified CFA’s oxide‑rich surface promotes electrostatic interactions and complexation with PFAS headgroups; PFAS adsorption is monolayer and chemisorption‑dominated (pseudo‑second-order kinetics, Langmuir isotherms) [R02].

**Precursors and structural variants**

- In tap water at ~5 ng/L, sulfonamide acetic acid precursors (EtFOSAA, MeFOSAA, C8) show higher sorption than PFCAs of similar chain length, attributed to additional electrostatic interactions from combined sulfonamide and carboxylic groups [R12].  
- Branched sulfonamide acetic acids adsorb more strongly than linear isomers, indicating tail geometry can modulate hydrophobic/electrostatic balance [R12].

**Take‑home**

- For **carbonaceous media**, hydrophobic domain development and suitable pore size distributions govern long‑chain PFAS uptake; positive surface charge and functional groups (oxides, aromatic moieties) modulate electrostatic contributions and selectivity, especially for shorter chains and alternatives.  
- For **IX/AERs**, quaternary amines with hydrophobic substituents and gel structures are strongly favoured when PFAS are the primary anionic targets at low ng/L.  
- **Green composites** can be tailored to combine electrostatic sites (oxides, ZVI, clays) with carbonaceous domains to balance multi‑contaminant capture.

### 2. How water quality and operating conditions alter adsorption performance

#### 2.1 Dissolved organic matter (DOM/NOM/EfOM) and TOC

**Ion exchange and IX‑like systems**

- In batch IX studies with PFOA/PFOS and different NOM isolates at 5 mg C/L, **DOC removals >60%** were achieved concurrently with complete PFAS removal under some conditions, but humic isolates (higher MW) caused pore blockage and size exclusion, limiting PFAS uptake and reducing model predictiveness [R06].  
- In more complex waters (wastewater effluent, membrane concentrate, landfill leachate), IX columns showed **rapid breakthrough of short‑chain PFAAs**, with >50% C/C₀ reached in <30,000 bed volumes, reflecting strong competition from DOC and inorganic ions [R07]. Authors explicitly note that short‑chain PFAA removal in these matrices is challenging and may require pretreatment (DOC/anion removal) or extended EBCT [R07].

**GAC in real and artificial waters**

- In Dutch tap water at 5 ng/L per PFAS, three GACs removed **<50% of most PFAS**, with only short‑chain precursors showing higher sorption; NOM and low concentration together limit driving force for mass transfer and capacity utilization [R12].  
- RSSCTs with eight GACs across four groundwaters show **earlier PFBS breakthrough** in a higher‑DOC groundwater (Well 4) than in lower‑DOC wells: F400 achieved significant PFBS breakthrough between about 75,000 and >90,000 BVs in lower‑DOC wells [R13], but in Well 4 PFBS 60% breakthrough occurred between ~10,000–20,000 BVs for several GACs [R13]. DOM characterization by EEM‑PARAFAC attributed earlier breakthrough in Well 4 to greater fulvic‑type DOC [R13].  
- In Chaudhary et al., wastewater organic matter significantly reduces adsorption of PFOA vs Milli‑Q and groundwater, but AC and a wood biochar still retain substantial capacities (7.7 and 16.1 mg/g for PFOA and PFOS) [R04].

**CAC and in situ barriers**

- For colloidal AC in simulated groundwater, DOM of different MW distributions decreases adsorption for all PFAA, **more strongly for short‑chain** species; high DOM scenarios substantially shorten modeled breakthrough times and barrier longevity for PFBA/PFBS relative to PFOA/PFOS [R11].

**Modified CFA in landfill leachate**

- In raw landfill leachate with high DOM, modified CFA can still remove **88% TOC and 93% UV254 at 400 g/L** [R02], but PFAS capacities are modest (3.5 mg/g in pure solutions; tens of ng/g for aggregated groups in leachate) [R02]. High DOM clearly co‑adsorbs and competes.

**Implications**

- **DOM can be both a co‑sorbate and a competitor**, reducing effective PFAS capacity and shifting performance more strongly for short‑chain PFAS.  
- IX and CAC designed for PFAA are particularly sensitive to humic/fulvic components; GAC is less sensitive in absolute terms but still experiences major life‑reduction in high‑DOC groundwaters.  
- For high‑DOM matrices (wastewater, leachate, membrane concentrates), pretreatment (coagulation, upstream carbon, ozonation) or **over‑design of EBCT and media dose** is often needed if adsorption is relied on for short‑chain PFAS control.

#### 2.2 Inorganic ions and ionic strength

- In IX systems, sulfate and nitrate compete for exchange sites; median PFAS–vs–chloride selectivity is high (K_PFAS/Cl ≈595), but median sulfate/chloride selectivity is lower (~189), and since sulfate concentrations often exceed nitrate, sulfate is highlighted as a practical interferent [R05].  
- In Murray et al., inorganic ions in complex waters (wastewater, membrane concentrate, landfill leachate) contribute to early breakthrough of short‑chain PFAAs even for IX, again reflecting competitive occupancy [R07].  
- In CAC systems, increased ionic strength (NaCl) preferentially suppresses adsorption of **short‑chain PFAA**, due to competitive anion adsorption and reduction of electrostatic contributions; long‑chain PFAA are less affected because hydrophobic interactions dominate [R11].  
- For modified CFA, cations (Na⁺, Ca²⁺) and anions (Cl⁻, CO₃²⁻) can promote PFAS adsorption via cation‑bridging and ligand exchange on metal‑oxide surfaces, but these effects are secondary to hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions [R02].

**Implications**

- If the water has **high sulfate, bicarbonate or chloride**, IX and CAC performance on short‑chain PFAS will drop more than for long‑chain PFAS.  
- Carbonaceous sorbents less dependent on electrostatics (microporous AC, some biochars) are less impacted by ionic strength but still see effects via double‑layer compression and co‑sorption.

#### 2.3 pH

- For IX, PFOA/PFOS removal and DOC co‑removal at pH 7 with 5 mg C/L NOM are robust; film diffusion—not equilibrium capacity—limits uptake at ≤50 µg/L when resin dose is low (100 mg/L) [R06]. Performance is designed around pH 7 in their guideline calculation (1000 mg/L dose can meet 70 ng/L at influent ≤50,000 ng/L) [R06].  
- In RSSCTs with artificial groundwaters, higher pH generally increased sorption across all PFAS and sorbents, yielding slower breakthrough and higher modeled q₀ in Thomas fits [R14].  
- For BIPGEM columns in canal water, PFOS removal efficiency was not significantly correlated with pH, while removal of PFOA, PFBS and GenX was **negatively correlated with effluent pH**, consistent with weaker electrostatic attraction at higher pH for these anions [R09].

**Implication**

- Around neutral pH, most PFAAs are fully anionic; pH mainly modulates:
  - resin surface charge (for weak‑base media),
  - oxide surface charge in specialty adsorbents, and
  - competition and Ca‑bridging in natural waters.

Engineers should treat pH as a **tuning parameter** but not a primary lever; shifting pH does not overcome severe competition from DOM and sulfate.

#### 2.4 Competing contaminants and co‑sorbed species

- **Short‑chain PFAS displacement**: In AER columns, desorption of PFBA and PFPeA occurs at later BVs (e.g., PFBA/ PFPeA effluent exceeding influent at ~30,000 and ~70,000 BVs in a commercial AER, and ~60,000 and ~150,000 BVs in Gel(12‑1‑1)), attributed to displacement by long‑chain PFAS or stronger sorbates [R05]. Similar displacement patterns are seen for PFBA/PFBS on CAC in binary systems: PFOS and PFOA reduce PFBA removal by up to 56% and PFBS removal by 30.2% relative to single‑solute cases [R03].  
- **Other anions and cations**:  
  - Ca²⁺ in canal water was negatively correlated with removal of PFOA, PFBS and GenX and positively correlated with PFBA removal, suggesting complex Ca‑bridging and pore‑scale effects [R09].  
  - In Chaudhary et al., Cd(II) is present at 10 mg/L in all matrices; PFOA/PFOS/GenX still adsorb strongly to AC and BC‑W, but isotherms show subtle mutual influences: increased PFAS loading causes modest increases in Cd(II) adsorption and vice versa [R04].

**Implication**

- In mixed PFAS plumes, **long‑chain species will preferentially occupy sites**, potentially leading to later desorption of short‑chain PFAS as long‑chain PFAS accumulate. Designs targeting short‑chain control should anticipate competitive displacement and avoid operating media close to saturation when long‑chain PFAS are present.

#### 2.5 Influent concentration and scale

- Many batch studies operate at **mg/L levels** for capacity and kinetics (e.g., 10 mg/L PFAS for AC/biochar [R04]; 0.4 mg/L per PFCA on CFA [R02]; 0.1–1 mg/L on CAC BM [R03]). These are useful for mechanism and rough capacity but overpredict performance at ng/L.  
- Where more realistic **ng/L influents** are used (e.g., 5 ng/L per PFAS in tap water on GAC [R12], 4 ng/L regulatory limit columns on AERs [R05]), removal of most PFAS is modest on GAC (<50%) at practical GAC doses [R12], whereas a highly selective gel AER maintains PFOS/PFOA and several other PFAS below 10 ng/L up to 180,000 BVs and meets 4 ng/L PFOS/PFOA limits up to ~97,000 BVs [R05].  
- For IFGEM‑7, capacity and life‑expectancy estimates are explicitly calculated for 100 ng/L influent at 80% removal [R10].

**Implication**

- **Do not transpose mg/L capacity data directly to ng/L regulatory scenarios.** For drinking water, column‑based or life‑expectancy analyses at or near regulatory concentrations (AER RSSCTs, IFGEM‑7 design scenarios) are more relevant than batch capacities.

### 3. Translating evidence into conditional media‑selection criteria

This section provides **decision logic**, not a universal ranking. The “best” medium depends on:

- PFAS composition (chain length, head group, presence of precursors)
- Matrix type and quality (DOC, ionic strength, co‑contaminants)
- Target effluent concentration and hydraulic constraints
- Practical considerations (residuals, regeneration, cost—not fully covered here)

#### 3.1 Step 1 – Characterize the PFAS profile

**Case A – Legacy long‑chain PFAS dominated (PFOS, PFHxS, PFOA, PFNA, PFDA, PFTeDA)**

- **In drinking/groundwater, low DOC, ng/L–low µg/L**  
  - **Candidate media**:  
    - GAC (bituminous) – demonstrated long media life for PFBS and long‑chain PFAS in low‑DOC wells (e.g., PFBS 60% breakthrough at 75,000–>90,000 BVs for F400 [R13]).  
    - Strong‑base AERs with long alkyl chains and gel structures – in real drinking water, Gel(12‑1‑1) maintains multiple PFAS <10 ng/L to 180,000 BVs and meets 4 ng/L PFOS/PFOA out to ~97,000 BVs; commercial AER only to ~42,000 BVs [R05].  
    - IX resins similar to Purolite A860 – batch data show complete PFOA/PFOS removal to <70 ng/L at influent ≤50,000 ng/L with 1000 mg/L dose at pH 7, 5 mg C/L NOM [R06].  
  - **Mechanistic match**: hydrophobic chain + strong sulfonate head; all three media exploit hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions.
  - **Selection**: where regulatory targets are stringent (≤4 ng/L) and space/EBCT are constrained, **AER/IX is preferable**; where DOC is low and co‑contaminants minimal, **GAC** can be cost‑effective for long‑chain removal.

- **In higher‑DOC groundwaters**  
  - Expect earlier GAC breakthrough; **DOC characterization** (EEM PARAFAC) helps quantify how fulvic vs hydrophilic fractions correlate with GAC life [R13].  
  - AER/IX will also suffer from NOM competition but can trade higher dose for capacity [R06]; modeling or pilot testing at site‑specific DOC is advisable.

**Case B – Short‑chain PFAS significant (PFBA, PFPeA, PFHxA, PFBS; GenX)**

- **Drinking water at ng/L**  
  - GAC: in real tap water at 5 ng/L, removal <50% for most PFAS, including many short‑chain PFCAs and PFSAs [R12]. Not robust for short‑chain at realistic doses.  
  - AER/IX:  
    - Selectivity order shows PFBA, PFPeA, PFHxA at the bottom; short‑chain PFCAs break through and can desorb as long‑chain species accumulate [R05].  
    - However, a highly selective gel AER (Gel(12‑1‑1)) shows better retention of PFHxA and 6:2 FTS vs commercial AER, with much later breakthrough and delayed desorption [R05].  
  - Specialty media: CPS/ZIPGEM remove short‑chain PFAS only partially (e.g., PFBA, PFPeA) in 2 h columns; long‑chain are favoured [R08].  

  **Conditional recommendation**:  
  - If short‑chain control down to ng/L is critical, and DOC is moderate, **high‑selectivity gel AERs with long alkyl chains** are preferred over generic AERs and GAC, but design must explicitly manage desorption risk for PFBA/PFPeA at later bed volumes.  
  - Design should incorporate **lead–lag columns** with conservative turnover before short‑chain desorption becomes significant.

- **Source‑zone or high‑concentration waters (AFFF sites, leachate, concentrate)**  
  - CAC BM and other advanced ACs: at 0.1–1 mg/L PFAS, CAC BM still shows much lower removal for PFBS/PFBA (55% and 30%) than for PFOS/PFOA [R03] and strong competitive suppression of PFBA/PFBS in presence of PFOS/PFOA [R03].  
  - Modified CFA: in leachate, short‑chain PFAS aggregate adsorption (40–60 ng/g CFA) exceeds long‑chain totals, but this is partly driven by higher short‑chain influent; with pure solutions, CFA still adsorbs long chains more strongly [R02].  
  - CAC barriers: modeling indicates **short‑chain PFAA lifetimes are much shorter** than long‑chain, especially under high ionic strength and DOM; thus CAC alone is not sufficient to stabilize a short‑chain plume long‑term [R11].

  **Conditional recommendation**:  
  - For mass‑reduction at mg/L in source areas, **AC‑based systems (GAC, PAC, CAC BM) and robust IX** can be appropriate primary treatments, recognizing that short‑chain removal will lag and may need polishing.  
  - In in situ barriers, **engineered CAC** can be used where long‑chain PFAA dominate; for short‑chain‑dominated plumes, co‑injection of more electrostatically focused media (e.g., IX resins) or upstream hydraulic control should be considered [R11].

**Case C – PFAS precursors prominent**

- At 5 ng/L in tap water, PFEAs, sulfonamides, and sulfonamide acetic acids show diverse sorption; ether linkages and sulfonamide groups generally **enhance sorption relative to analogous PFCAs**, and branched sulfonamide acetic acids sorb more than linear [R12].  
- Precursors can thus be more or less adsorbable than their terminal acids; **carbon sorbents designed solely for PFOA/PFOS may underperform or over‑perform** on precursors.  

**Conditional recommendation**:

- For systems with high precursor fractions (e.g., industrial sources, AFFF alternatives), **pilot tests with the specific precursor mixture** on candidate GAC and IX are necessary; structural surrogates cannot reliably predict behaviour.

#### 3.2 Step 2 – Match matrix and process constraints

**Low‑DOC groundwater (drinking supply):**

- **Preferred classes**:  
  - GAC for broad PFAS spectrum where short‑chain species are not regulatory drivers and existing GAC infrastructure is present [R13, R14].  
  - Strong‑base AER/IX where ng/L limits and short‑chain control are required, or when high DOC load is anticipated in the future [R05, R06, R14].  
- **Engineering adjustments**:  
  - Use RSSCT or column modeling (Thomas, IAST‑EBC) to derive site‑specific bed lives and costs [R07, R06, R14].  
  - Consider combined beds (AIX1+GAC) if both DOC and PFAS must be controlled and media costs differ [R14].

**High‑DOC groundwater / surface water / wastewater effluents:**

- **GAC**: strong reduction in life; early short‑chain breakthrough in high‑DOC well vs low‑DOC wells [R13]. Still valuable for long‑chain PFAS, co‑contaminant removal, and as pretreatment to membranes [R08].  
- **IX/AER**: good short‑chain performance in low‑DOC groundwater; fast breakthrough of short‑chain PFAAs in complex waters (WW, MC, LL), implying need for **pretreatment or longer EBCT** [R07].  
- **Green mixed media (CPS, ZIPGEM, IFGEM‑7, BIPGEM)**:  
  - Useful where **multi‑contaminant control** (nutrients, metals, PFAS) and operational simplicity are priorities; but PFAS capacities are relatively low and **short‑chain removal is weaker** than long‑chain [R10, R09, R08].  
  - Best positioned as **pretreatment/roughing filters** ahead of high‑selectivity PFAS polishers (e.g., NF/RO, AER).

**Landfill leachate and membrane concentrates:**

- Modified CFA can remove significant TOC and some PFAS (ng/g levels from leachate) [R02], but its PFAS capacity is only a few percent of activated carbon’s in pure solutions [R02].  
- IX in concentrates and leachate experiences rapid short‑chain PFAA breakthrough [R07].  
- These matrices may be better suited to **high‑pressure membranes (NF/RO)** with downstream concentrate destruction, with adsorption used to treat secondary streams or as polishing; this lies just outside strict adsorption selection but is relevant context.

**In situ barriers:**

- CAC barriers are attractive where hydraulic access is challenging; but modeling shows **short‑chain performance is highly sensitive to ionic strength and DOM**, with much shorter breakthrough times than for long‑chains [R11].  
- Use CAC where long‑chain PFAA dominate and DOM is moderate; pair with **site‑specific solute characterization** and longevity modeling to avoid under‑design.

#### 3.3 Step 3 – Consider process goals and residuals

- **Adsorption vs destruction**: all treatments surveyed **transfer PFAS to a solid phase**; none provide mineralization or defluorination evidence in these excerpts [R05–R14]. Residual management (spent media, regenerant, contaminated barriers) must be planned.  
- **Co‑removal of DOC and metals**: IX and CFA/IFGEM‑7 co‑remove DOC [R10, R06]; CFA and green medias can also capture metals or nutrients [R10, R09, R02, R04], which is advantageous or problematic depending on objectives.  
- **Desorption risk**: AER, CAC and GAC systems show competitive desorption of short‑chain PFAS at later times or after co‑sorbate accumulation [R05, R03, R09, R14]. Engineers should:  
  - Avoid operating to deep exhaustion when short‑chain control is critical.  
  - Use lead–lag configurations and conservative changeout criteria.

### 4. Practical trade‑offs, limitations, and research needs

**Trade‑offs:**

- **Selectivity vs robustness:** Highly selective gel AERs can meet stringent PFOS/PFOA targets at low ng/L with long bed lives in drinking water [R05], but may be more sensitive to sulfate and DOM in other matrices [R05, R06]. GAC is less selective but more robust to inorganic ions and easier to integrate where GAC is already used [R13, R14].  
- **Short‑chain vs long‑chain performance:** Media that excel on long‑chains often underperform on short‑chains, especially in high‑DOC or high‑ionic‑strength waters; no single adsorbent in the evidence set eliminates short‑chain PFAS under all realistic conditions.  
- **Cost vs performance:** While explicit cost data are limited here, Murray et al. show that adsorbent use rate and media cost can vary significantly by matrix even for the same adsorbent [R07]. For selection, **adsorbent use rate (g/m³) under site conditions** should be considered alongside capacity.

**Key evidence gaps:**

- **Low‑concentration kinetic and competitive data:** Many mechanistic studies use mg/L PFAS; more work is needed at ng/L to accurately size contactors and barriers.  
- **Precursors and transformation:** Several studies monitor only parent PFAS; more work is needed on sorption/desorption behaviour and downstream transformation of precursors on carbon and mixed media [R08, R12].  
- **Field‑scale validation for alternative/green media:** RSSCTs overpredict FS200 performance vs pilot [R13]; similar scale‑up uncertainties exist for specialty media (ZIPGEM, CPS, IFGEM‑7, BIPGEM, CFA) where long‑term fouling, regeneration and desorption are not fully evaluated.  
- **Integrated treatment trains:** Evidence suggests complementary roles (e.g., CPS/ZIPGEM pretreatment for NF [R08], IFGEM‑7 as pretreatment [R10], CAC barriers plus ex situ polishing [R11]), but systematic design rules for combined adsorption + membrane/oxidation trains are scarce.

### 5. Summary guidance for engineers

1. **Define PFAS targets and matrix.**  
   - Long‑chain legacy PFAS in low‑DOC groundwater → GAC or AER/IX; AER preferred for ng/L targets.  
   - Short‑chain PFAS or GenX at low ng/L → high‑selectivity AER/IX with careful design; GAC alone likely insufficient.  
   - High‑DOC or complex matrices (WW, leachate, MC) → adsorption is challenged; consider pretreatment and/or membranes, with adsorption for polishing or specific PFAS classes.

2. **Match media to mechanism.**  
   - Hydrophobicity‑driven media (AC, biochar) for long‑chain PFAS, DOC co‑removal, multi‑contaminant contexts.  
   - Electrostatics‑enriched media (quaternary amine IX/AER, oxide‑rich CFA) where short‑chain or low‑hydrophobicity PFAS are important and ionic competition is manageable.  
   - Mixed media (CPS, ZIPGEM, IFGEM‑7, BIPGEM, CFA) where co‑contaminant removal and green/sustainable sourcing weigh heavily, accepting lower PFAS capacity.

3. **Account explicitly for DOM and ions.**  
   - Measure DOC and major anions; if high, anticipate shorter bed lives and lower short‑chain PFAS removal, particularly for IX and CAC.  
   - Use site‑specific pilot/RSSCT or modeling (Thomas, IAST‑EBC) to translate lab capacities to design BVs under actual water quality.

4. **Design for desorption and longevity.**  
   - For AER/CAC systems, monitor short‑chain PFAS in effluents over time to detect competitive desorption.  
   - For CAC barriers, perform longevity modeling with realistic ionic strength and DOM scenarios [R11].  

By following these conditional criteria and grounding design in matrix‑ and PFAS‑specific evidence from lab and pilot studies, an engineer can select and size adsorption media that are appropriate to the particular PFAS profile and water matrix rather than relying on a one‑size‑fits‑all ranking.
