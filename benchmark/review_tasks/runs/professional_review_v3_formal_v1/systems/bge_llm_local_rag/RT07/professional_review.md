## 1. Role of Foam Fractionation as a Concentration Step

Across the evidence base, foam fractionation (FF) is consistently used and evaluated as a **phase‑transfer/concentration technology**, not as a destructive process:

- PFAS are transferred from bulk water into a PFAS‑rich foam or foamate, often with substantial enrichment and volume reduction, while treated water retains reduced but non‑zero PFAS levels [S01; S07; S09; S11; S13; S15; S16].
- Multiple studies explicitly position FF as the **first step in a treatment train** that must be followed by destruction (e.g., electrochemical oxidation, plasma, HALT, incineration) or by polishing adsorption processes [S01; S07; S08; S09; S11; S12; S13; S14; S16].

Any assessment of “treatment success” must therefore include both:  
(1) performance of the FF step as a concentrator, and  
(2) disposition or destruction of the PFAS‑rich foamate or secondary concentrates.

## 2. Performance Across Scales and Matrices

### 2.1 Groundwater and Drinking‑Water Contexts

**Bench‑scale synthetic and real groundwater**

- In synthetic groundwater without co‑surfactant, long‑chain PFOS and PFOA achieved plateau removals of ~90% and ~60%, respectively, at ~40 µg/L [S10]. Short‑chain PFBS and PFBA performed worse, consistent with lower surface activity [S10].
- Adding CTAB as a cationic co‑surfactant in synthetic groundwater expanded performance across a wide concentration range (0.4–400 µg/L). Long‑chain PFOS and PFOA achieved near‑complete removal in ~5 min, whereas PFBS and PFBA required ~50 min at the highest concentration to reach high removals, with 99% removal reported for both short‑chain PFBS and PFBA at the highest test concentration [S10].
- In real Australian groundwater with SDS as co‑foaming agent, bench‑scale FF reduced PFHxS, PFOA and PFOS to below 0.1 ppb (detection limit) within 60 min, whereas PFBA was not effectively removed [S03]. This underscores strong chain‑length selectivity in realistic matrices.

**Pilot‑scale foam fractionation on groundwater**

- A pilot groundwater FF + EO train achieved **~60% mean ΣPFAS removal in the FF step**, with ~90% removal of long‑chain PFAA and weaker removal of short‑chain PFAS [S08].
- A commercial SAFF deployment on PFAS‑contaminated groundwater (AACO) previously achieved ≥99.8% removal of PFOS and PFOA and ≥98.4% of PFHxS, with post‑SAFF concentrations ≤12, ≤1 and ≤17 ng/L, respectively [S13-E06]. Those data are for groundwater, but detailed foamate volumes and energy use are not given for that trial [S13].

**Nanofiltration–FF treatment trains for drinking water**

- A pilot NF plant treating AFFF‑impacted municipal groundwater reduced total detectable PFAS in permeate from ~77 ng/L to 1.4 ng/L; ΣPFAS4 from 56 ng/L to 1.0 ng/L [S09].
- Continuous pilot FF of the NF concentrate removed ~90±1% of total PFAS using air alone and 94% with a cationic co‑surfactant [S09].
- The combined NF+FF train shifted PFAS into:
  - A small foamate stream (~2% of NF feed volume) at >3000 ng/L (41× feed concentration) [S09], and
  - A retentate (~18% of NF feed volume) enriched in short‑chain PFAS, at 35 ng/L without co‑surfactant and 21 ng/L with co‑surfactant [S09].

This shows FF is effective as a **post‑NF concentrator**, particularly for long‑chain PFAS, while leaving a shorter‑chain‑enriched retentate that may require ion exchange polishing [S09].

### 2.2 Landfill Leachate

**Pilot‑scale continuous FF on raw leachate**

- A pilot continuous FF column treating real landfill leachate achieved **~60% plateau ΣPFAS removal**, with >90% removal for individual long‑chain PFAS and <30% removal for certain short‑chain PFAS [S01].
- Foamate volume was reduced to ~10% of inlet volume [S01].
- Mass balance was imperfect, with visual evidence of aerosols and possible matrix‑driven transformations [S01].

**Bench‑scale FF and hybrid MER systems on leachate**

- Bench‑scale FF with co‑surfactants in leachate achieved 1.2–4 log reductions for long‑chain PFAS and 1–2 logs for some short‑chain compounds when cationic/zwitterionic cosurfactants were used [S02].
- Bench‑scale landfill leachate FF at low ng/L–µg/L levels across multiple matrices showed >70% removal for most monitored PFAS, but systematically lower removal for short‑chains [S16].

**Membrane electrochemical reactor (MER) with foam fractionation**

- A low‑pH (~2) MER treating leachate achieved 95.6% total PFAS removal, with 98.3% removal of PFSAs and 85.5% of PFCAs [S05]. Short‑chain PFSAs (C≤6) reached 92.5–99.1% removal; short‑chain PFCAs (C≤6) 80.8–90% [S05].
- Residual short‑chain PFCAs in defoamed leachate remained non‑trivial (e.g., 19.2% of PFBA mass, 15.6% PFPeA, 10% PFHxA) [S05].
- Volume of defoamed leachate after MER was only 24% of the initial volume, with the remainder entrained in foam and solids [S05].

This MER configuration is not pure FF, but it shows that combining foam‑based separation with in‑situ electrochemical processes can greatly improve short‑chain capture compared to foam‑only treatments.

**Ozone vs air foam fractionation on leachate**

- Bench‑scale ozone FF removed >90% long‑chain PFAS and 8–95% of named short‑chains; some ultrashort species (<4 perfluorinated carbons) had recoveries <10% [S11].
- Long‑chain PFAS in bulk leachate dropped below 0.03–0.05 µg/L within 2–6 min; complete short‑chain removal was not achievable in that timeframe [S11].
- Ozone FF produced **foamate volumes 2–4× lower** than air FF and foamate wettability <4% vs 7–13% for air, while still concentrating PFAS up to 800 µg/L and increasing the number of detected PFAS (including several precursors) in foamate [S11].
- Air FF in the same leachate saw 10–30 percentage‑point gains in removal for short‑chains (PFHxA, PFPeS) and ~20 points for PFOA when increasing air flow from 0.5 to 1 L/min, but remained less effective for the very short‑chains [S11].

**Simple pH‑induced foaming**

- Acidification of leachate to pH 2.0 without electrochemistry separated 83.1% of total PFAS into foam and 11.3% into precipitated solids; with 0.1 M NaHCO₃ pre‑addition, foam‑phase separation increased to 92.5% [S05].
- Short‑chains (C≤6) were still partitioned significantly into foam (59.2–84.7%) and solids (5.7–13%) [S05].

Collectively, these leachate studies show that **foam‑based concentration is robust for long‑chains**, can be enhanced for some short‑chains with co‑surfactants, ozonation, or low‑pH operation, but still tends to leave residual short‑chain PFAS in treated leachate.

### 2.3 Municipal Wastewater and WWTP Effluent

**Biologically treated municipal wastewater (no or limited surfactant)**

- Batch FF on several MBR/MBBR/CAS effluents achieved average removals of:
  - 96±1% for ΣPFAS4,
  - 93±1% for ΣPFOAeq,
  - 53±2% for ΣPFAS21 [S07].
- Biologically treated effluents had low foaming potential; foam (top water layer) was removed by suction rather than a stable overflowing foam [S07].
- A fixed foamate extraction of 8% of treated liquid volume was used; authors judged it difficult to reduce this below ~5% without loss of performance, based on earlier studies [S07].

**Effect of cationic surfactant on short‑chain PFAS**

- Adding cationic surfactant K15(+) in MBR effluents raised removal of:
  - PFHpA from 48% to >76% (MBRsed),
  - PFPeA from 16% to 44% (MBRout),
  - PFHxA from 29% to >89% (MBRout; statistically significant) [S07].

**Sludge‑integrated operation**

- FF applied directly to sludge liquors (MBRsludge, CASsludge) showed **significantly lower PFAS removal** than for corresponding treated effluents [S07].
- Foamate from sludge matrices had higher total solids than the bulk liquid, so FF co‑concentrated biomass/solids with PFAS [S07].

These results suggest FF can be used as a **side‑stream or post‑biological concentrator**, but foaming and co‑concentration of solids complicate integration with biological treatment, especially if surfactants are added.

**WWTP effluent at low ng/L**

- A multi‑stage FF with added thickener (HPMC) treated WWTP effluent with initial PFOS and PFOA of 34.8 and 8.6 ng/L. After treatment, both were <5 ng/L, corresponding to 99% and 94% removal, respectively [S16].
- PFOS and PFOA were enriched 19× and 17× in third‑stage foam relative to the original effluent; mass recovery for most PFAS (except PFBA) was 70–130% [S16].

At very low ng/L levels, FF can still provide meaningful concentration and removal for long‑chains when assisted by thickeners.

### 2.4 Landfill Leachate and WWTP Combined View

- In landfill leachate, single‑stage FF left PFBA, PFOA and PFOS in the bottom solution at 10²–10³ ng/L after first stage, despite high nominal removals, indicating that FF alone cannot achieve the very low health advisory levels and must be paired with GAC/IX or other polishing [S16].
- FF co‑concentrated ~80% of TOC into foam, while PFAS were <0.01% of TOC, highlighting that **organic co‑contaminants dominate foamate mass** [S16].

## 3. Short‑Chain PFAS Recovery

Across matrices and scales, **short‑chain PFAS removal is consistently weaker** than that for long‑chain PFAS, but there are clear variations:

- **Poor short‑chain performance in air‑only FF**  
  - Pilot continuous FF on leachate: <30% removal for certain short‑chains vs >90% for long‑chains [S01].
  - Commercial SAFF on landfill leachate: post‑SAFF sum of SLV‑11 PFAS was 1260 ng/L, dominated by short‑chain PFHxA, PFBS, PFPeA, PFBA, while longer‑chains summed to only 14.4 ng/L [S15].
  - Bench‑scale synthetic groundwater controls: PFBS and PFBA had much lower plateaus than PFOS/PFOA [S10].
  - Bench‑scale groundwater FF with SDS: PFBA remained largely unremoved [S03].

- **Enhanced short‑chain capture via co‑surfactants and process intensification**
  - Cationic co‑surfactants (CTAB, Montaline C40, K15(+)) markedly improved PFPeA, PFHxA, PFBS, PFPeS removal in synthetic and NF‑concentrate systems [S09, S10, S07].
  - MER operation at low pH with foam fractionation achieved 80.8–90% removal of short‑chain PFCAs and 92.5–99.1% for short‑chain PFSAs [S05].
  - Simple pH‑induced foaming plus solids formation at pH 2 captured 59.2–84.7% of short‑chains into foam and 5.7–13% into solids [S05].
  - In synthetic systems using OTCS with micro‑nano bubbles, PFOA and PFOS removals approached ~99% in synthetic water [S04]; in natural lake and reservoir waters PFOA/PFOS removals exceeded 80% [S04]. These are long‑chains, but improved foam stability and interfacial area could, in principle, benefit some shorter chains.

- **Precursors and transformation**  
  FF often concentrates precursors into foam; TOP‑assay evidence in landfill leachate showed increased PFCAs and PFOS after oxidative assay, consistent with precursor presence in foam and treated leachate [S16]. Ozone FF concentrated several precursors (6:2 FTCA, PFECHS, FOSA, 8:2 FTS, N‑MeFOSAA) into foamate [S11].

In summary, **short‑chain PFAS are not reliably removed by simple air FF** at environmentally relevant concentrations. Co‑surfactants, low‑pH operation, hybrid electrochemical systems, or multi‑stage designs can partially overcome this limitation but rarely to the degree achieved for long‑chains.

## 4. Volume Reduction, Enrichment Factors, and Foamate Characteristics

### 4.1 Volume Reduction and Foamate Fractions

Quantified volume reductions span several orders of magnitude depending on configuration:

- **Direct leachate or wastewater FF (single‑step)**  
  - Pilot leachate FF: foamate volume ~10% of inlet [S01].  
  - Municipal wastewater FF: foamate extraction 8% of treated volume [S07].

- **NF–FF trains for drinking water**  
  - NF concentrate FF: foamate ~2% of NF feed volume; FF retentate ~18% [S09].

- **Ozone vs air FF**  
  - Ozone FF: foamate volumes 2–4× lower than air FF for the same leachate matrix, with wettability <4% vs 7–13% for air FF [S11].

- **Modelled multi‑stage FF for groundwater**  
  - Conceptual multi‑stage FF treating 1,000,000 m³ of groundwater was modelled to yield:
    - 154 m³ concentrate for a 2‑stage process, and
    - 0.6 m³ concentrate for a 3‑stage FF [S14].

- **Life‑cycle FF configurations (landfill leachate)**  
  - One‑stage FF with 20% foam fraction: 200 m³ foam waste per 1000 m³ leachate treated [S12].
  - One‑stage FF with 1% foam fraction: 10 m³ foam waste per 1000 m³ (by inference from foam fraction), though only the fraction itself (1%) is explicitly quantified [S12].
  - Three‑stage low‑foam FF: very low foam waste volumes are reported conceptually; residual foam waste per 1000 m³ is stated as <0.0006 m³ under some configurations [S12-E04], though specific to that scenario.

These values show that depending on design, foamate volume can range from **1–20% of influent** at unit scale to **sub‑percent levels via multistage concentration** or NF–FF combinations.

### 4.2 Enrichment Factors and Concentrations

Where enrichment is quantified:

- NF concentrate FF (drinking water): 41× enrichment in foamate relative to NF feed; foamate >3000 ng/L [S09].
- WWTP effluent FF with HPMC: PFOS and PFOA enriched 19× and 17× in third‑stage foam vs original effluent [S16].
- Mixed environmental waters: PFAS in final foams “up to over 30×” initial concentrations [S16].
- Ozone FF on leachate: total PFAS in foamate up to 800 µg/L, with a broader PFAS/precursor spectrum than in original leachate [S11].
- Leachate TOC co‑concentration: ~80% of TOC partitioned to foam in single‑stage FF, while PFAS represented <0.01% of TOC [S16].

### 4.3 Foamate Wetness and Co‑contaminants

- Air FF foamate is typically **wetter and more voluminous** (7–13% wettability; 2–4× ozone foamate volume) [S11].
- Foamate from sludge or mixed liquor is enriched in total solids and biomass [S07].
- Foamate from leachates and membrane concentrates contains high TOC and diverse co‑surfactants [S09, S16].

Thus, **foamate is not a “pure PFAS” stream**; it is a concentrated mixed waste that can be dominated volumetrically and chemically by non‑PFAS organics and, in some cases, solids.

## 5. Operational Stability and Energy Demand

### 5.1 Operational Stability

Evidence on long‑term continuous operation is limited:

- The NF pilot ran for almost 6 months, but FF was evaluated in shorter campaigns [S09].
- SAFF groundwater trials at AACO ran over years; for landfill leachate, only limited data are reported in the provided excerpts [S13, S15].
- Bench‑scale studies typically ran for minutes to an hour; few report extended continuous FF operation.

Foaming behavior is matrix‑dependent:

- Biologically treated wastewater had low foaming potential, requiring suction of the top water layer [S07].
- Sludge‑containing matrices produced foamate rich in solids, potentially problematic for hydraulics and downstream handling [S07].
- High ionic strength and added surfactants generally improved foam stability and PFAS removal [S01-E07, S03-E07, S09, S10-E05, S16-E07], but also increase co‑transfer of non‑PFAS organics.

### 5.2 Energy Demand

Direct measured FF energy use is sparse; we rely on:

- Qualitative characterizations: FF is described as **low energy, low consumables**, particularly compared to membrane and EO systems [S01-E01; S08-E04; S16-E05].
- Conceptual modelling:  
  - For multi‑stage FF treating 1,000,000 m³ groundwater, total FF energy was assumed to be ~0.8 kWh/m³, i.e., 800,000 kWh [S14].
  - HALT treatment of the resulting concentrates added only 2.5–6.7% more energy in the 2‑stage scenario and <0.03% in the 3‑stage scenario [S14].
- Life‑cycle GWP surrogates (landfill leachate FF):
  - One‑stage 20% foam fraction: 818 kg CO₂‑eq per 1000 m³ [S12].
  - One‑stage 1% foam fraction: 357 kg CO₂‑eq per 1000 m³ [S12].
  - Three‑stage FF: 402 kg CO₂‑eq per 1000 m³ [S12].

These LCA values integrate compressed‑air energy and foam waste incineration burdens; they confirm that **lower foam fractions and multistage concentration can materially reduce life‑cycle climate impacts** while producing a much smaller hazardous waste volume.

Energy data for hybrid FF+EO systems are not quantitatively given, but qualitative statements indicate that treating all water directly with EO is markedly more energy‑intensive than treating only concentrated foam, due to ~10‑fold larger treated volume [S08-E03].

## 6. Management and Downstream Treatment of PFAS‑Rich Foamate

Foamate management is central to any FF‑based treatment train.

### 6.1 Direct Disposal with Incineration

- The SAFF tertiary foamate at AACO is stored on‑site and the default disposal pathway is **high‑temperature incineration**, pending development of on‑site destruction cells [S13].
- LCA scenarios for landfill leachate FF assume **hazardous waste incineration** for foam wastes as the base‑case end‑of‑life option [S12].
- These LCAs highlight:
  - A one‑stage 20% foam fraction produces 200 m³ foam waste per 1000 m³ leachate treated and has the highest GWP [S12].
  - A 1% one‑stage and multistage low‑foam configurations substantially reduce foam waste volume and GWP [S12, S12-E04].

Thus, **overly large foamate fractions (e.g., 20%) can dominate life‑cycle environmental footprint via incineration burdens**, even if PFAS removal is good.

### 6.2 Electrochemical Oxidation (EO) of Foamate

Multiple studies evaluate EO as a destruction step for PFAS‑rich concentrates:

- **Pilot FF + EO (groundwater, leachate)**  
  - Overall mean PFAS degradation across FF+EO treatment trains was ~50%, with long‑chains degraded up to 86% and short‑chains up to 31% [S08].
  - EO of leachate‑derived foam improved toxicity metrics dramatically (TTR binding reductions up to 89%; bacterial toxicity up to 94% in some cases), but PFSA degradation in foam was limited, leaving persistent sulfonates [S08].
  - Authors explicitly recommend verifying at full scale that all PFAS removed in FF end up in foam and are degraded in EO, and note low PFSA degradation as a key limitation [S08].

- **Radiolabeled 14C‑PFOA systems (synthetic and natural waters)**  
  - Stirred‑batch FF removed 80–90% of 14C‑PFOA from synthetic solutions in 30 min, with 90–100% of PFOA in the foamate mineralized to ¹⁴CO₂ during EO [S06].
  - In 3500 mL double‑tank tests, FF removed 90% of PFOA from WWTP effluent but only 50% from drainage‑ditch water; EO half‑lives for PFOA in foamate were 8.7–15 min for both matrices, again with mineralization of the PFOA carbon to ¹⁴CO₂ [S06].

EO is clearly effective for some PFAS in foamate (notably PFOA), but **sulfonates and complex mixtures remain problematic**, and energy demand is non‑negligible, though reduced by concentrating PFAS first.

### 6.3 Hydrothermal Alkaline Treatment (HALT) of Foam Concentrates

- Two foam concentrates from FF field demonstrations (FC1 primary foam and FC2 tertiary foam) with initial PFAS up to g/L levels were treated by HALT at 350 °C, 1 M NaOH [S14].
- Results:
  - >90% **degradation and defluorination** of all 62 identified PFAS (anions, cations, zwitterions) within 90 min [S14].
  - Inorganic fluoride production was consistent with essentially complete C–F bond cleavage [S14].
- Residuals:
  - Even after a hypothetical 99% PFAS mass removal, residual PFAS concentrations in the small treated concentrate volume are expected to be mg/L–µg/L, still orders of magnitude above original groundwater; blending treated concentrate back into the large FF influent stream is proposed instead of direct discharge [S14].
  - HALT‑recalcitrant dissolved organic matter and NaOH are likewise proposed to be managed via dilution in the large influent stream [S14].

Conceptual energy analysis suggests HALT adds only a few percent to the FF energy footprint if applied to small concentrate volumes [S14]. HALT is therefore a compelling **second‑stage destruction option for highly concentrated foamates**.

### 6.4 Other Proposed Downstream Options

- **Plasma and electrochemical oxidation** are repeatedly proposed for treating concentrated foamates from FF of leachate and groundwater; prior studies cited in S01 show both effective PFAS removal, with plasma avoiding short‑chain byproducts observed under EO [S01].
- **Evaporation plus incineration of residual solids** is proposed for NF‑concentrate foamates [S09].
- **Polishing adsorption (anion exchange resins)** is recommended for short‑chain‑enriched FF retentates (not foamate) in drinking‑water applications [S09].

### 6.5 Treatment Train Design Considerations

The evidence supports FF as an advantageous concentration step when:

- **Influent PFAS concentrations are modest to high** (leachate, NF concentrates, AFFF‑impacted groundwater), allowing reasonable PFAS mass recovery in foam, and
- **Downstream destructive capacity is constrained**: by concentrating PFAS into <1–10% of the original volume, energy‑intensive technologies (EO, HALT, plasma) or costly incineration can be applied to a much smaller mass of water, improving feasibility [S01, S08, S09, S11, S12, S14, S16].

However, **FF alone does not yield regulatory‑compliant discharges in challenging matrices** (e.g., landfill leachate), particularly due to short‑chains and precursors [S15, S16].

## 7. When Is Foam Fractionation “Useful” as a Concentration Step?

Synthesizing across lab, pilot and commercial evidence:

### 7.1 Most Favorable Situations

Foam fractionation is most advantageous as a concentration step when:

1. **PFAS profile is dominated by long‑chain PFAA**  
   – Long‑chains show near‑complete removal in groundwater, NF concentrate, and leachate at bench, pilot and commercial scale [S01, S03, S09, S11, S13, S15, S16].

2. **Matrix foaming and ionic strength are supportive**  
   – High ionic strength leachate and NF concentrates exhibit robust foam formation and enhanced adsorption [S01-E07, S09, S11, S16-E07].

3. **A destructive or secure disposal route for foamate exists**  
   – Incineration capacity [S12, S13], EO [S06; S08], plasma [S01], or HALT [S14] is available or can be added.

4. **Treatment goals focus on bulk mass removal**  
   – E.g., reducing load to downstream GAC/IX or meeting intermediate discharge standards where short‑chains at tens–hundreds ng/L are acceptable.

5. **Integration with existing aeration is feasible**  
   – FF can be bolted onto aeration steps in wastewater or leachate systems with added foam collection [S01-E01, S07, S11].

6. **Life‑cycle impacts of foam waste can be controlled**  
   – Using low foam fractions and/or multistage designs to minimize hazardous waste volume and incineration burden [S12, S14].

### 7.2 Less Favorable Situations

FF is less suitable as the primary concentration strategy when:

- **Short‑chain PFAS dominate the risk profile** and very low discharge limits must be achieved; even with co‑surfactants and advanced FF, short‑chains are only partially removed [S01, S03, S10, S11, S15, S16].
- **Matrix has low foaming potential** (e.g., biologically treated wastewater) and surfactant addition is undesirable because of residual surfactant toxicity and foaming downstream [S07, S10].
- **Sludge or high solids are present**, as FF co‑concentrates solids and PFAS, complicating foamate handling and potentially requiring dewatering steps [S07].
- **Robust destruction is not available** and the only feasible endpoint is long‑distance incineration of large foam volumes; here, high foam fractions (e.g., 20%) can generate burdensome waste volumes and life‑cycle impacts [S12, S13].

## 8. Evidence Gaps and Specific Research Needs

Key gaps and needs emerging from this evidence set include:

1. **Quantitative energy use and operational stability for full‑scale FF**  
   - Most operational energy data are modelled or qualitative [S12; S14; S16-E05]. Long‑term pilot/commercial campaigns with measured kWh/m³ and maintenance history are needed, particularly for leachate and municipal wastewater.

2. **Short‑chain PFAS control at environmentally relevant concentrations**  
   - While co‑surfactants and hybrid MER systems improve short‑chain removal in some settings [S05, S07, S09, S10], robust performance at ng/L levels in complex matrices remains uncertain. Comparative studies of co‑surfactant types vs ozone vs low‑pH operation on real waters would be valuable.

3. **Foamate speciation & byproducts across downstream technologies**  
   - EO data show persistent sulfonates in treated foam [S08]. HALT achieves high defluorination but leaves non‑mineralized organics [S14]. Systematic PFAS and co‑contaminant speciation (including precursors) through FF+EO, FF+HALT, and FF+plasma trains is needed.

4. **Aerosol emissions and air‑phase PFAS**  
   - Pilot FF and NF–FF studies document aerosols and PFOS in aerosol traps [S01, S09]. Field‑scale controls and emission measurements are necessary to ensure FF does not simply shift PFAS from water to air.

5. **Integrated treatment‑train optimization**  
   - Practical demonstration of multistage FF + HALT with internal recirculation [S14] and NF+FF+AIX configurations [S09] is pending. Detailed techno‑economic and environmental optimization (foam fraction vs short‑chain capture vs incineration burden) is particularly important, as highlighted by the LCA work [S12].

6. **Regulatory alignment for residuals**  
   - Several field and pilot studies reach long‑chain concentration targets while exceeding stringent multi‑PFAS sum criteria due to residual short‑chains [S15, S16]. Policy‑driven definitions of acceptable residual PFAS classes and concentrations will shape how FF‑based trains are designed.

---

## 9. Conclusions

The compiled evidence supports foam fractionation as a **mature, scalable separation technology** that:

- Efficiently removes and concentrates **long‑chain PFAS**,  
- Provides substantial **volume reduction and enrichment** of PFAS‑rich streams, enabling more feasible downstream destruction, and  
- Can be integrated with NF, EO, HALT, plasma or incineration as part of a **Separation + Concentration + Destruction** treatment train [S08; S09; S12; S13; S14].

However, FF:

- **Does not destroy PFAS**,  
- Often leaves **short‑chain PFAS and precursors** at levels incompatible with the lowest health‑based criteria, and  
- Produces foamate that is **co‑contaminated with organics and sometimes solids**, requiring deliberate, often energy‑intensive downstream management.

FF is thus best viewed as a **front‑end concentrator**—highly useful when appropriately coupled to robust destruction or polishing steps and when designed to minimize foam waste volumes and life‑cycle impacts.
