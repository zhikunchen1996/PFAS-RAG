## 1. Overview and Scope

Foam fractionation (FF) removes PFAS by exploiting their accumulation at gas–water interfaces and subsequent foam harvesting. Across the evidence base, FF is applied to:

- relatively simple synthetic waters (single or few PFAS; DI water) [R01, R02, R03, R04],
- natural waters and municipal wastewaters [R05, R02, R06, R04],
- landfill leachate [R07, R08, R09, R10, R11, R12, R13, R14],
- nanofiltration (NF) concentrates [R15],
- AFFF-impacted groundwater [R01, R08, R16, R11, R15].

The review below focuses on how PFAS molecular properties, water chemistry, and reactor operation collectively control:

- **removal** (decrease in aqueous or leachate-phase PFAS),
- **recovery** (fraction of removed PFAS captured in foamate),
- **enrichment** (foamate concentration relative to feed),
- **mass-balance closure** (accounting of all PFAS among streams),
- **volume reduction** (ratio of feed volume to foamate or waste volume).

These endpoints are treated explicitly as distinct; trends are only generalized within the experimental conditions in which they were demonstrated.

## 2. PFAS Molecular Properties: Chain Length and Functional Group

### 2.1 Chain-length dependence under controlled conditions

Several studies confirm that, under otherwise fixed conditions, longer-chain PFAS are more effectively stripped into foam than short-chain analogues:

- In DI water at 100 µg/L each of PFBA (C4 PFCA), PFOA (C8 PFCA), 6:2 FTS, and PFOS (C8 PFSA), single‑stage batch FF over 2 min gave highest removals of 31% (PFBA), 66% (PFOA), 62% (6:2 FTS), and 88% (PFOS) as air flow increased to 15 L/min (PFOS optimum at 10 L/min) [R01]. This demonstrates strong chain-length and headgroup dependence in a low-ionic-strength, co‑surfactant‑free system.

- In synthetic groundwater without cosurfactant, semi‑batch vacuum FF (up to 120 min) with initial ~40 µg/L of each PFOS, PFOA, PFBS (C4 PFSA) and PFBA (C4 PFCA) produced plateau removals of ~90% for PFOS and ~60% for PFOA; PFBS and PFBA removals were qualitatively reported as much lower under these conditions [R16].  

- In simulated PFAS wastewater with SDS co‑surfactant and no added salts, PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS (C6 PFSA) showed clear removal over time, whereas PFBA exhibited “barely discernible” removal at fixed SDS dose [R03].

- In Australian groundwater with SDS and background cations, PFHxS, PFOA and PFOS were reduced to below 0.1 ppb within 60 min, while PFBA could not be effectively removed [R03].

In short: **under both simple (DI) and real waters, with or without co‑surfactants, long‑chain PFAS (≥C6–C8) are consistently much more foam‑amenable than short‑chain PFBA/PFBS**, all else equal [R01, R16, R03, R15].

### 2.2 PFSA vs PFCA and precursor behavior

Functional group effects are evident when PFSA and PFCA of similar chain length are considered under given matrices:

- In MER foam fractionation of landfill leachate at pH ≈2, 98.3% of PFSAs and 85.5% of PFCAs were removed from leachate [R08]. Residual PFCAs (14.5%) were dominated by short‑chain PFBA, PFPeA, PFHxA [R08]. Short‑chain PFSAs (C≤6) had removal of 92.5–99.1%, versus 80.8–90% for short‑chain PFCAs (C≤6); long‑chain PFAS (C>6) of both classes were 96.1–100% removed [R08].  

- In the same study’s simple pH‑induced FF (pH 2), foam separation of PASFs and FTCAs (precursors) was ≥87% and higher than PFCAs (79.3%) or PFSAs (83.6%) [R08], indicating even stronger interfacial activity of some precursors in that high‑ionic-strength, low‑pH matrix.

- NF–FF on AFFF‑impacted groundwater concentrate: without co‑surfactant, continuous pilot FF achieved 90% total PFAS removal; with cationic Montaline C40, removal increased to 94% [R15]. PFOA, PFPeS, PFHxS, PFOS all achieved 99% removal with or without co‑surfactant; short-chain PFCAs and PFBS were more sensitive to cosurfactant (PFPeA 59% with +37 percentage points vs no co‑surfactant; PFHxA 99% with +9 points; PFBS 96% with +34 points) [R15].

Thus, within the same system, **perfluorosulfonates and some precursors often partition more strongly to foam than perfluorocarboxylates of similar chain length**, especially under low pH and high ionic strength [R08, R15]. Short-chain PFCAs and PFBA remain the hardest to remove; their behavior can be shifted but seldom matched to long-chain PFAS performance.

### 2.3 Multi‑component competitive effects

Where PFAS are present as complex mixtures, competitive adsorption and matrix effects complicate simple chain‑length relationships:

- Bench FF on landfill leachate showed total PFAS removal 72–86% in batch [R09], but individual species grouped into:
  - 95–≥90% for PFOS, PFOA, PFHxS, PFHpA [R09],
  - 50–80% for PFNA, PFPeS, PFHxA, PFHpS, PFBS,
  - 0–50% for EtFOSAA, PFBA, PFDA, FOSA, PFPeA, MeFOSAA [R09].  

- In municipal wastewater FF, long‑chain PFAS were also more effectively removed than short‑chain analogues, but quantitative values are not given in the excerpt; importantly, removal differences are confounded by residence time (10 vs 30 min) and matrix stage (MBR influent vs sedimentation effluent) [R05].

Overall, **structural trends are robust (long‑chain > short‑chain; PFSA ≳ PFCA), but quantitative performance is strongly modulated by matrix‑specific competition and operating parameters**.

## 3. Water Chemistry Effects: Co‑surfactants, Organic Matter, Salts, and Competing Constituents

### 3.1 Co‑surfactants and thickeners

#### Hydrocarbon and functionalized co‑surfactants

A wide range of co‑surfactants have been tested to promote foam stability and enhance short‑chain PFAS removal; their effects are highly matrix‑ and dose‑dependent.

**Hydrocarbon surfactants in relatively clean waters**

- In simulated PFAS wastewater (no added salts), SDS at 3.5 mg/min enabled removal of PFOS, PFOA and PFHxS while PFBA remained essentially unremoved; this reflects the residual chain-length limitation even with co‑surfactant [R03].

- In Australian groundwater, SDS as co‑foaming agent allowed PFHxS, PFOA, and PFOS to fall below 0.1 ppb in 60 min, but PFBA was still not removed [R03].

- In synthetic groundwater and AFFF‑impacted groundwater, CTAB enhanced removal of short-chain PFBS and PFBA, with overall PFAS recovery, volume reduction factor (VRF), enrichment factor (EF) and co‑surfactant dose trading off against each other [R16]. At lower CTAB mass flow rates, VRF was roughly an order of magnitude greater than PFAS mass recovery, so EF was high despite incomplete recovery; at higher CTAB dosing, recovery increased due to more rapid formation of stable foam, but EF fell relative to VRF as foam volumes increased [R16]. This directly illustrates that **removal, recovery, EF, and volume reduction respond differently to co‑surfactant dosing.**

**Specialized co‑surfactants in complex matrices**

- In NF‑concentrate FF (Trial 1), a cationic surfactant (Montaline C40) increased total PFAS removal by 17 percentage points, while Marlinat 242/28 (+2), Simulsol SL10 (+4), and LAS (−3) had weaker or negative effects [R15]. For short-chain PFAS in this concentrate, Montaline C40 at the highest dose increased removal by 67 percentage points (PFPeA), 59 (PFHxA), 83 (PFBS), and 44 (PFPeS) relative to no co‑surfactant [R15]. However, total PFAS enrichment factor and foamate recovery *decreased* with increasing co‑surfactant dose, and PFAS mass balance averaged only 78%, suggesting adhesion to column walls [R15].  

- In the same NF‑concentrate system under continuous pilot operation (Trial 2), Montaline C40 increased total PFAS removal from 90 to 94% and markedly improved short-chain removal (PFPeA +37 points, PFBS +34), but foamate still represented only 2% of NF feed volume, with PFAS concentration 41× the feed [R15]. Foamate recovery averaged 82%, and mass balance closure 89% [R15].

- In municipal wastewater FF, adding anionic KAD(–) or zwitterionic K15(±) reduced PFBA removal from 15% (no surfactant) to 1–2% at 30‑min residence in MBR sedimentation effluent [R05]. This indicates that **co‑surfactant addition can *decrease* removal for some short‑chain PFAS when co‑surfactant–PFAS interactions are unfavorable or compete with air–water interfacial adsorption**.

- In the OTCS–micro‑nanobubble system (synthetic water, PFOA/PFOS), octanoyl‑modified chitosan functions as a co‑surfactant. With MNBs, PFOA and PFOS removal reached 98.73% and 99.21%, respectively, 1.47‑ and 1.29‑fold higher than OTCS with conventional air bubbles [R02]. CFs in synthetic water were 6.25–7.22 for PFOA and 5.72–6.60 for PFOS [R02]. Here co‑surfactant is central to both foam formation and adsorption.

**Thickeners and high‑NOM matrices**

- In AFFF‑contaminated groundwater treated by FF, HPMC (a thickener) contributed 93.9% of TOC; TOC recovery (111.3%) and the fact that 87.2% of TOC in foamate was from HPMC show that HPMC was almost entirely transferred to foam [R01]. PFASs accounted for <0.05% of TOC [R01]. HPMC greatly aids foam formation but introduces a large organic load; in this study it was efficiently removed to foam, but implications for downstream foam handling are non‑trivial.

- In activated‑sludge FF, solids and extracellular biopolymers act as “natural” surfactants. Mixed‑liquor TSS (~3.6 g/L) is enriched 2–3× in foamate in previous work [R06], and here foam‑forming bacteria (Gordonia, Microthrix) increased foamate fraction (Run B > C > A), but higher foamate volumes did **not** produce additional aqueous PFAS concentration reduction at low influent PFAS (<25 ng/L); the process was mass‑transfer limited [R06]. Thus microbial surfactants alter foaming and EF but not necessarily bulk removal under these conditions.

**Key implications**

- Co‑surfactants can dramatically enhance removal of short‑chain PFAS in some matrices (e.g., CTAB, Montaline C40 in relatively clean or NF‑concentrate waters [R16, R15]) but may be neutral or detrimental in others (municipal wastewater with KAD(–) or K15(±) for PFBA [R05]; LAS in NF concentrate [R15]).
- Higher co‑surfactant dose usually increases foam volume and short‑chain removal but tends to **decrease enrichment** and may reduce foamate recovery and mass-balance closure via wall losses [R15].
- Co‑surfactant residues in FF effluent and foamate represent additional contaminant streams; surfactant toxicity and treatability must be addressed (explicitly noted for municipal wastewater surfactants [R05]).

### 3.2 Salinity, cations, and ionic strength

Salts exert both beneficial and detrimental effects, largely via:

- enhancing PFAS and surfactant adsorption at the air–water interface (salting-out; screening),
- suppressing foam at high concentrations via surfactant–cation precipitation.

In simulated wastewater with SDS and controlled salts:

- Foam fractionation performance increased with cation charge density: Mg²⁺ > Na⁺ > K⁺, consistent with increased surfactant adsorption [R03].  
- At Na⁺ >100 mM, K⁺ >10 mM, Mg²⁺ >10 mM, or Ca²⁺ in 0.1–10 mM (with >4 ppm SDS), cation–SDS complexes precipitated and foam formation was suppressed, inhibiting PFAS removal [R03].

In real waters:

- Landfill leachate has high ionic strength; Vo et al. exploited this for FF and found short-chain PFAS removal could approach 93.7–99.99% for long‑chain PFAS but remained limited for short‑chain species unless combined with suitable co‑surfactants [R07].  
- Pilot‑scale FF on landfill leachate explicitly notes the **beneficial effect of high ionic strength on FF** and reduced fouling risk versus membrane technologies [R13].
- NF concentrate is inherently high in salts; NF increased PFAS 5× (393 ng/L vs 77 ng/L feed) while raising ionic strength and lowering DOC and hardness [R15]. FF on this concentrate achieved 90–94% total PFAS removal without operational clogging [R15].

Thus, **moderate salinity and “hard” waters can improve FF by increasing surface activity and reducing CMC, but high salt with ionic co‑surfactants can suppress foam** [R03, R13, R15]. Modeling of cation effects translated for SDS may not directly transfer to all co‑surfactants or mixed cation systems, but provides mechanistic guidance.

### 3.3 Natural organic matter, particulates, and competing surface‑active species

Organic matter and particulates compete for bubble surface area and modify foam stability:

- In OTCS–MNB treatment of four natural lake/reservoir waters (turbidity 8.72–27.94 NTU), removal of PFOA/PFOS remained ≥80% [R02], but CFs were 6.67–10.02% lower on average than in synthetic water, and overall mass recovery was lower [R02]. The authors attribute this qualitatively to competitive adsorption and bubble coalescence at higher turbidity [R02].

- In activated-sludge mixed liquor, solids are preferentially partitioned to foam; total solids in foamate about twice those in influent in related work [R06]. This enriches PFAS and TSS concurrently, but can complicate foamate handling and may limit achievable enrichment at fixed hydrodynamics.

- In landfill leachate, McCleaf et al. saw total PFAS mass balance ~72% for batch FF, with ~30% of removed PFAS not found in foamate [R09]; possible sinks include wall adsorption and unquantified phases, potentially exacerbated by organic fouling and complex co‑contaminants.

Organic thickeners and biological surfactants improve foaming but **do not automatically translate to higher PFAS enrichment or removal**, especially when PFAS concentrations are low or PFAS compete poorly against other surface‑active species [R01, R05, R02, R06].

## 4. Reactor Operation: Gas Flow, Bubble Size/Type, Contact Time, Recycle, Foam Collection, and Configuration

### 4.1 Gas flow rate and contact time

In synthetic DI water without co‑surfactant, increasing air flow from 4 to 15 L/min (over 2 min) increased PFBA, PFOA and 6:2 FTS removal, with PFOS peaking at 10 L/min [R01]. Enrichment factors decreased with higher air flows due to larger foam volumes [R01]. Thus:

- **Higher gas rates** → more bubble surface area, higher removal **per unit time**, but larger foamate volumes and reduced EF.

In landfill leachate:

- Air and ozone FF at 0.5–1 L/min for 5 min showed that higher gas flows increased short-chain removal, but with trade‑offs in foamate volume and wettability [R10]. Ozone FF achieved >90% long‑chain removal at all tested flows but removed short‑chain PFAS 10–30% less effectively than air at the same flow [R10].

Residence time interacts strongly with matrix:

- Municipal wastewater FF: MBR influent treated for 10 min vs 30 min for MBR sedimentation effluent; lower PFAS removal in MBR influent was attributed partly to shorter residence time [R05].
- Many systems (groundwater, leachate, NF concentrate) showed that most removal of long‑chain PFAS occurs within the first 10–15 min of aeration [R07, R09, R13, R15].

### 4.2 Bubble size, micro‑nano bubbles, and generation method

Bubble size and generation method are rarely quantified directly in PFAS removal endpoints, but several mechanistic insights are available:

- MNBs in pure water had average diameter ≈620 nm with negative surface charge and lowered surface tension; this increased interfacial area, foam stability, and electrostatic repulsion between bubbles [R02]. In synthetic PFAS water, OTCS–MNBs significantly outperformed OTCS with conventional air bubbles in removal and CF [R02]. Under otherwise fixed OTCS dose and 30‑min run time, MNBs enhanced both removal and enrichment relative to conventional bubbles in that Plexiglas column.

- A venturi-based recirculation FF system in simulated wastewater (SDS co‑surfactant) relies on bubble size (r32) to compute surface area; hydrodynamic coupling between gas flow and recirculation complicates independent tuning of bubble size and flow [R03].

- In the landfill-leachate continuous pilot, the foam was “wet” because foam was skimmed just above the water surface at controlled effluent flow; the authors describe this as a mix of bubble flotation and foam fractionation [R13]. This collection geometry increases foamate volume and lowers EF for a given removal.

Collectively, **smaller, stable bubbles with high specific interfacial area (MNBs, optimized spargers) favor high removal and CF at fixed foam fraction**, but practical systems must accommodate gas–liquid hydrodynamics and fouling.

### 4.3 Batch, semi‑batch, and continuous configurations; stage trains and recycle

FF reactors span:

- 200–3500 mL stirred or column batch systems in synthetic waters [R01, R16, R03, R04],
- 3.1 L batch columns for municipal wastewater and sludge [R05, R06],
- 9 L columns for landfill leachate [R07],
- semi‑continuous columns for landfill leachate (5‑min runs) [R10],
- continuous pilot columns for leachate and NF concentrate [R13, R15],
- multi‑stage SAFF trains for AFFF‑impacted groundwater and leachate [R11, R12],
- MER combining foam fractionation and electrooxidation [R08].

Configuration materially affects performance metrics:

**Single‑ vs multi‑stage and recycle**

- Two‑stage bench FF on PFAS‑spiked DI water (~1500 mL at 100 µg/L each PFBA, PFOA, 6:2 FTS, PFOS) used foamate from stage 1 as feed to stage 2 under “optimal” single‑stage conditions; quantitative stagewise removal is not provided, but the design illustrates progressive concentration and volume reduction by re‑treating foamate [R01].

- Industrial SAFF on AFFF‑impacted groundwater (three stages, semi‑batch, with recycle of secondary/tertiary rejectate to primary feed) achieved feed PFAS 4.29 µg/L and primary-stage enrichment factor 5.3; overall enrichment after tertiary reached 870,000, with tertiary foamate stored for off‑site disposal [R11]. This is an extreme example of **volume reduction and enrichment**, not necessarily additional removal beyond what primary/secondary already achieve for the treated water.

- Commercial SAFF on landfill leachate achieved 98.8–100% removal of PFOA, PFOS, 6:2 FTS, PFNA, PFDA with influent 5–586 ng/L [R12]. Stage configuration is not fully specified, but this confirms that multi‑stage FF with recycle can reach regulatory‑relevant treated‑water levels while generating a small, highly concentrated foamate.

- LCA modeling comparing one‑stage (1% vs 20% foam fraction) and three‑stage arrangements for leachate shows environmental and cost trade‑offs at system scale but does not itself report PFAS removals [R14]. It assumes (using literature) that higher foam fraction favors short‑chain PFAS capture, while multi‑stage design improves overall short‑chain removal [R14].

**Batch vs continuous**

- Bench FF on landfill leachate (2.4 L) produced 72–86% total PFAS removal in batch mode [R09], while continuous bench/pilot units, at similar feed matrices and conditions, reached overall PFAS removal averages of ~81% [R09] and mass-balance closures of 66–104% [R13]. Continuous mode offered similar removal but with wet foam and more complex mass-balance interpretation.

- In NF concentrate FF, batch trials (Trial 1) showed 78% total PFAS removal without co‑surfactant [R15]; continuous pilot (Trial 2) achieved 90% without and 94% with co‑surfactant [R15], at 2% foamate volume and 41× enrichment vs NF feed [R15].

Thus, **continuous FF can match or exceed batch removal for comparable matrices and PFAS suites**, but foam collection mode (wet vs dry) and hydrodynamics complicate EF, VRF, and mass-balance interpretation.

### 4.4 Foam collection mode, vacuum vs overflow, and aerosol pathways

Foam collection strongly influences volume reduction, EF, and unmeasured PFAS pathways:

- In bench DI‑water FF, foam was vacuum‑collected, collapsed with methanol, and analyzed with bottom water, enabling straightforward mass accounting between water and foam phases [R01].

- In several columns (municipal wastewater, activated sludge, mixed liquor), inverted funnels under vacuum collected foam continuously into flasks, promoting relatively dry foamate and limiting aerosol escape [R05, R06].

- In the landfill‑leachate continuous pilot, foam was skimmed by controlling effluent flow with the foam outlet located just above the water–air interface; foamate was relatively wet, and operation was described as mixed bubble/foam fractionation [R13]. Foam fraction was adjusted between 3–30%, affecting foam concentration and removal but with only limited influence on mass-balance closure [R13].

- In NF‑concentrate FF, an aerosol trap experiment (no vacuum; foam allowed to accumulate) detected PFOS at 0.5±0.2 ng/L in trap water; other PFAS were below detection [R15]. Standard runs with vacuum foam collection and 200‑cm columns had higher mass-balance closure (89%) than earlier leachate FF (72%) [R15, R09].

- At the municipal‑wastewater lab scale with air recycle, aerosol PFAS losses were not quantified; risk was considered low due to recirculation but acknowledged as unmeasured [R05].

These results underscore that **recovery and mass-balance closure depend not just on removal but on foam collection efficiency and aerosol/wall losses**, which are configuration‑specific.

### 4.5 Hybrid reactors: MER and NF–FF–EO

Two hybrid systems illustrate additional operating levers:

- **MER foam fractionation** operates at pH≈2 in an anode chamber, using in situ electrochemistry to both generate foam and degrade precursors. MER achieved 95.6% total PFAS removal, 98.3% PFSA removal, 85.5% PFCA removal from leachate in 7 h, with complete loss (to below detection) of FTCAs and PASFs from defoamed leachate [R08]. Short‑chain PFAS removal ranged 80.8–99.1%, long‑chain 96.1–100% [R08]. No solids precipitates were formed; all separation was via foam [R08]. Here, **removal** includes both physical transfer to foam and electrochemical transformation of some precursors; **recovery** into foam is not fully resolved separately from degradation.

- **FF+EO** (Comfort et al.): 200‑mL stirred batch FF with CTAB and 14C-PFOA removed 80–90% of PFOA in 30 min [R04]. PFOA mass recovered in foamate ranged ~86–94% depending on pH and temperature [R04], EF ranged 1.30–1.64, and foamate volumes 110–140 mL [R04]. Subsequent EO of foamate on BDD electrodes mineralized 90–100% of PFOA (14COOH → 14CO₂) with half‑lives 8.7–15 min [R04]. FF+EO in WWTP effluent (3.5 L double‑tank) removed 90% of spiked 14C‑PFOA, but only 50% in a drainage ditch water [R04]. Here FF mainly enriches and transfers PFOA to a small foamate volume; EO provides **destruction**; combined performance cannot be interpreted as “removal by FF alone.”

## 5. Distinct Performance Measures: How They Interact and Trade Off

Across the corpus, five main quantitative measures appear:

1. **Removal efficiency (aqueous or leachate phase)**  
   % decrease from initial concentration or mass in the bulk liquid (feed, retentate, leachate).  
   – DI water: PFOS 88%, PFOA 66%, PFBA 31% in 2‑min single‑stage [R01].  
   – Leachate: long‑chain PFAS >90% by air or ozone FF under 5‑min runs [R10].  
   – Leachate: 72–86% total PFAS removal in batch FF [R09]; pilot continuous 76–81% across modes [R09, R13].  
   – NF concentrate: 90–94% total PFAS removal [R15].  
   – MER: 95.6% total PFAS removal, with class‑specific fractions [R08].  

2. **Recovery into foamate**  
   Fraction of *removed* PFAS mass captured in the foamate.  
   – Landfill leachate batch FF: mass ratio foamate/removed PFAS averaged 0.72 (≈72%) [R09].  
   – NF‑concentrate FF: foamate recovery 66% (Trial 1) and 82% (Trial 2) [R15].  
   – SAFF landfill leachate: not explicitly reported, but tertiary foamate clearly contains a minor fraction of total mass in a highly concentrated form [R11, R12].  
   Recovery is always ≤ removal; losses via wall adsorption or aerosols reduce recovery without necessarily reducing “removal” as measured in the bulk.

3. **Enrichment factor (EF)**  
   Ratio C_foamate / C_feed (or C_initial).  
   – Landfill leachate batch FF: EF averaged 2.9 vs theoretical 3.7 (assuming complete recovery) [R09].  
   – NF‑concentrate FF (continuous): foamate 41× NF feedwater concentration, with 2% foamate volume [R15].  
   – Synthetic water with OTCS–MNBs: CF (a form of EF) 6.25–7.22 for PFOA and 5.72–6.60 for PFOS [R02].  
   – SAFF primary stage: EF 5.3; after tertiary, overall EF 870,000 relative to original feed [R11].  

   EF increases with higher VRF and high recovery; at fixed PFAS mass, generating less foam (low foam fraction, higher VRF) increases EF but may compromise removal or mass balance if too aggressive.

4. **Volume reduction / foam fraction / VRF**  
   – Volume fraction of feed removed as foam (e.g., "% foam") or VRF=V_feed/V_foamate.  
   – Pilot leachate FF: foam fractions 3–30%; lower foam fractions produced higher foam concentrations but affected removal mainly below 10% foam [R13].  
   – NF‑concentrate FF: foamate ≈2% of NF feed volume (VRF ~50) [R15].  
   – SAFF multi‑stage: tertiary EF=870,000 implies extreme VRF across stages, far exceeding single‑stage values [R11].  
   – LCA modeling explicitly compares 1% vs 20% foam fraction scenarios and shows that **short‑chain PFAS removal generally drops at very low foam fractions (e.g., 60%→40%) even if long‑chain removal remains >90%** [R14].

5. **Mass-balance closure**  
   – Leachate batch FF: 72% average [R09].  
   – NF‑concentrate FF: 78% (Trial 1), 89% (Trial 2) [R15].  
   – Landfill leachate continuous FF: 66–104% for individual runs [R13].  
   – Batch FF on leachate in Smith et al.: 42% (bottom effluent) to 59% (top effluent) closure [R13].  

   Poor closure reflects unmeasured pathways (wall sorption, aerosols, stagnant zones) and analytical variability; it does **not** necessarily mean low removal from the treated stream. However, it undermines confidence in recovery and EF estimates, and can hide potential emissions (e.g., aerosolized PFOS observed in NF‑FF pilot [R15]).

These metrics answer different questions:

- **Removal**: How much PFAS leaves the treated water? (relevant for compliance)
- **Recovery**: How much of the removed PFAS is controllably captured in foamate? (relevant for downstream destruction/logistics)
- **Enrichment**: How concentrated is the waste stream? (relevant for designing EO/incineration)
- **Volume reduction / foam fraction**: How small is the waste volume? (relevant for storage, transport, costs)
- **Mass-balance closure**: How confident are we that untracked losses are minimal? (relevant for environmental risk assessment and scale‑up)

Optimizing one often compromises another. For instance, in CTAB‑enhanced groundwater FF [R16], low dosing rates yielded high VRF and EF but lower PFAS recovery; higher doses improved recovery but produced more foam, reducing EF relative to VRF.

## 6. Cross‑cutting Observations and Evidence Gaps

### 6.1 Short‑chain PFAS remain the limiting case

Even with optimized co‑surfactants and high ionic strength, short‑chain PFBA/PFBS/PFPeA/HxA typically lag:

- Cannot be removed in SDS‑assisted FF from Australian groundwater beyond detection limits, unlike longer PFAS [R03].
- Show 10–30% lower removal by ozone vs air FF at matched gas flow in leachate [R10].
- Require higher foam fractions and/or co‑surfactants for acceptable removal in pilot leachate FF [R13, R14].
- Benefit substantially from cationic co‑surfactants in NF concentrate (PFPeA gains +37 points with Montaline C40) [R15], but still have lower absolute EF and greater sensitivity to operating conditions than PFOS/PFOA.

Research needs include:

- systematic mapping of short‑chain removal vs foam fraction, co‑surfactant chemistry, and ionic strength under well‑characterized matrices,
- quantification of how much of apparent limited removal is mass‑transfer limited vs intrinsic surface activity.

### 6.2 Matrix complexity and competing surfactants

Complex matrices (leachate, sludge, WWTP effluents) introduce real conflicts:

- They **help** by providing ionic strength and “free” surfactants/organics that stabilize foam [R09, R13, R06].
- They **hurt** by competing for interfaces, adding wall‑adsorbing species, and complicating surfactant–PFAS pairing (e.g., anionic surfactants suppressing PFBA removal [R05]).

Many studies explicitly note that results are matrix‑specific and may not generalize to other waters or co‑surfactant suites [R01, R07, R05, R15].

### 6.3 Aerosols and unaccounted loss pathways

Several studies recognize aerosol or wall‑sorption pathways:

- NF–FF aerosol traps show low but measurable PFOS in exhaust (0.5±0.2 ng/L) [R15].
- Landfill‑leachate pilot FF authors call for exhaust air sampling to close mass balances and assess off‑gas emissions [R13].
- Municipal wastewater FF recycles off‑gas air but does not quantify aerosol PFAS [R05].

Given high surface activity of PFOS and other PFSA, **aerosol generation is a plausible emission route** whose magnitude remains poorly quantified, especially at field scale.

### 6.4 Hybrid and downstream destruction

FF is inherently a separation, not a degradation, step. Several efforts couple it with destructive technologies:

- MER combines foam fractionation with electrochemical degradation of precursors; however, total organofluorine and carbonaceous byproducts are not fully reconciled [R08].
- FF+EO (CTAB, BDD electrodes) achieves near‑complete PFOA destruction in foamate under well‑mixed, low‑volume conditions [R04].
- LCA modeling of FF + EO highlights trade‑offs in energy and greenhouse emissions as functions of air–water ratio and stage count [R14].

Yet **comprehensive PFAS mass balances including defluorination and organofluorine byproducts after destruction are generally missing**.

## 7. Implications for Design and Future Research Priorities

Based on the evidence:

1. **PFAS structure:**  
   - Long-chain PFSA and PFCA are reliably removed at ≥90% in well‑tuned FF across matrices [R01, R07, R08, R09, R10, R13, R15].  
   - Short-chain PFAS require tailored co‑surfactants and/or higher foam fractions; trade‑offs in EF and foam volume must be explicitly considered [R16, R03, R13–R15].

2. **Water chemistry:**  
   - Moderate to high ionic strength is generally favorable, but high salinity with ionic surfactants can suppress foam via precipitation [R03].  
   - Co‑surfactant selection should consider PFAS charge, background organics, and salt regime; cationic surfactants often improve short-chain removal in relatively clean or NF‑concentrate waters, but can be ineffective or detrimental in complex municipal wastewaters [R05, R16, R15].  
   - Organic thickeners (e.g., HPMC) and bio‑surfactants (sludge communities) substantially affect foam behavior and must be accounted for in both removal and mass-balance analyses [R01, R06].

3. **Reactor operation:**  
   - Gas flow and residence time must be tuned jointly: higher flows/longer times increase removal but potentially at the cost of EF and energy [R01, R10, R13].  
   - Micro‑nano bubbles show promise for simultaneous high removal and enrichment, at the cost of higher energy per m³ (here 8.83 kWh/m³) [R02].  
   - Foam collection geometry (wet vs dry) and use of vacuum strongly influence foamate volume, EF, and aerosol losses [R13, R15].

4. **Performance metrics:**  
   - System design must specify target **removal**, **recovery**, **EF**, and **foam fraction** separately, rather than optimizing a single metric.  
   - For downstream EO or incineration, achieving high EF and volume reduction (e.g., SAFF tertiary EF 870,000; NF‑FF foamate 2% of feed at 41× concentration [R11, R15]) is valuable even if overall removal is only modestly improved between stages.  
   - Mass-balance closure should be monitored routinely; unclosed balances in the 40–80% range indicate potential hidden emissions or process inefficiencies [R09, R13, R15].

5. **Research gaps:**
   - Quantitative aerosol and off‑gas measurements across scales and matrices (NF concentrate, leachate, mixed liquor).  
   - Systematic experiments isolating bubble size distribution effects (including MNBs) at fixed gas throughput and water chemistry.  
   - Long-term co‑surfactant fate, especially for cationic surfactants and functionalized biopolymers, in both treated effluents and residual foams [R05, R16, R02, R15].  
   - Integrated treatment trains (e.g., NF→FF→EO) with full PFAS and organofluorine mass balances, including byproduct speciation [R08, R04, R15].  
   - Validation of LCA/LCCA predictions with real operational data, particularly around optimal foam fraction and compressed‑air energy use [R14].

In sum, foam fractionation is a demonstrably effective physical separation for long‑chain PFAS across a wide range of waters, and—with appropriate co‑surfactants and operating strategies—can substantially address many short‑chain PFAS as well. Its performance, however, is inseparable from the interplay of PFAS structure, water chemistry (salts, organics, co‑surfactants), and reactor operation. Robust design and scale‑up therefore require **explicit, multi‑metric optimization** rather than reliance on “percent removal” alone.
