## Overview

Membrane-based PFAS treatment is intrinsically a separation step; it creates PFAS‑rich residual streams and PFAS‑bearing solid materials that must be characterized and managed across the whole treatment train. The evidence base here spans:

- High‑pressure NF/RO for groundwater, landfill leachate, wastewater, and textile wastewater [R01,R02,R03,R04,R05,R06].
- Foam fractionation (FF) applied to raw waters, leachates, and NF/RO concentrates [R07,R08,R09,R06].
- Sorbent polishing (GAC, IX) and regenerable IX systems that shift PFAS into solids or regenerant still bottoms [R02,R10,R11].
- PFAS in membranes themselves and their leaching [R12,R13].
- Destructive technologies coupled to concentrates, principally UV‑sulfite and electrochemical oxidation (EO) [R03,R08].

Below, I address the three requested components.

---

## 1. How recovery, concentration factor, PFAS composition, and co‑contaminants define membrane‑derived residual streams

### 1.1 Aqueous concentrates from NF/RO

**Groundwater NF/RO**

- In municipal groundwater, pilot NF at 80% recovery reduced total detectable PFAS from ~77 to 1.4 ng/L (98% removal) and generated a 20% concentrate at 393 ng/L, about 5× the feed concentration [R06]. Individual removals spanned 89–99% depending on PFAS (e.g., PFOS‑C8 99%, PFHxA/PFHxS/PFPeS 97%) [R06].  
  → The concentrate is a 5‑fold enriched PFAS stream with concomitant concentration of hardness, U‑238 (97% removal to permeate), and DOC (2.7 → <1 mg/L in permeate; elevated in concentrate) [R06]. This co‑contaminant profile affects downstream options (e.g., scaling, fouling, and some destruction processes).

- AFFF‑impacted groundwater treated by a closed‑circuit RO (CCMS) at 80–90% recovery produced a retentate stream explicitly identified as a “concentrated PFAS-containing waste stream (retentate)” whose management is a “major concern” [R12]. At higher recovery, retentate PFAS concentrations increase and permeate quality declines; high recovery improves volume reduction but worsens retentate treatability and permeate residuals [R12].

**AFFF‑impacted groundwater, NF vs RO concentrates**

- For an AFFF‑impacted groundwater, bench testing of NF vs RO at 97% recovery showed that, although RO achieved higher PFAS rejection, the **RO concentrate retained much higher levels of co‑contaminants**: sodium 3000 vs 1700 mg/L, nitrate 370 vs 4 mg/L, silica 270 vs 12 mg/L, and DOC >100 vs 37 mg/L for NF at the same recovery [R03].  
  → This defines a *more saline and organic‑rich* RO concentrate, with higher fouling/scaling potential and stronger inhibition of photochemical PFAS treatment (especially via nitrate) than NF concentrate [R03].  
  → PFAS in both concentrates fall in the 250–500 g/mol MW range [R03].

**Hybrid NF–UV‑sulfite pilot**

- In a pilot NF–UV‑sulfite train for AFFF‑impacted groundwater, each 568 L batch was concentrated to 57 L reject (10% of the volume; 90% recovery) [R03]. NF rejection of individual PFAS averaged 92–98% over 30 cycles, with longer‑chain PFAS more strongly rejected [R03].  
  → PFAS were concentrated by a factor consistent with 90% recovery [R03], and the reject also contained elevated nitrate, silica, DOC, and divalent cations that are known to inhibit UV‑sulfite performance or promote fouling [R03].  
  → Thus, the NF reject entering UV‑sulfite is both PFAS‑rich and chemically complex.

**Wastewater NF**

- Loose NF270 on tertiary wastewater at 90% recovery provided >92% rejection of C≥4 PFAAs and >98% rejection of GenX, with 63–92% rejection of ultrashort PFAS and FBSA [R02]. DOC dropped from 4.2 to 0.9 mg/L in the permeate [R02].  
  → This implies a concentrate at ~10% of the feed volume with enriched PFAS (especially longer chain) and elevated EfOM and salts—explicitly identified as a separate PFAS‑laden waste stream requiring destructive treatment [R02].

**Textile wastewater UF–RO**

- In a UF‑RO hybrid on cotton textile wastewater, 11 PFAS were detected in influent; RO permeate PFAS were mostly below LOQ [R04]. PFAS load in the effluent dropped from 14.61 mg/day to 0.602 mg/day (and 3653 to 150.5 mg/year) across UF+RO [R04].  
  → The authors did not measure retentate PFAS concentrations, acknowledging that retentate disposal is “one of the biggest problems” in membrane PFAS treatment [R04]. Given batch circulation, the retentate is expected to accumulate PFAS along with the high organic/surfactant load characteristic of textile wastewater (COD, surfactants, dyes, phenols, etc. [R04]).

**Full‑scale municipal AO–MBR–RO**

- In a full‑scale AO–MBR–RO plant, >94% of PFAS were rejected to the brine, with only trace PF4OPeA (9 ng/L) in permeate [R05]. ΣPFAS in brine was 296 ng/L, with substantial contributions from PF4OPeA (185 ng/L), PFOS (70.5 ng/L), PFOA (10.28 ng/L), PFHxA (17.3 ng/L), PFHxS (9.12 ng/L), and 6:2 FTS (3.87 ng/L) [R05].  
  → Mass‑balance estimates indicate ~4120 mg/day of ΣPFAS shifted into RO brine and ~1 kg ΣPFAS/year released in mixed effluent predominantly composed of RO brine [R05].  
  → PFAS composition in brine skews toward both short‑chain PFAAs and precursors (6:2 FTS, PF4OPeA), making this brine a critical residual stream.

**Landfill leachate RO concentrate**

- A national‑scale model for Thailand assumes two‑pass RO on biologically treated landfill leachate, producing concentrate rich in “salts, impurities, and PFAS” [R01]. That concentrate is either:
  - Sent to evaporation ponds, with residual sediments incinerated [R01], or
  - Recirculated back into landfills, eliminating the explicit concentrate disposal infrastructure but not PFAS mass [R01].  
  PFAS concentrations in the concentrate were not measured [R01]; characterization is qualitative.

### 1.2 Foam fractionation concentrates and still bottoms

**Raw matrices (leachate, AFFF groundwater, WWTP effluent)**

- Bench‑scale FF on landfill leachate produced PFAS enrichment factors of ~10× in the foam after three stages and transferred ~80% of TOC to the foam, while PFAS comprised <0.01% of TOC [R07]. Mass balances for PFAS and TOC (70–130% and ~103% recovery, respectively) were “satisfactory” [R07].  
  → The foam concentrate is dominated by non‑PFAS organic matter even though PFAS are enriched relative to the feed; this has direct implications for destruction technologies sensitive to organic load.

- For AFFF‑contaminated groundwater with added hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC), PFAS constituted <0.05% of TOC and HPMC contributed 93.9% of TOC in the pre‑treated solution; TOC recovery was 111.3% [R07].  
  → Again, foam concentrates carry most of the thickener‑derived organics with PFAS representing a very small fraction of TOC.

**NF/RO concentrate FF**

- NF concentrate from municipal groundwater (PFAS ≈ 5× feed) fed to batch FF: without co‑surfactant, total PFAS removal averaged 78±4%, with ≥94–95% removal for long‑chain PFOA, PFHxS, PFOS [R06]. A cationic co‑surfactant (Montaline C40) increased total PFAS removal by 17 percentage points and dramatically improved removal of C4–C5 PFAS (e.g., PFPeA +67 points, PFHxA +59, PFBS +83, PFPeS +44) [R06].  
  → The foamate is a further‑concentrated PFAS stream; quantitative enrichment factors are not given in the excerpt, but mass is clearly shifted from NF concentrate into a smaller foam phase, with composition strongly dependent on PFAS chain length and added surfactants.

- Pilot‑scale continuous FF on landfill leachate achieved a ΣPFAS removal plateau at ~60%, constrained by the inlet composition: ~46% short‑chain PFCA were “only marginally removed” [R09]. Co‑contaminant chemistry shows similar Ca/Na/K in influent, effluent, and foam, but P and Fe are modestly higher in the foam [R09], indicating some inorganic carry‑over into the foamate.

**Regenerant still bottoms**

- Regeneration of PFAS‑laden AERs with methanolic brine followed by distillation recovered >90% of methanol with negligible PFAS in the recovered solvent, leaving a **minimal‑volume aqueous still‑bottoms stream** containing concentrated PFAS [R11].  
  - Example regeneration conditions: regenerable resins required 10 bed volumes (BVs) of 70% methanolic brine; PFAS‑selective resins required 30 BVs of 90% methanol or n‑propanol brine [R11].  
  - Distillation thereby converts tens of BVs of regenerant to <10 BVs of still bottoms capturing PFAS from >100,000 BVs of treated groundwater in illustrative scenarios [R11].  
  → Still bottoms are high‑strength PFAS concentrates with high salt and residual cosolvent load, singled out as the primary candidate for high‑energy destruction [R11].

### 1.3 Solid PFAS‑bearing residuals

**Treatment solids within membrane trains**

- In a pilot CCMS treating AFFF‑impacted groundwater, methanol extraction of solids showed PFOS dominated sorbed PFAS (60–85% of mass) across 0.2 μm cartridge filters, RO membrane sections, greensand, and ion‑exchange softening resin [R12].  
  - On a per‑gram basis, the 0.2 μm filter accumulated the most PFAS; RO membrane and softening resin were similar; greensand was lowest [R12].  
  → These pretreatment and membrane elements are PFAS‑bearing residuals even when permeate meets water‑quality goals.

- Long‑chain PFAS at low aqueous concentrations “preferentially adsorb” to these materials [R12], implying that solids can store substantial PFAS mass even when feed concentrations are modest.

**Sludge and biosolids in membrane‑coupled WWTPs**

- In the AO–MBR–RO WWTP, ΣPFAS in sludge increased from 3097 ng/kg (primary sludge) to 69,184 ng/kg in dried sludge, with PFOS dominating throughout (e.g., 45,043 ng/kg in secondary sludge) and PF4OPeA enriched in biological sludge (up to 8200 ng/kg) [R05]. Final dried biosolids reached ΣPFAS ≈ 69,814 ng/kg [R05].  
  - Annual PFAS release via biosolids was estimated at 1.7 kg ΣPFAS/year [R05].  
  → Sludge processing concentrates PFAS; biosolids are a major solid residual pathway separate from membrane concentrates.

**PFAS in membrane materials**

- Five commercial spiral‑wound polyamide RO membranes used for drinking water were found to contain PFOS (branched and linear), PFBA, PFHxA, PFNA, and PFOA [R13]. Leaching tests with Milli‑Q water showed tens of mg ΣPFAS per element could be released, giving modeled permeate concentrations between sub‑pg/L and hundreds of pg/L, depending on assumed leaching kinetics and produced water volume [R13].  
  → New or spent membranes are themselves PFAS‑containing residuals, and initial leaching can re‑contaminate permeate at low levels.

**Spent sorbents and single‑use media**

- In conceptual WRRF alternatives, spent GAC and PFAS‑selective AIX are routed to high‑temperature incineration (HTI) or GAC reactivation as the final destruction step [R10]. PFAS mass is thus transferred from water to a solid phase, with ultimate management via thermal destruction.

## 2. Comparison of downstream options: concentration, destruction, recycle, disposal, and hybrid trains

Here I compare options using compatible endpoints—primarily PFAS mass distribution, concentration factors, residual volumes, and where available, energy and cost. I keep clear that membrane steps are separation, not destruction.

### 2.1 Non‑destructive management and recirculation of concentrates

**Landfill leachate RO with evaporation ponds vs recirculation (Thailand)**

- With evaporation ponds, RO concentrate is directed to ponds; residual sediments are incinerated [R01]. At national scale (111 landfills, 8.2 million ton/yr MSW), net present cost (NPC) was 577.9 million USD with unit costs 1.72–2.71 USD/m³ [R01]. Investment costs (~578 million USD) were ~0.11% of 2019 GDP [R01].  
  → This option immobilizes PFAS in pond sediments and then incinerates them, but the study does not quantify PFAS destruction efficiency in incineration or PFAS mass in sediments.

- Without evaporation ponds, PFAS‑rich RO concentrate is recirculated back into landfills, giving a lower NPC of 391.9 million USD and unit costs 1.06–2.09 USD/m³; investment costs (~383 million USD, approximate) are ~0.07% of GDP [R01].  
  → This avoids a dedicated concentrate residual facility but effectively returns PFAS to the landfill leachate generation system. Long‑term mass accumulation or release risks are not quantified [R01].

**Full‑scale AO–MBR–RO WWTP brine discharge**

- In the municipal plant, RO brine is not separately treated; it is blended with an MBR bypass to form the final effluent (TE3) with ΣPFAS 186 ng/L and ~1 kg/year ΣPFAS discharge [R05].  
  → RO reduces PFAS in permeate but *shifts* the burden to a smaller, more contaminated brine that is then discharged—no destruction is provided.  

These cases demonstrate that **separation alone** can meet local effluent standards but leaves PFAS mass largely unmanaged or recirculated, which is not a complete solution.

### 2.2 Destructive treatment integrated with membrane concentrate management

#### 2.2.1 NF + UV‑sulfite for AFFF‑impacted groundwater

- Configuration: sediment and cation‑exchange pretreatment → NF270 (three 2540 elements) operated semi‑batch at 90% recovery (568 → 57 L) → NF reject (≈57 L) amended with 10 mM sulfite and pH 11.2 → recirculating UV reactor for 23 h → GAC polishing of both permeate and treated reject [R03]. One cycle per day for ~30 days [R03].

- Performance:
  - NF rejection for individual PFAS 92–98% over 30 cycles; longer chains rejected more strongly [R03].
  - PFAS in the NF reject were described as “concentrated,” reducing the volume requiring UV‑sulfite [R03].
  - UV‑sulfite achieved “PFAS destruction” in the reject, with rates for individual PFAS consistent with small‑scale studies [R03]. The evidence basis is parent‑compound loss; mineralization/defluorination is not resolved.

- Co‑contaminant effects:
  - Nitrate, silica, DOC, divalent cations accumulate in the reject and are known to inhibit UV‑based PFAS treatment and promote fouling [R03].  
  - pH adjustment to 11.2 and subsequent neutralization are identified as operational and cost challenges [R03].

- Residuals:
  - All treated streams pass through a GAC polisher, implying a secondary PFAS‑bearing solid waste stream [R03]. Composition of GAC residuals is not quantified.

**Trade‑off:** NF reduces treatment volume 10‑fold, making UV‑sulfite more plausible energetically, but co‑contaminant build‑up imposes process and energy penalties. Destruction endpoints are PFAS‑specific parent loss; carbon fate and inorganic fluoride balances are not reported.

#### 2.2.2 Foam fractionation + electrochemical oxidation (FF‑EO)

**FF‑EO on groundwater and landfill leachate**

- Pilot FF‑EO at a landfill treated both PFAS‑contaminated groundwater and landfill leachate: FF concentrated PFAS into foam, and EO was applied to collapsed foam fractions [R08]. Direct EO of bulk water was also evaluated.

- Performance:
  - Across FF‑EO, mean total PFAS “degradation” (parent‑compound loss) was 50%; long‑chain PFAS up to 86%, short‑chain up to 31% [R08].
  - Bioassays (transthyretin binding and bacterial bioluminescence) showed at least 13% reduction in toxic potency for groundwater after treatment, implying degradation of PFAS and other bioactive compounds exceeded byproduct formation under tested conditions [R08].

- Energy:
  - EO on PFAS‑enriched foam: ~270 kWh/m³ for 50 L batches and ~93 kWh/m³ for 150 L batches after 9 h [R08].  
  - Normalized energy per log removal of PFOA: 240 kWh/m³/log (50 L) and 160 kWh/m³/log (150 L) [R08].  
  → These intensities are high but applied to small volumes.

- Limitations:
  - FF preferentially removes surface‑active PFAS; short‑chain or non‑amphiphilic PFAS are less efficiently captured [R08].  
  - Extractable organofluorine (EOF) removal in FF was lower than target PFAS removal, implying non‑target PFAS remain in treated streams [R08].  
  - EO showed “low degradation” of perfluorosulfonic acids (PFSA) in fractionated foam; this challenges EO applicability to PFAS‑rich foam unless mass‑transfer and matrix effects are better managed [R08].  
  - Mass‑balance closure in FF was incomplete; some PFAS likely partition to aerosols/exhaust air [R08].

**Direct EO on bulk water**

- Applying EO directly to all contaminated water yielded higher PFAS degradation than FF‑EO but required treating ~10× more volume, driving up energy demand [R08].  
  → This highlights why pre‑concentration is critical when using high‑energy destructive methods.

**Positioning relative to membrane trains**

- Conceptually, FF‑EO can follow RO/NF by treating their concentrates, as in the McCleaf NF+FF scheme [R06]. The small volumes (e.g., 10–20% of feed) and higher PFAS concentrations are well‑matched to EO energetics (>100 kWh/m³ typical for such technologies [R11,R08]) provided matrix effects (salts, organics, surfactants) are controlled.

#### 2.2.3 AER regeneration + distillation + destruction

Although not membrane processes, these regenerate and concentrate PFAS separated by IX, and thus provide a relevant benchmark for membrane‑derived brines and foams.

- Regenerable and PFAS‑selective AERs loaded during eight months of AFFF‑groundwater treatment can be regenerated using methanolic brines [R11]:
  - Regenerable resins: near‑complete PFAS desorption with 10 BVs of 70% methanolic brine [R11].
  - PFAS‑selective resins: 30 BVs of 90% methanol or n‑propanol brine [R11].

- Distillation recovers >90% of methanol with negligible PFAS in the distillate, leaving a minimal still‑bottoms volume containing concentrated PFAS [R11].

- Conceptual analysis shows that PFAS from >100,000 BVs of groundwater can be concentrated into <10 BVs of still bottoms [R11]. Given destruction technologies with energy demands >100 kWh/m³ [R11], such volume reduction is essential for feasibility.

- LCA and cost modeling of these schemes (38,000 gal/d, ~50 μg/L PFAS source) indicate that:
  - Operating PFAS‑selective AERs in regenerable mode with cosolvent recovery substantially reduces the volume of residuals requiring hazardous waste disposal and cuts environmental impacts by >80% in some categories compared to less‑concentrated waste streams [R11].
  - Regenerable PFAS‑selective AERs have lower overall environmental impacts and lower treatment costs than single‑use PFAS‑selective AER or conventional regenerable AER within the scenarios modeled [R11].

These results are directly relevant conceptually to membrane‑derived concentrates: they show that a **two‑step concentration (IX→regeneration→distillation)** can deliver very small, destruction‑ready liquid volumes, improving life‑cycle performance.

#### 2.2.4 NF + GAC/IX sorption trains (wastewater)

- In tertiary wastewater:
  - NF270 at 90% recovery removes EfOM (DOC 4.2→0.9 mg/L) and most PFAS, but concentrate is a PFAS‑laden waste stream requiring destructive treatment [R02].
  - GAC RSSCTs:
    - Without NF pretreatment, MCL‑based breakthrough occurred after 81 BVs [R02].
    - With NF pretreatment (GAC treating NF permeate), >5000 BVs were treated before MCL exceedance [R02].
  - IX RSSCTs:
    - Without NF, MCL exceedance after 9000 BVs; with NF pretreatment, IX treating NF permeate never exceeded MCLs over 450,000 BVs [R02].

These data show that **NF pretreatment dramatically extends sorbent life**, shifting a larger PFAS mass onto GAC/IX for a given volume of treated water and thereby reducing the frequency and volume of spent media sent to HTI or reactivation. However, PFAS mass on the media and management routes (HTI, reactivation, landfill) were not experimentally quantified in this study.

#### 2.2.5 RO + GAC + IX + HTI / reactivation at WRRF scale

- A conceptual Alternative 2 for WRRF effluent (0.1–10 MGD) routes tertiary effluent through RO; the low‑flow RO concentrate is treated by lead–lag GAC and then lead–lag AIX; spent media go to HTI or GAC reactivation [R10].  
  - PFAS in blended effluent (RO permeate + polished concentrate effluent) are designed to be below ~5 ng/L reporting limits [R10].  
  - GAC changeout frequency was estimated at 4.5 times per year under the selected design [R10].

- Cost structure at 10 MGD: 22% of 20‑year costs to pretreatment, 33% to PFAS‑management capital (RO + sorbents + destruction infrastructure), and 45% to PFAS‑management O&M [R10]. At 0.1 MGD, pretreatment is 33%, PFAS management capital 16%, and PFAS management O&M 51% [R10].

- Compared to a dual‑media sorption alternative without RO (Alternative 1, sorption on GAC + AIX at full flow; not reconstructed in detail here), the RO‑based alternative has ~50% higher 20‑year total cost at 10 MGD [R10], driven by RO capital and energy plus similar media consumption.

**Implications:**

- RO pre‑concentration reduces sorbent vessel size (media sees only the concentrate flow) but, given similar mass of PFAS to capture, media usage rates remain substantial [R10].
- Adding RO shifts more cost into energy and membrane capital but reduces hydraulic footprint of sorption and potentially the volume of residual solids to HTI/reactivation.

### 2.3 Hybrid trains combining membrane concentration and FF

**NF + FF on municipal groundwater**

- NF at 80% recovery provides a 5× PFAS concentrate [R06]. Batch FF on this concentrate without co‑surfactant removes ~78% of total PFAS, with >94–95% removal of long‑chain PFAS [R06]. Montaline C40 increases total PFAS removal to ~94% and specifically boosts C4–C5 removals dramatically [R06].

- Continuous FF pilot runs with Montaline C40 were conducted, but quantitative removals are not in the provided excerpts [R06]. The authors highlight the need for polishing FF retentate (e.g., with tailored AIX) and for destruction of foamate (e.g., EO) [R06].

**FF on landfill leachate**

- Pilot continuous FF on raw leachate achieved ΣPFAS removal plateau ~60% due to poor removal of short‑chain PFCA that comprised 46% of influent PFAS [R09]. Differences between enriching vs stripping and batch vs continuous modes were <30% [R09].

**Comparison with membrane‑only concentration**

- FF offers an alternative or complement to NF/RO as a pre‑concentrator:
  - It can handle high ionic strength and complex matrices without fouling concerns [R09].
  - It is inherently selective for surface‑active PFAS and depends heavily on PFAS chain length and matrix surfactants [R07,R09,R06].  
  - Concentration factors can reach ~10× in multi‑stage FF, but co‑concentrated organics dominate TOC [R07].

- When used *after* NF (as in McCleaf [R06]), FF concentrates PFAS from an already volume‑reduced concentrate into an even smaller foamate, making high‑energy destruction more plausible, at the cost of managing PFAS‑rich surfactant solutions.

## 3. How residual management choices shape energy, cost, environmental burden, and the practical value of membrane separation

### 3.1 Energy use and scale dependence

**High‑energy destruction technologies**

- For liquid‑phase destruction technologies such as hydrothermal alkaline treatment, EO, plasma, and SCWO, typical energy requirements exceed 100 kWh/m³ [R11]. These are too high for bulk groundwater or effluent volumes but become reasonable for small, high‑strength concentrates—e.g., <10 BVs of still bottoms from >100,000 BVs of groundwater treated by AER [R11], or tens of liters of EO‑treated foam [R08].

- EO in the FF‑EO study consumed ~93–270 kWh/m³ of foamate after 9 h, with 160–240 kWh/m³ per log removal of PFOA [R08]. Direct EO on all water achieved higher degradation at the cost of a 10‑fold increase in treated volume and energy [R08].  

**Membrane energy vs downstream destruction**

- NF vs RO: NF270 was chosen over RO in wastewater due to lower pressure and energy and lower salt rejection, which reduces concentrate salinity [R02]. In the pilot CCMS, RO required higher pressures and energy than NF to achieve comparable recoveries [R12].

- RO pre‑concentration in WRRF Alternative 2: while explicit energy intensities are not given, the authors note that O&M costs are higher for the RO‑based alternative than for dual‑media sorption without RO, due partly to added energy consumption [R10].

**Implication for design:**

- The **practical value** of membranes is maximized when they reduce the mass‑normalized energy intensity of destruction—i.e., when their recovery and concentration factors are matched to a destruction step with high kWh/m³. Excessive recovery that harms permeate quality or produces highly inhibitory concentrates can waste energy without proportional PFAS mass benefit.

### 3.2 Cost and economic tradeoffs

**Landfill leachate RO (Thailand)**

- With evaporation ponds + sediment incineration: NPC 577.9 million USD; unit cost 1.72–2.71 USD/m³ [R01].
- Without ponds (recirculation): NPC 391.9 million USD; unit cost 1.06–2.09 USD/m³ [R01].  
  → Recirculation lowers apparent treatment cost by 32% in NPC terms but provides no destruction endpoint; PFAS remain in the system.

**WRRF PFAS management alternatives**

- Alternative 2 (RO + GAC + AIX + HTI/reactivation) at 10 MGD: 20‑year costs 200–420 million USD, with 22% pretreatment, 33% PFAS management capital, 45% O&M [R10]. At 0.1 MGD, pretreatment 33%, PFAS management capital 16%, PFAS O&M 51% [R10].  
  → Membranes and sorbent/destruction infrastructure dominate PFAS management costs; pretreatment is a substantial fraction, especially at small scale.

- For a sorption‑only alternative (Alternative 1), pretreatment costs at 0.1 MGD were ~46% of total 20‑year costs, with PFAS‑management O&M ~39% and capital ~15% [R10]. Overall, Alternative 2 (with RO) was ~50% more expensive than Alternative 1 at 10 MGD [R10].  
  → RO is economically justified only if its ability to concentrate PFAS substantially lowers life‑cycle costs in the destruction step or delivers additional co‑benefits (e.g., non‑PFAS contaminant control, water reuse quality) that sorption alone cannot.

**AER regeneration and LCA**

- LCA of regenerable PFAS‑selective AER systems showed that:
  - Concentrating PFAS wastes and recovering cosolvent via distillation reduces environmental impacts by >80% in some categories compared to less‑concentrated waste streams, largely by cutting hazardous‑waste disposal volumes [R11].
  - Regenerable PFAS‑selective AERs operated with cosolvent recovery had lower environmental impacts and lower costs than single‑use PFAS‑selective resins or conventional regenerable AERs in the modeled scenario (38,000 gal/d, ~50 μg/L PFAS) [R11].

**Interpretation relative to membranes**

- The key lesson is that **volume reduction and internal recycling of regenerant/solvent** can more than offset the environmental burdens of additional unit processes. Analogously, membrane systems that:
  - optimize recovery to minimize concentrate volume without excessive co‑contaminant build‑up; and
  - couple to destruction methods that exploit this concentration  
  are likely to have better life‑cycle performance than membrane‑only trains that discharge concentrates or send large volumes to disposal.

### 3.3 Environmental burdens and residual risks

**Incomplete destruction and non‑target PFAS**

- FF‑EO: Short‑chain PFAS degraded poorly (≤31% across the train) and PFSA degradation in foam was low [R08]. EOF data indicate the presence of non‑target PFAS that are not removed as efficiently as targeted analytes in FF [R08].  
  → Even with integrated separation and destruction, residues of short‑chain and non‑standard PFAS remain an issue.

- NF + GAC/IX: While the NF–GAC/IX train can produce effluents below stringent MCLs [R02], PFAS mass is shifted to solid sorbents. Destruction efficiencies for HTI or GAC reactivation are not quantified in these studies; potential formation of toxic byproducts from incineration of aminated polymers (e.g., IX) is explicitly identified as an uncertainty [R11].

**PFAS in biosolids and brine discharge**

- Full‑scale AO–MBR–RO: Even with high PFAS rejection in RO, the plant releases ~1 kg/year ΣPFAS via mixed effluent (dominated by brine) and ~1.7 kg/year via biosolids [R05].  
  → Membrane separation without downstream residual treatment leaves significant PFAS mass entering surface waters and soils.

**PFAS leaching from membranes**

- PFAS in RO membranes can leach at levels giving permeate concentrations two to four orders of magnitude below current Dutch drinking waters but still measurable [R13].  
  → This adds a small but non‑zero PFAS source that must be accounted for in mass balances and risk assessments, especially where other treatment steps aim for very low ng/L or pg/L targets.

**Air emissions from FF**

- Pilot FF on landfill leachate observed mass‑balance gaps and recommended future aerosol and exhaust‑air sampling [R09].  
  → Without managing off‑gas, FF may transfer some PFAS mass to air, creating an additional exposure pathway.

### 3.4 Practical value of membranes within integrated trains

Taken together, the evidence suggests membranes add most value when:

1. **They are embedded in trains that explicitly address their concentrates and PFAS‑bearing solids.**  
   - NF‑UV‑sulfite [R03], NF+FF+EO [R08,R06], NF+GAC/IX with eventual HTI/reactivation [R02,R10], or IX regeneration + distillation + high‑energy destruction [R11] are all examples where volume reduction is purposefully linked to destruction.

2. **Recovery and pretreatment are tuned to balance volume reduction vs. concentrate treatability.**  
   - Very high recovery (e.g., 97% RO) can create concentrates with extreme nitrate, DOC, and silica that inhibit photochemical treatment and promote fouling [R03].  
   - NF pretreatment for sorbents improves adsorbent service life and shifts more PFAS mass into solids, but also leaves an NF concentrate requiring destructive treatment [R02].

3. **Solid residuals—including spent membranes—are explicitly managed.**  
   - Pilot evidence shows meaningful PFAS mass on pretreatment filters, membranes, IX resins [R12], biosolids [R05], and the RO membranes themselves [R13].  
   - Conceptual WRRF alternatives route these solids to HTI/reactivation [R10], but empirical data on destruction efficiency and byproduct formation are sparse.

4. **Life‑cycle impacts and costs are evaluated at system scale, not per‑unit process.**  
   - National scale RO for landfill leachate [R01] and WRRF alternatives [R10] indicate that adding complex residual treatment (evaporation ponds, RO, HTI) significantly increases capital and O&M costs but can reduce PFAS discharges if destruction is effective.  
   - AER LCA results [R11] emphasize that concentrating PFAS before destruction greatly reduces environmental impacts and cost associated with residual disposal—an insight that should be translated to membrane concentrate management.

## 4. Critical gaps and research needs

Across the studies, several consistent gaps limit robust design of treatment trains for membrane‑derived PFAS concentrates and materials:

1. **Incomplete PFAS mass balances and non‑target fluorine:**
   - FF studies show variable mass‑balance closure and lower EOF removal than target PFAS removal [R08,R09].  
   - Membrane and destruction studies rarely report total organofluorine, inorganic fluoride, or byproducts.

2. **Sparse characterization of concentrate composition vs. destruction performance:**
   - UV‑sulfite and EO studies identify inhibition by nitrate, DOC, and salts [R03,R08], but do not systematically map destruction kinetics across realistic concentrate chemistries from different membranes, recoveries, and pretreatments.

3. **PFAS on solid components and their end‑of‑life:**
   - Masters et al. show substantial PFOS‑dominated sorption to filters, membranes, and IX softening resin [R12]; Ika et al. show strong PFAS enrichment in biosolids [R05]; Sadia et al. show PFAS in RO membranes [R13].  
   - There is very little experimental work on destruction or long‑term leaching from these solids under real disposal conditions.

4. **Quantitative energy and cost for integrated trains:**
   - EO and NF‑UV‑sulfite provide partial energy data [R03,R08]; WRRF and landfill leachate models provide cost and coarse energy implications [R01,R10]; but fully coupled techno‑economic and LCA evaluations of membrane+FF+destruction trains are lacking.

5. **Short‑chain and emerging PFAS:**
   - Short‑chain PFAAs consistently show poorer removal in FF [R07,R09,R06] and lower degradation in FF‑EO [R08].  
   - Emerging ether carboxylates like PF4OPeA behave differently (high in RO brine and sludge) [R05], and their treatment performance in NF/RO concentrates and destruction steps is not well characterized.

6. **Membrane‑derived PFAS leaching under realistic operation:**
   - Sadia et al. provide lab‑scale leaching scenarios [R13], but the kinetics and real‑plant permeate concentrations under typical flush/conditioning protocols remain uncertain.

Targeted research and pilot demonstrations that explicitly close mass balances (water, solids, air), track non‑target PFAS and organofluorine, and couple separation with destruction and life‑cycle assessment are needed to design membrane‑based PFAS treatment trains that are truly protective, not just redistributive.
