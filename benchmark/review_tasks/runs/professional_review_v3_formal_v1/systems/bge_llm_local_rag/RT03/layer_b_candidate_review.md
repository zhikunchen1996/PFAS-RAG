### 1. Framing: Regeneration vs Replacement vs Destructive End‑of‑Life

Across the studies in the evidence table, adsorption/ion‑exchange is consistently a *separation* step: PFAS are moved from large water volumes onto media or into regenerant solutions. Whether regeneration is preferable to single‑use replacement depends on:

- How completely PFAS can be *desorbed* versus *destroyed*.
- How much adsorption capacity is recovered and for how many cycles.
- How the media itself degrades under regeneration.
- What secondary liquid/solid/gaseous wastes are generated and how they are ultimately destroyed.
- Site‑specific costs, energy/chemical use, and constraints on waste handling.

Below, I compare regeneration approaches, residual streams, and the additional evidence needed to choose among regeneration, replacement, and destructive end‑of‑life for PFAS‑loaded media.

---

## 2. Comparison of Regeneration Approaches

### 2.1. Simple inorganic brines and bases (no organic cosolvent)

**Ion exchange resins in drinking water matrices**

Liu & Sun regenerated PFAS‑loaded PS‑DVB resins using chloride brines across 0.01–10% (w/v) without organic solvent, defining “regeneration recovery” as the mass fraction of preloaded PFAS released to the brine [R01]. At 10% brine:

- Median PFAS recoveries across multiple PFAS were low for most resins:
  - A592E: 8%
  - PFA694E: 4%
  - DOWEX1: 5%
  - TAN1: 16%
  - IRA910: 22% [R01].
- Even under the “optimal” 10% brine, PFAS‑specific, gel‑type resins (A592E, PFA694E) showed <50% recovery for almost all PFAS; IRA910 (macroporous) was the most regenerable [R01].

These are *separation* endpoints: PFAS are transferred to a chloride brine; no PFAS degradation or defluorination is reported.

**PFAS‑loaded resins in drinking water treatment (pilot & batch)**

Riegel et al. observed that:

- Standard inorganic regenerants (NaOH, HCl, NaCl, Na₂SO₄, Na₂CO₃) provided only minor or no regeneration for PFHxA, PFHpA, and PFOA on both weak‑ and strong‑base AERs, though PFBA and PFPeA were more readily extracted from WBA MP 62 [R02].
- Alternative mixed systems (NH₄OH/NH₄Cl, KCl/KOH) also did not achieve sufficient regeneration [R02].

Again, this means only a small fraction of sorbed PFAS is desorbed; any claimed “regeneration” for these systems is partial at best.

**Activated carbon with inorganic solutions**

Karanja‑based activated carbons regenerated with 5% (w/v) NaCl, NaOH, NH₄Cl, KCl or NH₄OH desorbed only ~32% of PFOA from unmodified AC and ~25% from CTAB‑modified AC in the best cases (NH₄Cl or NH₄OH, mapping inexact) [R03]. These are single‑step desorption tests with no multi‑cycle capacity data [R03].

**Implications**

- **PFAS‑selective and gel‑type AERs are effectively non‑regenerable** with brines alone under practical concentrations, particularly for longer‑chain PFAS [R01].
- Macroporous, non‑selective resins (e.g., IRA910) are more amenable to brine regeneration but still show modest recoveries without solvent [R01].
- Brine‑only regeneration generates **PFAS‑laden brines** requiring downstream management, with low desorption fractions for many PFAS. It should not be treated as PFAS destruction.

For a site relying on simple brines, the default management path remains **spent‑media replacement plus destructive treatment** of resins/GAC, not true closed‑loop regeneration.

### 2.2. Brine + organic solvent regeneration

Several studies combine salt with an organic cosolvent to enhance PFAS desorption.

#### 2.2.1. Methanol or other alcohols with brine

**IX resins with 60–70% methanol brines**

- Liu et al. regenerated PFAS‑loaded IRA910 and PFA694E with 60% methanol + 5% NaCl over three cycles:
  - IRA910: >90% PFAS release to regenerant in all water matrices [R04].
  - PFA694E: mean PFAS release 45.6% (median 42.7%) [R04].
  - Both resins maintained ≥90% PFAS *removal* from water over three cycles, within <8% of pristine performance, despite declining recoveries in the third cycle [R04].
- Ellis et al. regenerated both conventional and PFAS‑selective AERs in continuous flow using methanolic brines:
  - Conventional “regenerable” resins: near‑complete PFAS desorption with 10 bed volumes (BVs) of 70% methanol + 1 wt% NaCl [R05].
  - PFAS‑selective resins: required ≈30 BVs of more aggressive regenerants (90% methanol brine or n‑propanol brine) for near‑complete PFAS desorption [R05].
  - Increasing regenerant EBCT had minimal effect on desorption [R05].

These systems achieve high *desorption* for many PFAS, especially on generic/macroporous resins, but PFAS remain in a mixed methanol–brine stream.

**Carbonaceous adsorbents with 50% methanol**

Chaudhary et al. used 50% methanol to regenerate AC and wood‑biochar cocontaminated with PFOA, PFOS, GenX, and Cd(II):

- Methanol removed >90% of PFAS from both adsorbents [R06].
- Methanol did **not** remove Cd(II); that remained on the solids [R06].
- After methanol regeneration, both adsorbents retained >90% adsorption performance for PFAS and Cd(II) across three cycles [R06].

**Ethanol‑containing regenerants in field IWEX columns**

Riegel et al. optimized ethanol–acidic regenerants:

- For strong‑base A532E, 1 M NaCl in 45% ethanol/water achieved “complete extraction” (~100%) of adsorbed PFCA in batch [R02].
- For weak‑base D5706, 0.5 M HCl in 45% ethanol gave the highest batch regeneration rates [R02].
- In a waterworks column using D5706 treating PFAS‑polluted groundwater, regeneration in filter mode with 0.5 M HCl in 45% ethanol:
  - 2nd–3rd regenerations: ~60–80% of each PFCA desorbed [R02].
  - 4th regeneration with 6 BV regenerant: “almost complete regeneration” (~100%) for PFCA C4–C8, and PFPeA, PFHxA, PFHpA, and PFOA concentration factors of 3500–6000 in the combined 6 BV regenerant relative to raw groundwater [R02].

Again, these are high **desorption** and **enrichment** factors, but no PFAS destruction.

**Electrospun nanofiber membrane regeneration**

Pervez et al. regenerated a PVA‑CTAC electrospun nanofiber adsorbent with 1% methanolic NH₄OH:

- Three successive 30‑min extractions (per cycle) with 1% methanolic NH₄OH, followed by DI wash, CTAC reconditioning, and drying [R07].
- Across three cycles, PFBS, PFHxA, PFHpA, PFOA, 6:2 FTSA, GenX, PFNA, and PFDA had recovery “consistently at or above 95%” [R07].
- PFAS removal efficiencies remained high; only PFHxS showed decreased removal in later cycles, and PFOS showed improved recovery after the first regeneration [R07].

This is one of the strongest demonstrations of repeated high desorption and maintained capacity for a sorbent, but only over three cycles and in controlled lab conditions.

#### 2.2.2. Sequential methanol–acid regeneration (pollutant separation)

Chaudhary et al. evaluated:

- **Methanol‑only**: >90% PFAS desorption, no Cd(II) desorption; >90% PFAS and Cd(II) *removal performance* retained over three reuse cycles [R06].
- **Acid‑only (0.1 M HCl)**: effectively removed Cd(II), but PFAS desorption was limited; adsorption performance over cycles was still >90% for both PFAS and Cd(II) [R06].
- **Sequential methanol then acid**:
  - PFAS desorbed in methanol step, Cd(II) desorbed in subsequent acid step [R06].
  - Total (PFAS + Cd) recovery >90% [R06].
  - Adsorbents maintained >90% adsorption performance for both PFAS and Cd(II) over three cycles [R06].
  - Two separate concentrated liquid streams: methanol‑rich PFAS stream and acidic Cd(II) stream [R06].

This design intentionally creates distinct PFAS and metal residuals, simplifying downstream PFAS destruction and metal recovery, but still relies on subsequent destructive technologies.

#### 2.2.3. Distillation of methanolic regenerants

When methanol is used, distillation is often introduced:

- Liu et al. distilled 60% methanol/5% NaCl regenerants:
  - Methanol reduced to ~5% in still bottoms, increased to ~75.6% in condensate [R04].
  - PFAS concentrated ~10× in still bottoms using IRA910; condensate contained <50 ng/L PFAS and could be reused as regenerant [R04].
- Ellis et al. distilled methanolic brines:
  - >90% methanol recovery with negligible PFAS in the distillate, leaving a small‑volume PFAS‑concentrated still bottom for later destruction [R05].

In both cases, distillation is a **phase‑separation** step: PFAS remain in still bottoms; the cosolvent is mostly recovered.

#### 2.2.4. Summary and trade‑offs

- **Desorption efficacy** is generally high for macroporous or non‑selective IX (IRA910, TAN1, conventional regenerable AERs) and for carbonaceous adsorbents when ≥50–70% alcohol is used.
- PFAS‑selective AERs are harder to regenerate even with high solvent fractions: PFA694E shows limited recovery (~45% mean), and Ellis et al. required 30 BVs of 90% methanol or n‑propanol brines for near‑complete desorption [R04, R05].  
- These approaches unequivocally *transfer* PFAS into concentrated liquid streams; **none of these methods provide PFAS destruction**.

From a practical perspective:

- High cosolvent fractions favor **regeneration** (desorption and capacity recovery), but force the system to manage:
  - Large energies for solvent recovery by distillation (quantified in some studies only by assumptions, not measured).
  - Methanol‑rich still bottoms requiring destructive treatment (e.g., UV/sulfite, GAC adsorption + incineration, SCWO, or HTI).

### 2.3. Oxidative/destructive regeneration

#### 2.3.1. Direct SR‑AOP on IX resins (Gao et al.)

Gao et al. investigated sulfate radical‑based AOP (heat‑activated peroxodisulfate, PDS) applied directly to PFOA‑loaded resins [R08]:

- Feasibility tests at 10 mM PDS (Route 1) measured:
  - PFOA degradation and fluoride release/defluorination fraction as indicators of PFOA destruction on the resin [R08].
- In cyclic experiments at 30 mM PDS over six adsorption–regeneration cycles:
  - Cumulative PFOA removal from water increased linearly with cycle number [R08].
  - Authors state that a “low PDS dose” (30 mM) effectively recovered adsorption capability with only **slight resin destruction**, as verified by SEM/FTIR after six cycles (some surface cracks but largely intact) [R08].
- At 300 mM PDS:
  - Severe resin surface damage with many large cracks, and irreversible loss of adsorption capacity despite PFOA degradation [R08].

Key points:

- This is one of the few truly **destructive regeneration** approaches, in which PFAS on the resin are chemically degraded and defluorination is monitored.
- The regenerant stream, according to the authors, becomes a small, “non‑hazardous” solution primarily containing potassium and sulfate ions plus inorganic fluoride byproducts [R08].
- However, even at 30 mM PDS, there is measurable resin damage over six cycles; at 300 mM PDS, the resin is not sustainably regenerable.

#### 2.3.2. UV/sulfite destruction of PFAS in regenerant still bottoms (Liu et al.)

Liu et al. treated distillation still bottoms (PFAS‑concentrated, reduced methanol) with UV/sulfite:

- Treatment train: IX → methanol/NaCl regeneration → distillation (PFAS to still bottoms) → UV/sulfite on still bottoms [R04].
- With prior distillation, UV/sulfite in still bottoms achieved “almost 100% defluorination” of PFAS overall, except a few short‑chain sulfonates and ethers; PFBS was notably recalcitrant [R04].
- Without distillation (methanol‑rich regenerant), UV/sulfite achieved only 2.8% PFAS defluorination, illustrating strong inhibition by high methanol [R04].
- Heterogeneous UV/sulfite directly on PFOS‑laden resins produced ≤20% defluorination, and was considered ineffective [R04].

Here destruction takes place **in the liquid still bottoms after prior concentration**; the resin regeneration step itself is still purely separative.

Energy use is quantified via EE/O:

- 18.8–35.1 kWh/m³ of still bottoms, or 0.9–1.8 kWh/m³ normalized to original water volume treated [R04], excluding PFBS.

#### 2.3.3. SCWO of PFAS‑laden media (Chiang et al.)

SCWO treats **spent media** rather than regenerating for reuse:

- Spent GAC and AIX from full‑scale groundwater systems, plus AIX from a mobile treatment unit, were slurried and treated in a 1 wet‑ton/day tubular SCWO reactor [R09].
- Authors state SCWO “is a very effective PFAS destruction technology for spent GAC and AIX,” and that spent media were “completely mineralized to water, CO₂ and a negligible amount of residual minerals” [R09].
- However:
  - Inorganic fluoride increases expected from complete PFAS mineralization (~0.06 mg/L) could not be resolved above background, so **defluorination and PFAS mineralization were not confirmed by mass balance** [R09].
  - The system was not optimized specifically for PFAS; further work is needed on PFAS and fluorine mass balances and on energy, air quality and life‑cycle comparisons to incineration [R09].

SCWO functions purely as an **end‑of‑life destruction** option, not as regeneration.

#### 2.3.4. Summary

- **SR‑AOP on resins and UV/sulfite on still bottoms provide true PFAS destruction**, with direct measurements of parent loss and, in the SR‑AOP case, fluoride release [R08, R04].
- The **regeneration–distillation–destruction** trains still generate liquid or solid streams (e.g., still bottoms, sludge) and rely on additional energy‑intensive steps.
- SCWO is promising for full destruction of PFAS‑laden media, but defluorination was not demonstrated quantitatively and its energy and cost performance vs incineration are not yet well characterized [R09].

### 2.4. Thermal regeneration and incineration as end‑of‑life

**GAC thermal reactivation**

- Ellis et al. modeled GAC systems with off‑site thermal reactivation (treated as hazardous) and showed:
  - Thermal reactivation substantially reduces environmental impacts compared with single‑use GAC, though GAC with reactivation remains more impactful and more costly (USD 0.44/m³) than single‑use PFAS‑selective AER (USD 0.28/m³) [R10].
- Ling et al. assumed:
  - Facilities ≥1 MGD send spent GAC to site‑dedicated reactivation, which both restores capacity and thermally decomposes sorbed PFAS; smaller facilities send GAC to high‑temperature incineration (HTI) [R11].
  - These destruction steps are needed to prevent re‑release of PFAS [R11].

No destruction efficiencies are measured; fate of PFAS during incineration/regeneration is handled via generic life‑cycle inventory data, not PFAS‑specific emissions [R12, R10, R11].

## 3. Recovered Capacity, Multi‑Cycle Performance, and Media Deterioration

### 3.1. Ion‑exchange resins

- **Brine‑only regeneration** (Liu & Sun) yields low PFAS recoveries (≤22% median, up to ~89–94% for select PFAS/resin combinations) with **no multi‑cycle capacity data** [R01]. Regeneration is incomplete; PFAS accumulation in deeper pores is inferred in other work.
- **Methanol + brine regeneration** (Liu 2025):
  - IRA910: >90% PFAS release; nearly 100% recoveries in cycle 2, but recoveries decline in cycle 3; PFAS removal from water remains ≥90% over three cycles [R04].
  - PFA694E: ~45% mean PFAS release despite 60% methanol; PFAS removal also ≥90% over three cycles [R04].
  - Authors attribute declining recoveries in cycle 3 to PFAS migration into deeper pores, implying eventual resin replacement even if per‑cycle removal remains high [R04].
- **PFAS‑selective resins in brine‑only** systems (Liu & Sun) appear effectively non‑regenerable, aligning with vendor recommendations of one‑time use [R01].

### 3.2. Carbonaceous adsorbents

- **AC and BC‑W with 50% methanol and 0.1 M HCl**:
  - >90% adsorption performance for PFAS and Cd(II) maintained over three cycles for all regeneration strategies (methanol only, acid only, sequential) [R06].
  - No physical damage is reported over three cycles; structural characterization beyond that timescale is not provided.
- **PVA‑CTAC ENM**:
  - Maintained high PFAS removal (R%) and regeneration recoveries ≥95% for most PFAS across three cycles [R07].
  - Pore blockage indicated for PFHxS, but no macroscopic deterioration reported [R07].

### 3.3. SR‑AOP on IX resins

- **Low PDS dose (30 mM)**:
  - Six cycles with slight surface cracking but largely intact morphology and effective capacity recovery [R08].
- **High PDS dose (300 mM)**:
  - Severe material damage and irreversible loss of adsorption capacity despite PFOA degradation [R08].

This highlights a core trade‑off: higher oxidant doses accelerate destruction but rapidly destroy resin integrity.

## 4. Residual Streams: Transfer vs Destruction

### 4.1. Liquid residuals

Across studies, desorption‑based regeneration invariably produces liquid residuals:

- **Brines (0.5–10% NaCl or NH₄Cl)**: carry low to moderate fractions of sorbed PFAS, particularly for gel‑type and PFAS‑selective resins [R01].
- **Alcohol–brine mixtures**:
  - 50–70% methanol or 45% ethanol mixtures carry 80–90% (or more) of the initial PFAS loading in highly concentrated form [R03, R04, R02, R06].
  - Distillation concentrates PFAS further into small‑volume still bottoms, while generating a methanol‑rich condensate with negligible PFAS that can be reused [R04, R05].
- **Acid regenerants** contain desorbed metals (Cd(II) in Chaudhary et al.) and low PFAS, enabling separate metal recovery streams [R06].

These streams are *PFAS‑rich liquids* requiring either:

- Further concentration and destruction (e.g., UV/sulfite on still bottoms [R04], GAC adsorption + incineration [R12]).
- Direct incineration/destruction (modeled, not measured) [R12, R10].

### 4.2. Solid residuals

- **Spent single‑use AER and GAC**:
  - Accumulate highly concentrated PFAS, especially in lead column sections, often exceeding isotherm‑based capacities [R13].
  - Are modeled or assumed to be destroyed by HTI or thermal reactivation [R10, R11], though PFAS destruction pathways and byproducts are not experimentally resolved in these LCA/modeling studies [R12, R10].
- **Resins after SR‑AOP**:
  - Retain cracks and chemical modifications; inorganic fluoride appears in the regenerant, but resin fragments or transformation products are not characterized [R08].

### 4.3. Gaseous residuals and air emissions

- SCWO is described qualitatively as having “cleaner air emissions and lower energy requirement than incineration,” with energy recovery via heat exchangers [R09], but PFAS‑specific off‑gas composition was not measured.
- Incineration, HTI, and GAC reactivation are treated via generic life‑cycle inventory data with no PFAS‑specific emissions or byproducts [R12, R10, R11].

In all such cases, destruction is **assumed**, not quantitatively verified.

## 5. Evidence Needs for Site‑Specific Decisions

### 5.1. Operational and performance evidence

**Breakthrough and media usage**

Pilot and LCA studies show that media usage rates (MURs) and the relative advantage of regeneration vs replacement are dominated by:

- PFAS mixture and concentration (e.g., high source‑zone groundwater >10 μg/L total PFAS [R13], vs WRRF effluent with 15 ng/L PFBA/PFBS [R11]).
- Breakthrough/changeout criteria:
  - Detection of “any PFAS” versus UCMR3‑listed compounds or 50% breakthrough strongly alters bed volumes to replacement/regeneration [R13, R10].
  - PFBA is detectable within 21,000 BVs for all resins, driving very frequent changeout under stringent criteria [R13].
- Media type:
  - Single‑use PFAS‑selective AERs treat many more BVs before 50% breakthrough for long‑chain PFAS than regenerable resins [R13].
  - GAC tends to have higher MUR than AER for the same criteria [R10].

**Regeneration effectiveness in real waters**

Most regeneration experiments are in **laboratory matrices** (UPW, synthetic brines):

- Matrix effects (NOM, competing anions, co‑contaminants like Cd) alter regeneration behavior and may reduce recoveries [R04, R06, R07].
- There is limited direct evidence for long‑term regeneration in **full‑scale** systems beyond Riegel’s D5706 field column (4 regenerations in a waterworks) [R02].

### 5.2. Energy and chemical use

Empirically quantified energy data are limited:

- UV/sulfite: EE/O 18.8–35.1 kWh/m³ of still bottoms, or 0.9–1.8 kWh/m³ normalized to water treated [R04].
- SCWO: autothermal operation requires minimum calorific content ~2.4 MJ/L (COD 120–180 g/L), with energy partially recovered; specific PFAS‑matrix energy figures are not provided [R09].
- Distillation of methanolic regenerants: methanol recovery >90% is demonstrated [R04, R05], but energy demand for distillation is treated only in LCA models (Emery, Boyer, Ellis) without experimental measurement [R12, R10, R05].

Chemical consumption is much better characterized:

- Regenerant compositions (brine %; methanol or ethanol fractions; acid/base strengths) and bed volumes per regeneration are explicitly given in several studies [R04, R02, R05, R10].
- The more PFAS‑selective the resin, the **higher** the required cosolvent fraction and regenerant volume (e.g., 30 BVs of 90% methanol for PFAS‑selective AERs [R05]).

### 5.3. Cost and sustainability evidence

**Process‑level techno‑economic indicators**

- **Drinking water ion exchange vs GAC for short‑chain PFCA**:
  - Single‑use D5706 WBA has a specific consumable material cost of ~€0.20/m³ versus €0.11/m³ for GAC‑CC [R02].
  - With regeneration, the number of adsorption–regeneration cycles needed for D5706 to be cheaper (per PFAS) than GAC‑CC varies:
    - PFHxA, PFHpA: 2 cycles.
    - PFPeA: ≥5 cycles [R02].
- **System‑level PFAS groundwater remediation** (Ellis 2023):
  - Modeled life‑cycle treatment costs (USD/m³) under a common baseline scenario:
    - Single‑use PFAS‑selective AER: 0.28
    - Regenerable AER: 0.40
    - GAC with thermal regeneration: 0.44
    - Single‑use GAC: 0.50 [R10].
  - Environmental impacts:
    - Single‑use PFAS‑selective AER had lower overall impacts than regenerable AER or GAC under baseline assumptions [R10].
    - For regenerable AER, impacts are dominated by management of PFAS‑contaminated brine/cosolvent waste and by distillation efficiency and cosolvent fraction [R10].

**WRRF‑scale wastewater treatment**

Ling et al. modeled tertiary GAC/AIX treatment for PFBA–PFBS:

- O&M cost multipliers for short chain vs long chain PFAS:
  - PFBA treatment cost is 1.2–1.7× that of PFOA; PFBS 1.4–2.9× PFOS [R11].
- Upgrading secondary WRRFs to MBRs for PFAS treatment accounts for ~40–80% of total retrofit costs [R11].
- Combined PFAS separation plus destruction steps are likely to render rates “unaffordable” by EPA affordability criteria without external funding [R11].

**Regeneration LCA** (Boyer 2021; Ellis 2025)

- Boyer et al. showed:
  - Baseline NaCl/methanol regeneration with incineration of regenerant has the highest environmental impacts; incineration dominates [R12].
  - Recycling methanol (and optionally brine) via distillation plus GAC adsorption reduces impacts by lowering waste mass sent to incineration [R12].
  - Alternative salts and inorganic bases at 0.5% (m/m) generally increase environmental impacts vs NaCl, especially when chemically produced [R12].
- Ellis et al. (2025) showed that higher cosolvent fractions for PFAS‑selective AERs, paradoxically, can reduce overall impacts by greatly reducing still‑bottom volumes that must be incinerated [R05].

**Sustainability assessment caveats**

Crucially, none of these LCAs quantify:

- PFAS destruction efficiency or toxic byproduct formation during incineration, reactivation, or SCWO [R12, R10, R09].
- Long‑term media deterioration under repeated regenerations at full scale.

A regeneration option cannot be deemed “sustainable” on the basis of one or a few cycles alone; durability, energy, chemicals, and secondary wastes all significantly affect sustainability.

## 6. Implications for Choosing Regeneration vs Replacement vs End‑of‑Life Destruction

### 6.1. When is regeneration attractive?

Based on the evidence:

- **Macroporous, non‑selective IX resins (e.g., IRA910)**:
  - Exhibit high PFAS release (>90%) and stable removal over at least three cycles with methanolic brines [R04].
  - Brine‑only regeneration remains modest (22% median recovery [R01]).
  - For such resins, multiple regeneration cycles before replacement appear technically feasible, especially when followed by distillation + UV/sulfite or other destruction of still bottoms.
- **Weak‑base AERs with ethanol/acid regenerants (e.g., D5706)**:
  - Demonstrated almost complete regeneration of PFCA at the 4th cycle in a field column using 0.5 M HCl in 45% ethanol [R02].
  - This is a rare example of repeated in‑situ regeneration at pilot/field scale with strong PFAS enrichment factors (3500–6000×).
- **Carbonaceous adsorbents with sequential methanol–acid regeneration**:
  - AC and BC‑W maintained >90% adsorption performance for at least three cycles while enabling >90% separation of PFAS and Cd(II) into different streams [R06].
  - Particularly attractive where mixed PFAS–metal contamination is a concern.

For such systems, regeneration can reduce consumable media costs and solid waste volumes; however, any judgment of “sustainability” must include the fate of PFAS in regenerant liquids, energy for distillation, and the impacts of destruction processes.

### 6.2. When is single‑use + destruction preferable?

Several lines of evidence indicate that **some media are better operated in single‑use mode with destructive end‑of‑life**:

- PFAS‑specific gel‑type PS‑DVB resins (A592E, PFA694E) have low regenerability with brines; even with 60% methanol, PFA694E shows limited mean PFAS release (~45.6%) [R01, R04].
- Manufacturers recommend one‑time use for PFAS‑specific resins, consistent with Liu & Sun’s regeneration results [R01].
- System‑level LCA/LCC (Ellis 2023) shows that **single‑use PFAS‑selective AER has lower cost and environmental impact** than regenerable AER and GAC under realistic, non‑extreme breakthrough criteria [R10].
- Pilot‑scale breakthrough tests show single‑use resins achieve much higher bed volumes to 50% breakthrough for key PFAS than regenerable resins, especially for long‑chain PFSAs and PFCAs [R13].

For such media, the focus should shift to:

- Optimizing **incineration, thermal reactivation, or SCWO** as the end‑of‑life destruction step.
- Ensuring real‑world destruction efficiencies and byproducts are characterized, rather than assuming generic incineration performance.

### 6.3. Role of destructive regeneration

SR‑AOP on IX resins and UV/sulfite on still bottoms demonstrate proof‑of‑concept for combined separation and destruction:

- SR‑AOP regeneration can both **degrade PFOA** and restore resin capacity at modest oxidant doses, but resin damage sets a hard limit on feasible cycle numbers [R08].
- UV/sulfite after distillation can achieve near‑complete defluorination of most PFAS in still bottoms at moderate EE/O, with short‑chain sulfonates remaining challenging [R04].

For high‑priority sites where long‑term liability of PFAS wastes is unacceptable, such combined regeneration–destruction trains may be warranted despite higher energy and chemical demands.

## 7. Key Evidence Gaps and Future Research Needs

To support robust, site‑specific decisions among regeneration, replacement, and end‑of‑life destruction, the following evidence is needed:

1. **Full PFAS and fluorine mass balances**  
   - For SCWO, thermal reactivation, and incineration of PFAS‑laden media and regenerant concentrates; current studies lack quantitative defluorination and byproduct characterization [R09, R12, R10, R11].

2. **Long‑term multi‑cycle regeneration durability**  
   - Beyond 3–6 cycles for IX and carbonaceous adsorbents; current work rarely extends past six cycles (Gao SR‑AOP) or four regenerations (D5706) [R08, R02], and no studies track >10 cycles under realistic water chemistries.

3. **Energy and resource use quantification**  
   - Experimental rather than modeled energy demands for methanol/ethanol distillation, UV/sulfite destruction, SR‑AOP, and SCWO on PFAS‑laden streams, including sensitivity to PFAS loading and co‑contaminants [R04, R09, R12, R10].

4. **Field‑scale regeneration data**  
   - Pilot/field demonstration of solvent‑based and destructive regeneration schemes under complex water matrices (NOM, competing ions) and variable loading, beyond the few existing examples (D5706 in groundwater [R02]).

5. **Integrated cost–impact–performance assessments**  
   - Harmonized frameworks that jointly consider:
     - Media usage and regeneration frequency as a function of PFAS mixture and criteria [R10, R13, R11].
     - Operational energy, chemicals, and destruction options.
     - Long‑term risk reduction from actual PFAS destruction rather than transfer.

6. **Guidance on matching media and regeneration to PFAS mixtures**  
   - Systematic comparisons that couple resin/adsorbent type, PFAS speciation (chain length, headgroup, ether structures), and preferred regeneration/destruction pathways (e.g., macroporous resins with methanol brines + UV/sulfite vs PFAS‑selective resins with single‑use incineration).

## 8. Practical Take‑Home Points for Decision‑Makers

- **Desorption is not destruction.** All brine and alcohol‑based regenerations in this evidence set simply move PFAS to smaller liquid volumes, which then require robust destructive treatment.
- **Media choice drives regeneration feasibility.**
  - Macroporous, non‑selective IX and carbonaceous adsorbents can often be regenerated effectively with alcohol‑brine or sequential methanol–acid, at least over a few cycles.
  - PFAS‑selective and gel‑type resins are often practically non‑regenerable with simple brines and remain challenging even with high solvent fractions.
- **Regeneration trains must be evaluated as systems.**  
  IX + regeneration + distillation + destruction (UV/sulfite or SR‑AOP) can substantially reduce solid waste but introduce new energy and chemical demands and generate concentrated liquid/slurry wastes.
- **Single‑use PFAS‑selective AERs can be economically and environmentally competitive** under many groundwater scenarios, as long as end‑of‑life destruction is effective and verifiable [R10, R13].
- **End‑of‑life destructive options (SCWO, HTI, thermal reactivation)** are essential backstops for PFAS‑loaded media, especially where regeneration is incomplete or not feasible; their PFAS destruction performance must be transparently characterized to support long‑term risk management [R09, R12, R10, R11].

In sum, choosing among regeneration, replacement, and end‑of‑life destruction for PFAS‑loaded media is inherently **site‑ and media‑specific**. It requires data not only on per‑cycle PFAS desorption and recovered capacity, but also on media durability, energy and chemical use, and the fate of PFAS in all residual streams.
