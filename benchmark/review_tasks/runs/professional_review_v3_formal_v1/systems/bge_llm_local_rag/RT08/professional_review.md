## 1. Scope and framing

This review synthesizes evidence on three destructive technologies for PFAS‑rich liquids:

- Electrochemical oxidation (EO)
- Non‑thermal plasma, represented by atmospheric RAdial Plasma (RAP)
- Sonolysis / ultrasonication

and focuses on how to match them to specific liquid streams and PFAS profiles, based strictly on the provided studies.

The technologies are at different stages:

- **Sonolysis**: extensive bench‑scale data across simple solutions, groundwaters, AFFF, foam fractionate, and concentrated investigation‑derived waste (IDW), plus one groundwater field demonstration [S01; S04; S06].
- **EO**: pilot‑scale in a foam‑fractionation–EO (FF–EO) train on groundwater and landfill leachate [S03], plus process‑scale sustainability modeling [S05].
- **RAP plasma**: bench‑scale on relatively simple PFOA/PFOS aqueous solutions [S02].

Throughout, “destruction” in the original papers is most often measured as **parent‑compound loss**; only some sonolysis work evaluates **defluorination** explicitly. Where only parent loss or total/extractable organofluorine changes are reported, I do not equate this with complete mineralization or full defluorination.

## 2. Reaction environments, feed requirements, and PFAS scope

### 2.1 Sonolysis / Ultrasonication

**Reaction environment**

- High‑frequency ultrasound (typically 700 kHz) generating acoustic cavitation; bubble collapse creates localized hot spots with very high temperature and pressure that induce pyrolysis and radical reactions [S01; S04; S06].
- Configurations:
  - Bench‑scale closed reactor (700 kHz, 1040 W, 1000 mL) and open reactor (700 kHz, 250 W, 200 mL) [S06].
  - Laboratory reactors for PFAS in Milli‑Q (MQ), seawater (SW), creek water (CW) and groundwater [S04].
  - Field mobile system with two 50‑L chambers (total ~100 L capacity) treating highly contaminated groundwater in batch [S01].

**Feed requirements / matrices**

- **Simple solutions and natural waters**:
  - PFOA, PFOS, and 6:2 FTS spiked into MQ, SW, and CW [S04].
  - Deionized water, low‑TDS groundwater, high‑TDS groundwater with seven PFAS individually and as a 24‑compound mixture [S06].
- **Complex PFAS mixtures**:
  - AFFF and foam fractionate (FF) diluted 1000× into MQ, SW, CW, and tested also in groundwater [S04].
  - AFFF and concentrated, high‑TDS IDW as received (IDW volume 1000 mL in closed 700 kHz, 1040 W reactor) [S06].
- **Field groundwater**:
  - PFAS‑laden groundwater from source‑zone conditions at contaminated sites [S01].

These data collectively span dilute to highly contaminated feeds, including high‑salinity and high‑organic matrices.

**PFAS structures evaluated**

- **Perfluoroalkyl acids in simple matrices**:
  - PFOA, PFOS, 6:2 FTS: parent‑loss and defluorination assessed [S04].
- **PFAS mixtures and AFFF‑derived species**:
  - Complex AFFF formulations and FF, with total PFAS quantified, and evidence that ultrasonication produces mainly short‑chain PFAS intermediates [S04].
  - In IDW, 41 PFAS detected and evaluated for defluorination [S06].
- **Novel PFAS**:
  - HFPO‑DA and 6:2 FTAB with near‑stoichiometric fluoride release, indicating extensive defluorination [S06].
- **Field mixture**:
  - Source‑zone groundwater with at least PFOA and PFOS explicitly quantified, plus a broader PFAS mixture inferred from TOP and AOF [S01].

### 2.2 Non‑thermal plasma (RAP)

**Reaction environment**

- Atmospheric **RAdial plasma (RAP) discharge** operating with argon bubbling in small, batch aqueous systems [S02].
- Plasma is generated over or at the liquid interface (the precise geometry is partially inferred), creating a dense network of radial discharges and a highly reactive plasma–liquid interface [S02‑E02; S02‑E05].

**Feed requirements / matrices**

- Laboratory‑prepared **aqueous solutions** of single PFAS in water:
  - Low‑volume batches (30–100 mL) [S02].
  - No explicit co‑contaminants, salinity, or organic background; these are effectively clean matrices [S02].

**PFAS structures evaluated**

- **PFOA**:
  - Low concentration: ~41–41.4 µg/L in 30 mL [S02‑R01].
  - Higher concentrations: 4.14–41.4 mg/L with volume up to 100 mL [S02‑R02].
- **PFOS**:
  - 5 mg/L in water [S02‑R03].
- Transformation products:
  - Shorter‑chain perfluorocarboxylic acids (PFHpA, PFHxA, PFPA, PFBA) as transient intermediates from PFOA [S02‑E08].
  - Minor polyfluorinated acids with partial H substitution [S02‑E08].
  - Lower‑homologue PFOS impurities (perfluoroheptane and perfluorohexane sulfonates) degraded but not produced via chain‑shortening [S02‑E01].

No RAP data here address complex PFAS mixtures, high salinity, or organics‑rich feeds.

### 2.3 Electrochemical oxidation

There are two distinct EO evidence streams:

1. **Pilot‑scale FF–EO system on real waters** [S03].
2. **Generic process‑scale LCA model of EO** [S05].

**Reaction environment (pilot)**

- EO is applied downstream of foam fractionation:
  - FF first preconcentrates PFAS from groundwater or landfill leachate into a PFAS‑rich foam [S03‑E01; S03‑E05; S03‑R01; S03‑R02].
  - EO then treats PFAS‑enriched foam and associated waters in a flow‑cell configuration (details of hydrodynamics and current density are not resolved in the extracts).
- Mechanistically, PFAS degradation on boron‑doped diamond (BDD) electrodes is discussed, with direct electron transfer followed by radical‑mediated steps and known issues such as surface fluorination and scale formation [S03‑E06].

**Feed requirements / matrices**

- **Preconcentration step (FF)**:
  - PFAS‑contaminated **groundwater** and **landfill leachate** with pH ~7.5–7.8, conductivity 450–470 mS/m, TOC 2.1–2.7 mg/L [S03‑R01; S03‑R02].
  - FF selectively enriches long‑chain PFAS in a foam, leaving short‑chain PFAS in the de‑foamed effluent [S03‑E07].
- **EO treatment step**:
  - PFAS‑enriched foam originating from FF on these waters [S03‑R03].
  - Extractable organofluorine (EOF) and target PFAS concentrations measured before and after EO in treated waters [S03‑E03; S03‑E08].
  - Specific chloride/bromide levels for the tested matrices are not given, but the authors note that in waters containing chloride or bromide, EO can generate perchlorate, bromate, and toxic organic halides [S03‑E06].

**PFAS structures evaluated**

- FF–EO addresses:
  - ΣPFAS and **long‑chain PFAA** in groundwater and leachate [S03‑R01; S03‑R02].
  - PFCA and PFSA classes (including PFOA and PFOS) are focal for health/toxicity discussion [S03‑E05; S03‑E08].
  - PFSA specifically highlighted as showing **low electrochemical degradation in fractionated foam** [S03‑E04].
- Many unknown PFAS contribute to EOF in both raw and treated waters [S03‑E03].

**EO LCA model**

- Treats PFAS as a class in water (no compound resolution) in an **aqueous conductive solution** between anode and cathode [S05‑E03; S05‑R01].
- Reaction pathways include direct anode oxidation and indirect radical mediated pathways, with substantial current lost to water electrolysis and oxidation of co‑contaminants [S05‑E03; S05‑E04; S05‑R01].

## 3. Effects of concentration, salinity, co‑contaminants, operation, kinetics, and energy

### 3.1 Sonolysis

#### 3.1.1 Concentration and PFAS loading

- **Synthetic PFAS solutions (MQ, SW, CW)**:
  - For PFOS and 6:2 FTS in seawater, degradation (parent loss) was very high (97% and 95%, respectively), but defluorination efficiencies were only 2.3% and 4% under the same conditions [S04‑R01]. This indicates that high apparent “destruction” based on parent loss can coexist with limited defluorination.
- **AFFF and foam fractionate (1000× diluted)**:
  - AFFF (2200 ppb PFAS) showed ~60% (MQ), 54% (SW), 83% (CW) total PFAS reduction [S04‑R02].
  - FF (1950 ppb) showed ~96% total PFAS reduction across MQ, SW, CW [S04‑R02].
  - The authors attribute lower reduction for AFFF (versus FF) partly to higher initial PFAS levels saturating the bubble surface and limiting effective cavitation [S04‑E03].
- **Concentrated IDW (high TDS)**:
  - Significant defluorination of 41 PFASs in high‑TDS IDW at 700 kHz, 1040 W, 1000 mL [S06‑R03].
  - Reported energy demand was “3 kWh g” (denominator in the unit is truncated, so the mass basis is unknown) [S06‑E05].

Thus, sonolysis tolerates high PFAS loadings (AFFF, FF, IDW), but high load can alter cavitation behavior and degrade energy efficiency.

#### 3.1.2 Salinity and ionic composition

- **Matrix‑dependent performance**:
  - In low‑TDS groundwater (388 mg/L), degradation rates of sulfonates and short‑chain PFASs were 30–60% higher than in deionized water [S06‑E05].
  - In high‑TDS groundwater (10.2 g/L), PFAS degradation rates were lower than in low‑TDS groundwater [S06‑E05].
- **Seawater vs other waters**:
  - For PFOS and 6:2 FTS in seawater, high parent‑compound loss but very low defluorination (2.3–4%) suggests that salts and ions influence radical pathways or facilitate escape/partitioning of ultrashort‑chain intermediates [S04‑E06].
  - For AFFF/FF in SW vs MQ/CW, total PFAS reduction was systematically lower in SW, and authors attribute this to high salinity inhibiting cavitation and scavenging radicals or forming stable complexes [S04‑E03].
- **General conclusion**: ionic composition (TDS, specific salts) materially affects degradation and defluorination; seawater and high‑TDS matrices are more challenging.

#### 3.1.3 Co‑contaminants and organics

- **Groundwaters and AFFF/IDW**:
  - In AFFF and IDW, PFAS constitute a small fraction of total organics. Competitive adsorption to ultrasonic cavities and degradation of non‑fluorinated organics reduce PFAS‑specific degradation rates and complicate energy allocation [S06‑E03; S06‑R01].
  - In IDW, energy metrics based solely on identified PFAS overestimate PFAS‑specific energy consumption; including unidentified PFAS and other organics lowers apparent energy per unit mass degraded but makes interpretation difficult [S06‑E02; S06‑R03].
- **Field groundwater**:
  - The field ultrasonic study monitored metals, anions, TOC, COD, PFAS TOP, and AOF and found little evidence of problematic PFAS by‑products; however, gas‑phase intermediates were not characterized [S01‑E02; S01‑E04].

Sonolysis is thus sensitive to organic loading and matrix complexity; competitive processes may both consume energy and alter PFAS degradation pathways.

#### 3.1.4 Reactor operation and kinetics

- **Kinetics**:
  - Batch sonolysis typically follows **pseudo‑first‑order** kinetics for PFAS degradation [S01‑E08; S04‑E06; S06‑E08].
  - High‑frequency (700 kHz) systems in argon‑saturated environments show measurable rate constants for individual PFAS (e.g., PFOA k ≈ 0.035 min⁻¹; PFOS k ≈ 0.0016 min⁻¹ in a 250 W open system, 200 mL) [S06‑E08].
- **Power density and configuration**:
  - Removal rates decrease as power density decreases (e.g., due to higher volume or lower power) [S06‑E03; S06‑E08].
  - In a 1040 W, 700 kHz closed system, PFBS removal increased as power density was raised from 1040 to 5200 W/L; at very high power density, atomization of bulk liquid was observed [S06‑E03].
  - Field reactor operated as batch, with PFAS degradation increasing with power density and reaching ~80% average PFAS degradation after 12 h [S01‑E08].
- **Temperature behavior**:
  - In the 1040 W closed system, the reaction mixture temperature increased to ~40 °C over 120 min and then stabilized despite cooling, indicating significant energy dissipation as heat [S06‑E04].

#### 3.1.5 Energy demand

- **Bench‑scale IDW**:
  - Sonication of concentrated, high‑TDS IDW with significant defluorination required “3 kWh g” (mass basis not fully visible) [S06‑E05].
- **Reactor electrical consumption**:
  - Inline metering in the 1040 W closed reactor recorded 0.4 kWh over 4 h, giving an estimated electricity cost of $0.18 per gallon at $0.12/kWh [S06‑E04]. This indicates that nameplate power (1040 W) overstates actual electrical draw in that setup.
- **Field groundwater ultrasound**:
  - Total power consumption per treatment cycle was 317 kWh, with a corresponding power cost of $1.57/gal ($0.41/L) at $0.15/kWh [S01‑E08].
  - Electric energy per order (EEO) for PFOA and PFOS in one test (Test 2, Reactor 2) were 2973 and 5572 kWh/m³/order, respectively [S01‑E07]. Authors explicitly caution that these EEOs are from a prototype and may not reflect optimized performance [S01‑E07].

**Implications**: Sonolysis can achieve high parent loss and, in some cases, extensive defluorination, but current reactors are energy‑intensive. Power density strongly controls kinetics, but raising power density can create operational challenges (e.g., atomization) and high energy costs.

### 3.2 Non‑thermal plasma (RAP)

#### 3.2.1 Concentration and volume effects

- **Low concentration (41.4 µg/L PFOA)**:
  - ≥99% PFOA conversion in 2.5 min in 30 mL [S02‑R01; S02‑E07].
  - Rapid treatment at low concentration associated with **higher specific energy costs**; authors note a trade‑off between short reaction times and energy efficiency [S02‑E07; S02‑R01].
- **Moderate to high concentration (4.14–41.4 mg/L PFOA)**:
  - ≥99% conversion in 30 min at 41.4 mg/L [S02‑R02].
  - G50 as high as 2365 mg/kWh at 41.4 mg/L, indicating much better energy efficiency at higher concentration but longer treatment times [S02‑R02; S02‑E07].
- **Volume scaling**:
  - Increasing the treated volume from 30 to 100 mL (4.14 mg/L PFOA) did not compromise process rate and achieved >99% degradation in 15 min with good energy efficiency [S02‑E06; S02‑R02].

RAP thus benefits energetically from **higher PFAS concentration and modest volume scaling**, with a clear concentration–energy trade‑off.

#### 3.2.2 Salinity and co‑contaminants

- The RAP experiments used **simple aqueous solutions** with argon bubbling and no reported salinity or co‑contaminants [S02‑R01; S02‑R02; S02‑R03]. Effects of real‑world salinity, dissolved organics, or metals are unknown in this dataset.

#### 3.2.3 Reactor operation and kinetics

- **Kinetics**:
  - Degradation is modeled as **pseudo‑first‑order**, with rate constants used to derive half‑life (t₁/₂ = ln 2/k) and t₀.₉ (time to 90% degradation, ln 2.3/k) [S02‑E03].
- **Energy metrics**:
  - G50 (mg/kWh) based on the time to 50% conversion, and EE/O (kWh/m³/order) based on time to 90% degradation [S02‑E03].
  - High G50 (>2000 mg/kWh) reported across 41 µg/L–4.1 mg/L PFOA [S02‑R01].
  - For PFOS at 5 mg/L, G50 = 893 mg/kWh and EE/O = 4.51 kWh/m³, values the authors state are markedly more energy‑efficient than reactors used in a comparative PFOS plasma study [S02‑E06; S02‑R03].
- **Reactor design**:
  - Dense radial discharges distributed over the liquid surface, creating an extended plasma–liquid interface with strongly reducing and oxidizing species [S02‑E05].

#### 3.2.4 Transformation vs mineralization

- For PFOA:
  - Sequential formation and decay of PFHpA, PFHxA, PFPA, and PFBA indicate chain‑shortening steps [S02‑E08].
  - After 30 min, the sum of all **detected organic products** accounts for only 6% of initial PFOA TOC; the rest resides in undetected organic species, as inorganic fluoride and CO₂ data are not reported in the provided text [S02‑E08].
- For PFOS:
  - Perfluoroheptane and perfluorohexane sulfonates were present at time zero as impurities and were degraded; no evidence of sulfonate chain‑shortening via SO₃• trapping was found in this system [S02‑E01].
- Thus, RAP demonstrates fast parent‑compound loss and chain‑shortening, but **mineralization and defluorination extents are not quantified** in this evidence set.

### 3.3 Electrochemical oxidation

#### 3.3.1 Concentration, salinity, and co‑contaminants (pilot FF–EO)

- **Feed waters**:
  - Groundwater and leachate had pH ~7.5–7.8, conductivity 450–470 mS/m, and TOC 2.1–2.7 mg/L [S03‑E07].
- **Foam fractionation performance (pre‑concentration)**:
  - Mean ΣPFAS removal: 60% in groundwater; 51% in leachate [S03‑E07].
  - Long‑chain PFAA removal ≈90% in both matrices [S03‑E07].
  - FF preferentially removes long‑chain, surface‑active PFAS; short‑chain and non‑amphiphilic PFAS are less efficiently removed [S03‑E01; S03‑E07].
  - Mass balance closure incomplete: ~87 ± 35% and 58 ± 13% recovery of influent ΣPFAS in effluent+foam for groundwater and leachate, respectively, indicating unaccounted PFAS fractions [S03‑E07].
- **EO on PFAS‑enriched foam and associated waters**:
  - Extractable organofluorine (EOF) in waters generally decreased after EO, with magnitudes similar to target PFAS degradation; overall EOF reductions up to 44% are reported for evaluated treatments [S03‑E03; S03‑E08; S03‑R03].
  - However, EO of PFSA in fractionated foam showed **low electrochemical degradation**, potentially challenging EO applicability for PFAS‑rich foams [S03‑E04].
  - In chloride/bromide‑containing waters, EO can generate **perchlorate, bromate, and toxic organic halides**, noted by the authors as a substantial obstacle for industrial application [S03‑E06].

The PFAS mixture, salinity, and presence of other organics/co‑anions thus directly constrain both FF selectivity and EO by‑product formation.

#### 3.3.2 Reactor operation, kinetics, and energy

- **Kinetics and regimes**:
  - While explicit rate constants are not given in the excerpts for the pilot, the paper discusses EO behavior on BDD electrodes:
    - High current densities can lead to mass‑transfer limitations, scale formation, and electrode fluorination, requiring acid rinsing or UV irradiation to restore activity [S03‑E06].
    - Remaining in the reaction‑limited regime (e.g., by decreasing current density over time or ensuring strong mass transfer) can improve energy efficiency at the cost of longer treatment times [S03‑E06].
- **Energy and sustainability (modeling)**:
  - An LCA using common functional units and TRACI categories finds an integrated sustainability index of 0.90 for EO, compared to 0.48 for UVAOP and 0.29 for incineration, under balanced environmental–economic–social weighting [S05‑E08; S05‑R01; S05‑R02; S05‑R03].
  - The EO model emphasizes that a substantial fraction of applied current is diverted to side reactions (water electrolysis, oxidation of co‑contaminants), strongly affecting energy consumption and sustainability [S05‑E03; S05‑E04; S05‑R01].
  - No direct kWh per mass PFAS data are given for the pilot FF–EO in the supplied text, so quantitative energy comparison with sonolysis or RAP is not possible here.

#### 3.3.3 Transformation, defluorination, and toxicity

- **Parent loss and organofluorine**:
  - Decrease in EOF after EO was similar to degradation of measured target PFAS [S03‑E03], suggesting that EO does not simply convert targeted PFAS to equally persistent, unmeasured PFAS at similar fluorine mass, although residual EOF remained.
  - The maximum reported decrease in EOF concentration of water after the evaluated treatments was up to 44% [S03‑E08; S03‑R03]; this does not resolve how much of the remaining EOF is shorter‑chain PFAS vs other organofluorine species.
- **Partial degradation and by‑products**:
  - Insufficient total applied energy density leads to PFAS being degraded mainly to shorter‑chain compounds rather than fully destroyed [S03‑E06].
  - In chloride/bromide‑bearing matrices, formation of perchlorate, bromate, and organic halides is a documented risk [S03‑E06].
- **Toxicity**:
  - TTR binding and Aliivibrio fischeri bioluminescence assays were applied to evaluate toxic potency of waters under the evaluated treatments, and responses were expressed as PFOS‑equivalent and triclosan‑equivalent concentrations [S03‑E02].
  - The provided excerpts do not clearly support the direction or magnitude of toxicity change; only that these metrics were assessed [S03‑E02; S03‑E08].

## 4. Translating evidence into conditional technology‑selection criteria

Here I synthesize how each technology should be matched to specific PFAS‑rich liquid streams, conditioning on matrix, PFAS profile, endpoint, reactor configuration, and scale.

### 4.1 PFAS‑contaminated groundwater (moderate TDS)

**Relevant evidence**

- **Sonolysis**:
  - Bench‑scale: low‑TDS groundwater (388 mg/L TDS) showed 30–60% higher degradation rates for sulfonates and short‑chain PFAS compared with deionized water [S06‑E05].
  - Field‑scale: a 100 L high‑frequency ultrasonic system achieved an order‑of‑magnitude reduction in PFAS concentration and ~80% average parent PFAS degradation after 12 h per batch, with decreasing AOF (organofluorine) and no clear evidence of problematic PFAS by‑products in the monitored species [S01‑E04; S01‑E08].
  - Defluorination/mineralization was not closed at field scale; gas‑phase intermediates remain uncharacterized [S01‑E04].
- **EO (as FF–EO)**:
  - FF removed ~60% ΣPFAS and ~90% long‑chain PFAA from groundwater [S03‑E07].
  - FF is inherently less effective for short‑chain/non‑amphiphilic PFAS, so de‑foamed groundwater will retain these species [S03‑E01; S03‑E07].
  - EO on foam reduced target PFAS and EOF in associated treated waters, but PFSA in foam degraded poorly [S03‑E03; S03‑E04; S03‑R03].
- **RAP plasma**:
  - No direct groundwater data; all evidence is from clean aqueous solutions.

**Conditional recommendations**

- **If the primary goal is substantial reduction of a broad PFAS suite (incl. precursors) in on‑site groundwater with moderate TDS and relatively high concentrations**:
  - High‑frequency sonolysis has direct field evidence of order‑of‑magnitude decreases in PFAS and decreasing AOF [S01].
  - However, current field reactors are small (100 L capacity) and energy‑intensive (317 kWh/treatment cycle; ~$1.57/gal), and defluorination/mineralization is not fully characterized [S01‑E08; S01‑E04]. Use where energy cost is acceptable, treatment volumes are relatively modest, and the need for on‑site destruction outweighs operational cost.
- **If the strategy allows a treatment train with separation plus destruction**:
  - FF–EO is promising where groundwater has a substantial fraction of long‑chain PFAA:
    - FF effectively transfers long‑chain PFAS into a foam (≈90% removal) [S03‑E07].
    - De‑foamed water may meet regulatory goals for long‑chain PFAS but will still contain short‑chain PFAS.
    - EO can then be applied to the PFAS‑enriched foam, though low degradation of PFSA in foam in this study signals a major design and optimization need [S03‑E04].
  - This approach shifts the energy demand to a small, highly contaminated foam volume, which is conceptually advantageous but needs better evidence on defluorination and by‑products in real foams.
- **RAP plasma** for groundwater:
  - Existing RAP data in this record do not cover real groundwater matrices, so recommending RAP for untreated groundwater would be speculative.

### 4.2 High‑TDS groundwater and seawater

**Relevant evidence**

- **Sonolysis**:
  - High‑TDS groundwater (~10.2 g/L): PFAS degradation rates were lower than in low‑TDS groundwater [S06‑E05].
  - Seawater: PFOS and 6:2 FTS exhibited 97% and 95% degradation but only 2.3% and 4% defluorination, respectively [S04‑R01]. For AFFF and FF in SW, total PFAS reduction was lower than in MQ and CW [S04‑E03].
  - Authors attribute these effects to salinity suppressing cavitation, scavenging radicals, and altering interactions between PFAS and degradation intermediates [S04‑E06; S04‑E03; S06‑E05].
- **EO and RAP**:
  - No explicit data in these records for high‑salinity waters.

**Conditional recommendations**

- **For seawater or highly saline groundwater with PFAS**:
  - Sonolysis is **technically capable** of high parent‑compound loss but may deliver very limited defluorination in some cases (PFOS, 6:2 FTS in SW) [S04‑E06].
  - If the endpoint is parent reduction only (e.g., as a polishing step after primary separation), sonolysis may still be attractive; however, if the requirement is **substantial defluorination/fluorine mass closure**, the current evidence suggests sonolysis performance in seawater must be considered uncertain and matrix‑dependent.
  - For RAP and EO, further data are needed on high‑salinity performance before robust selection; salinity‑driven radical scavenging and by‑product formation (for EO) are potential constraints.

### 4.3 AFFF concentrates and foam fractionate

**Relevant evidence**

- **Sonolysis (lab)**:
  - 1000×‑diluted AFFF and FF in MQ, SW, CW:
    - AFFF: ~54–83% total PFAS reduction depending on matrix [S04‑R02].
    - FF: ~94–96% total PFAS reduction across matrices [S04‑R02].
    - Ultrasonication produced mainly short‑chain PFAS, with multiple lines of evidence cited by the authors (external to this dataset) suggesting these are less toxic, but they remain organofluorine residues [S04‑E03; S04‑R02].
    - Defluorination of diluted AFFF and FF was assessed via F⁻ release, but specific numbers are not provided in the supplied text; HF formation is a potential by‑product, captured in NaOH scrubbers [S04‑E04; S04‑E05; S04‑R02].
- **Sonolysis (bench‑scale real wastes)**:
  - AFFF and concentrated IDW at full strength (not 1000× diluted) were treated in 700 kHz, 1040 W closed system (1000 mL) with significant defluorination of 41 PFAS in IDW and measurable energy consumption [S06‑E05; S06‑R03].
- **FF–EO (pilot)**:
  - FF is already used as a preconcentration step in the pilot FF–EO train, producing PFAS‑enriched foam [S03‑E01; S03‑E05].
  - EO on fractionated foam had **low PFSA degradation**, raising doubts about using EO alone to mineralize PFAS in foam [S03‑E04].

**Conditional recommendations**

- **For PFAS‑rich AFFF and FF streams**:

  - **Sonolysis is currently the most directly supported destructive option** in this evidence set:
    - It can handle complex mixtures and high PFAS loads (IDW, AFFF, FF) [S04; S06].
    - In diluted AFFF/FF, total PFAS reductions of ~54–96% were achieved depending on matrix [S04‑R02].
    - Significant defluorination of PFAS in IDW was demonstrated, including near‑stoichiometric fluoride release from novel PFAS HFPO‑DA and 6:2 FTAB [S06‑E05; S06‑R01; S06‑R03].
    - However, energy demand per mass destroyed in concentrated wastes remains high (e.g., “3 kWh g” for IDW, mass basis not fully specified) [S06‑E05], and HF capture and handling are required [S04‑E04].
  - **EO alone** appears less suitable as a primary destruction technology for PFAS‑rich foams:
    - Low PFSA degradation in fractionated foam could leave a large fraction of sulfonated PFAS intact [S03‑E04].
    - Unknown by‑products and organofluorine residues remain after EO, and hazardous oxyhalides can form in halide‑rich matrices [S03‑E06; S03‑E03].
    - If used, EO should likely follow some combination with other destructive technologies and be carefully engineered with respect to PFSA degradation and by‑product control.
  - **RAP plasma** has no data yet on full‑strength AFFF or foams; extrapolation from simple PFOA/PFOS solutions is not justified here.

Given this, for PFAS‑rich AFFF and FF liquids, current evidence supports favoring **high‑frequency sonolysis** as a leading candidate, recognizing high energy demand, incomplete mineralization in some matrices, and HF handling as key design issues.

### 4.4 Investigation‑derived waste and other complex, high‑TDS process waters

**Relevant evidence**

- **Sonolysis (IDW)**:
  - Concentrated, high‑TDS IDW containing 41 PFASs treated in a 700 kHz, 1040 W closed reactor (1000 mL) achieved significant defluorination, with an energy demand reported as “3 kWh g” (denominator censored) [S06‑E05; S06‑R03].
  - Energy losses in conversion from electrical power to effective cavitation are very high; energy efficiency is strongly influenced by matrix chemistry and frequency selection [S06‑E07; S06‑R03].
  - Frequency optimization is compound specific and not practical for complex mixtures like IDW [S06‑E07].
  - A substantial amount of co‑occurring organics is degraded alongside PFAS, complicating energy attribution [S06‑E02; S06‑E03; S06‑R03].

**Conditional recommendations**

- For **complex, high‑TDS, organics‑rich wastes** such as concentrated IDW:
  - High‑frequency sonolysis has direct bench‑scale evidence of significant PFAS defluorination under high‑TDS conditions [S06‑E05].
  - However, energy intensity is high, and defluorination/mineralization pathways for all organofluorine species remain incompletely characterized [S06‑R03].
  - EO and RAP data for similarly complex, high‑TDS liquids are absent here.
- Practical implication:
  - Sonolysis may be among the few destructive technologies with demonstrated capability on such matrices, but energy and scalability must be explicitly evaluated, potentially in combination with preconcentration (e.g., FF) or tailored matrix conditioning.

### 4.5 Dilute PFAS in relatively clean waters (e.g., RO concentrates, dilute industrial effluents)

**Relevant evidence**

- **RAP plasma**:
  - At 41.4 µg/L PFOA in 30 mL, RAP achieved ≥99% conversion in 2.5 min, with trade‑offs: short times at low concentrations imply higher specific energy, whereas higher concentrations improve energy efficiency (G50 > 2000 mg/kWh) [S02‑E07; S02‑R01].
- **EO (LCA and prior data referenced)**:
  - EO has been shown in other studies (cited in the LCA) to achieve >99% PFAS removal from contaminated wastewater and RO concentrates [S05‑E06]. The LCA suggests EO is the most sustainable of the three modeled destruction options under their assumptions [S05‑E08].
  - The pilot FF–EO treats real leachate and groundwater with low TOC and moderate conductivity; while numerical EO energy metrics are not provided here, the sustainability analysis implies that, under modeled conditions, EO can be competitive in energy and cost [S05‑E08; S05‑R01].

**Conditional recommendations**

- **For relatively clean, low‑salinity concentrates with modest co‑contaminant loads**:
  - Both RAP plasma and EO are plausible candidates for destructive treatment:
    - RAP offers very fast kinetics and high energy efficiency at mg/L PFOA/PFOS, though chain‑shortening dominates, and mineralization/defluorination remain unquantified [S02].
    - EO offers strong class‑level removal based on referenced studies (via the LCA) and, in principle, can achieve high degrees of oxidation; however, by‑product formation (short‑chain PFAS, oxyhalides) must be controlled [S03‑E06; S05‑E06].
  - Technology selection should consider:
    - PFAS speciation (carboxylates vs sulfonates vs novel PFAS).
    - The need for defluorination vs parent removal.
    - Available infrastructure and whether FF preconcentration is feasible.

---

## 5. Cross‑technology trade‑offs and residual streams

### 5.1 Parent loss vs defluorination/mineralization

- **Sonolysis**:
  - Can achieve:
    - High parent‑compound loss with **low defluorination** (e.g., PFOS, 6:2 FTS in SW) [S04‑E06].
    - Near‑stoichiometric defluorination for some novel PFAS (HFPO‑DA, 6:2 FTAB) [S06‑E05].
    - Significant defluorination of complex mixtures in IDW [S06‑E05].
  - Field groundwater treatment shows strong parent loss and decreased AOF but does **not** present a full fluorine balance, and gas‑phase intermediates remain unquantified [S01‑E04].
- **RAP plasma**:
  - Demonstrates fast parent loss and sequential chain‑shortening for PFOA with only 6% of initial TOC captured in identified organic intermediates after 30 min [S02‑E08].
  - The absence of fluoride and CO₂ data in this record means neither defluorination nor mineralization can be inferred.
- **EO**:
  - Parent PFAS degradation is accompanied by reduction in EOF of up to 44% across the evaluated treatment technologies, but substantial EOF remains [S03‑E03; S03‑E08].
  - Shorter‑chain PFAS form when energy input is insufficient [S03‑E06]; full defluorination is not demonstrated in these pilot data.

Hence, **none of the technologies can be assumed to deliver complete mineralization based solely on parent PFAS loss**. Defluorination and total organofluorine metrics must be explicitly planned, measured, and reported.

### 5.2 Residuals and by‑products

- **Sonolysis**:
  - Tends to generate **short‑chain PFAS intermediates**, though these may be less toxic than long‑chain analogs according to cited literature [S04‑E03; S04‑R02]. They remain organofluorine residues.
  - HF is a likely inorganic by‑product; mist and HF were captured in NaOH scrubbers in laboratory setups [S04‑E04].
  - Significant defluorination in IDW and novel PFAS indicates inorganic fluoride generation; however, detailed carbon‑fate analysis is missing [S06‑E05; S06‑R03].
  - Field groundwater ultrasound showed little evidence of problematic PFAS by‑products among measured species, but gas‑phase intermediates were not assessed [S01‑E02; S01‑E04].
- **RAP plasma**:
  - Produces chain‑shortened perfluorocarboxylic acids and minor polyfluorinated acids; 94% of initial TOC is in undetected organic species after 30 min, so transformation products are largely unknown [S02‑E08].
- **EO**:
  - Produces shorter‑chain PFAS when energy density is insufficient [S03‑E06].
  - In halide‑containing waters, EO can generate perchlorate, bromate, and toxic organic halides [S03‑E06].
  - Pilot FF–EO reduced EOF but did not identify residual PFAS species contributing to EOF [S03‑E03].

These residual streams and transformation products must be explicitly managed in process selection and design.

## 6. Practical guidance for fit‑for‑purpose selection

Based on the above synthesis:

### 6.1 When to favor sonolysis

Consider sonolysis when:

- **Matrix is complex or highly contaminated** (AFFF, FF, IDW, high‑concentration groundwater) and:
  - PFAS include legacy and novel compounds for which other technologies have limited data (e.g., HFPO‑DA, 6:2 FTAB in IDW) [S06‑E05].
  - Preconcentration is either already done (FF) or off‑site shipment of concentrates is impractical.
- **High energy cost is acceptable** relative to risk and logistics:
  - Bench‑scale and field data show significant energy demand (e.g., 317 kWh per 100 L field batch; lab IDW at “3 kWh g” on an unspecified basis) [S01‑E08; S06‑E05].
- **Design considerations**:
  - Ensure adequate HF capture and mist management [S04‑E04].
  - Optimize power density while avoiding problematic atomization at very high values [S06‑E03].
  - For high‑TDS/seawater matrices, anticipate lower defluorination and consider additional monitoring or coupled processes [S04‑E06; S06‑E05].

### 6.2 When to favor RAP plasma

Consider RAP (or analogous non‑thermal plasma) when:

- **PFAS are present as relatively simple PFOA/PFOS solutions**, with:
  - Concentrations in the µg/L–mg/L range.
  - Modest volumes (tens to hundreds of mL in current evidence).
- **Goals**:
  - Rapid, near‑complete **parent‑compound loss** with good energy efficiency, especially at mg/L concentrations [S02‑R02].
- **Caveats**:
  - Transformation to unknown organics is extensive; mineralization and defluorination must be quantified before deploying RAP for drinking‑water endpoints [S02‑E08].
  - Performance in saline or complex matrices is unknown; additional studies are needed before extrapolating to real wastewaters.

RAP appears particularly well‑suited, on current evidence, to **treating pre‑treated or relatively clean concentrates** where PFOA/PFOS dominate and rapid parent removal is the primary design driver.

### 6.3 When to favor EO (with or without FF)

Consider EO when:

- **Matrix allows preconcentration** by FF or other separation:
  - FF–EO is attractive for waters where long‑chain PFAA dominate and can be selectively transferred into foam (≈90% removal), reducing volume and mass for destruction [S03‑E07].
  - EO then treats the foam; careful attention to PFSA degradation is essential [S03‑E04].
- **Economic and sustainability considerations are central**:
  - LCA suggests EO has the highest integrated sustainability index (0.90) among EO, UVAOP, and incineration under the conditions modeled [S05‑E08].
- **Limitations to manage**:
  - Avoiding incomplete degradation that leaves shorter‑chain PFAS [S03‑E06].
  - Mitigating formation of oxyhalides in halide‑bearing matrices [S03‑E06].
  - Addressing mass‑transfer limitations, electrode scaling, and surface fluorination (especially for BDD) through reactor design and operating strategies [S03‑E06].
  - Ensuring that all PFAS stripped in FF are quantitatively transferred to foam and thence to the EO reactor; current mass balance gaps (87 ± 35% and 58 ± 13% recovery) indicate this is not yet assured [S03‑E07].

Given the available evidence, EO is best matched to **moderately complex, moderate‑salinity waters where FF preconcentration is feasible and where class‑level sustainability is a driving criterion**, rather than extremely saline or highly organics‑rich feeds.

## 7. Key research and implementation gaps

To better match EO, non‑thermal plasma, and sonolysis to PFAS‑rich liquids, the following targeted gaps should be addressed:

1. **Fluorine and carbon balances**
   - Systematic measurement of inorganic fluoride, total organic fluorine (TOF/AOF/EOF), and CO₂ is needed for all three technologies in representative matrices, especially:
     - RAP plasma beyond chain‑shortening [S02‑E08].
     - Field and pilot sonolysis [S01; S06‑R03].
     - EO on PFAS‑enriched foams and treated waters [S03‑E03].

2. **Matrix‑specific performance and energy**
   - Standardized energy metrics (kWh per g PFAS defluorinated or per g F released) are needed for:
     - Sonolysis of AFFF, FF, IDW and saline waters [S04; S06‑R03].
     - RAP in the presence of realistic salinity and organics [S02].
     - EO in FF–EO systems, disaggregating FF and EO contributions [S03].

3. **By‑product speciation and toxicity**
   - For sonolysis, identification of short‑chain PFAS and non‑fluorinated organics under different matrices and conditions [S04; S06].
   - For RAP, structural elucidation of the unidentified 94% of TOC after PFOA treatment [S02‑E08].
   - For EO, systematic tracking of shorter‑chain PFAS, oxyhalides, and potential toxic organohalides, combined with bioassay‑based toxicity evaluation [S03‑E02; S03‑E06].

4. **Scale‑up constraints**
   - Sonolysis:
     - Engineering designs to improve energy translation efficiency, control atomization, and maintain high power density at scale [S06‑E03; S06‑E07; S01‑E04].
   - RAP:
     - Scaling from 30–100 mL to liter or pilot scales while preserving discharge uniformity and energy efficiency [S02‑E06; S02‑E07].
   - EO:
     - Flow cells and hydrodynamics that minimize mass‑transfer limitations and electrode fouling in real matrices [S03‑E06].

5. **Technology‑train integration**
   - Quantitative evaluation of combined **FF–EO**, **FF–sonolysis**, or **FF–RAP** trains for different feed streams, with explicit accounting for:
     - Residual PFAS in de‑foamed effluent.
     - Destruction efficiency and by‑products in the destructive step.
     - Overall energy and cost per volume and per mass PFAS (and F) destroyed.

---

## 8. Summary

No single destructive technology emerges from the current evidence as **universally superior** across all PFAS‑rich liquid streams. Instead:

- **High‑frequency sonolysis** is the most broadly tested on real and complex matrices, capable of high parent‑compound loss and, in some cases, extensive defluorination, at the cost of high energy demand and potential for incomplete mineralization in saline systems.
- **RAP plasma** offers exceptionally rapid and energy‑efficient parent‑compound loss for PFOA/PFOS in simple aqueous solutions, but transformation products dominate the carbon balance and performance in real waters is untested here.
- **Electrochemical oxidation**, especially in an FF–EO train, offers a promising route to treat real groundwaters and leachates, with strong preconcentration of long‑chain PFAS and favorable modeled sustainability, but currently shows limitations in degrading PFSA in foam and risks producing problematic oxyhalides in halide‑rich matrices.

Fit‑for‑purpose selection must therefore be **stream‑specific**, balancing PFAS profile, matrix chemistry, target endpoints (parent loss vs defluorination vs mineralization), allowable residuals, and energy and sustainability constraints, using the conditional evidence summarized above.
