## 1. Distinguishing Removal, Transformation, Defluorination, and Destruction

### 1.1 Parent‑compound loss (disappearance)

Across technologies, the most common endpoint is loss of the monitored parent PFAS in the treated phase:

- **Batch and continuous SCWO**  
  – PFOS destruction of 70% in batch SCWO at 500 °C, 60 min was defined from parent loss only [S02].  
  – In continuous SCWO, DRE > 99.999 % for PFOS, PFHpS, and PFOA at 650 °C, ~30 s is likewise parent-compound loss in the liquid effluent [S04; S03].  
  – Field SCWO validation reported >99.999 % DRE for a suite of 12 PFAAs in AFFF waste [S09].

- **Hydrothermal alkaline treatment (HALT)**  
  – Batch HALT of AFFF (>3 wt % PFAS) reported >99 % reduction of 40 targeted PFAS in all tests [S13].  
  – Pilot HALT of industrial still bottoms and HPRO concentrates uses %Degradation from LC‑MS/MS as the primary PFAS endpoint [S05].

- **Mechanochemical destruction (MCD)**  
  – AFFF and contaminated soil: targeted subgroups (PFSAs, PFCAs, FTSs, FASAs) showed 99.88–100 % destruction by LC‑MS/MS [S08].

- **Cavitation, plasma, ultrasound, photocatalysis, electrochemistry**  
  – Hydrodynamic cavitation: PFOS “degradation” follows apparent first‑order kinetics; removal per pass is reported [S06].  
  – Gliding arc plasma: 21 % PFOA “destruction” at 20 min, 150 W [S14].  
  – Ultrasonication: 81–100 % “degradation” of 10 ppm PFOA/PFOS/6:2 FTS in Milli‑Q, 67–99 % in creek water [S11].  
  – Photocatalysis: 93 % PFOA and 88 % PFOS “degradation” in 120 min [S15].  
  – Electrochemical removal: 95.3 % PFOA and 98 % PFOS loss in 1 h with no measurable fluoride [S10]; continuous electrochemistry achieved large reductions in several PFAS but only parent concentrations were tracked [S10].

These measurements unequivocally demonstrate that the monitored parent concentration in the sampled phase decreases. They **do not** by themselves distinguish:

- complete mineralization (CO₂ + F⁻),  
- transformation to shorter‑chain or other PFAS,  
- sorption or phase transfer (e.g., to solids, gas), or  
- conversion to non‑PFAS organofluorine.

Several studies explicitly acknowledge this limitation, e.g., the stonebridge fluoride‑electrode paper notes that PFAS “degradation effectiveness is typically evaluated by quantifying both parent compounds and degradation products” and warns that fluoride alone should not be equated with complete mineralization [S12].

### 1.2 Transformation‑product analysis

Transformation endpoints ask whether parent loss corresponds to new organofluorine species:

- **Short‑chain PFAS intermediates**  
  – Ultrasonication of single PFAS: PFPeA, PFHxA, PFHpA, PFHpS formed from PFOA, PFOS and 6:2 FTS in all matrices [S11]. Degradation percentages exceeded defluorination because much fluorine remained in these intermediates [S11].  
  – Ultrasonication of AFFF/foam fractionate and contaminated groundwater: numerous shorter‑chain and ultrashort‑chain PFAS are inferred; analytical limits for TFA and PFPrA prevent full accounting [S11].  
  – SCWO of PFOS: in a detailed PFOS/PFHpS study, no perfluoroalkanoic acids were detected in the liquid under high‑T conditions, but earlier work in the same group on PFOS mixtures showed pools of PFCAs at 420–600 °C [S02; S04].  
  – Photocatalysis: LC‑HRMS/MS identifies multiple short‑chain PFCAs and PFSAs (e.g., PFHxA, PFPeA, PFBA) as PFOA/PFOS transformation products [S15].  
  – HALT: in PFOS/PFHpS work, TFA persists at 600 °C, ~32 s as the most recalcitrant intermediate [S04]; in AFFF HALT, PFCAs remain as intermediates consistent with proposed stepwise decarboxylation [S13].

- **Precursors and rearranged PFAS**  
  – AFFF MCD: time‑courses show PFBA, PFHxA, PFPeA rising then falling, clearly behaving as intermediates from 6:2 FTS and other precursors, with PFOS itself acting as an intermediate [S08].  
  – Field SCWO and lab SCWO: PFDA, PFTrDA, and 6:2 FTS undetected in influent but above MDL in effluent, consistent with precursor transformation [S17].  
  – EO of landfill leachate and groundwater: suspect/non‑target screening revealed multiple new PFAS classes (e.g., FASHN, MeOH‑FASA, H‑PFSA, PFESA‑related species) and halogenated nitrogenous byproducts, indicating complex transformation rather than simple destruction [S16].

- **Incomplete or qualitative TP information**  
  – Hydrodynamic cavitation: aqueous shorter‑chain PFCAs were below 0.05 mol % of initial PFOS, but only the water phase was screened; intermediates confined to the cavitation bubble or gas are not resolved [S06].  
  – Gliding arc plasma: LC‑MS/MS and QTOF confirm some chain‑shortened PFAS (e.g., PFOA from PFOS, PFOS from 8:2 FtS) and head‑group cleavage, but most fluorine is not recovered in quantified PFAS [S14].  
  – HALT of industrial wastes: LC‑MS/MS tracks PFAS subclasses, but non‑fluorinated organic products are not comprehensively characterized [S05].  
  – Photocatalysis: ATR‑FTIR and HRMS show “short‑chain carboxylate bands” and surface‑bound fluorinated species, but many intermediates remain unidentified [S15].

Transformation‑product analysis therefore distinguishes **parent loss** from **structural modification**, but its interpretive strength depends on:

- breadth of the targeted/non‑target list,  
- matrix complexity (AFFF, industrial brine, landfill leachate vs. clean water), and  
- detection limits and ionization biases.

### 1.3 Fluoride release (defluorination)

Defluorination endpoints quantify conversion of organofluorine to inorganic fluoride, typically via IC or F‑selective electrodes:

- **High defluorination but incomplete carbon mineralization**  
  – PFOS batch SCWO: max defluorination 78.2 % at 500 °C, 60 min, with PFOS conversion 70 % and >75 % fluorine mass balance at 475–500 °C [S02].  
  – Continuous SCWO of PFOS/PFHpS/PFOA: complete PFOS defluorination at 650 °C, ~30 s [S04]; mixture runs at 650 °C showed “complete defluorination” at bench scale [S03].  
  – HALT of AFFF: a dedicated fluorine‑balance test measured fluoride at 120.5 % of theoretical fluorine from 40 measured PFAS after 2 h, interpreted as effective defluorination of both measured and unmeasured PFAS [S13].  
  – BN‑ball milling of undiluted AFFF: DeF = 99.7 % after 6 h for 100 µL and >98 % after 8 h for 200 µL in bench jars, referencing initial TOF by CIC and targeted PFAS [S01].  

- **Moderate or low defluorination**  
  – Continuous‑flow SCWO mixed PFAS: despite >99.999 % DRE at 650 °C, liquid‑phase defluorination is not complete at lower temperatures; at 510 °C a substantial fluorine fraction remains as undetected organofluorine or gases [S03].  
  – Mixed AFFF SCWO validation: overall defluorination ratio 62.6 %, leaving ~28.1 % of fluorine unrecovered even after >99.999 % PFAA DRE [S09].  
  – Hydrodynamic cavitation: at 5 mg/L PFOS, defluorination reaches ~20 %, though fluorine mass balance (F⁻ + PFOS‑F) is 97–104 % [S06].  
  – Photocatalysis: for PFOA, only ~2.35 % of initial fluorine is recovered in all measured pools (solution F⁻, residual parent, identified TPs, catalyst‑adsorbed) after 120 min; most fluorine remains unaccounted [S15].  
  – Ultrasonication: defluorination of 10 ppm PFOA is 86 % in Milli‑Q, 56 % in synthetic seawater, 85 % in creek water, but short‑chain PFAS retain much fluorine [S11].  
  – High‑frequency ultrasound field reactor: fluoride increased as PFAS decreased, but F⁻ production was consistently less than AOF loss, leaving an unexplained organofluorine fraction [S07].

- **No detectable defluorination despite substantial removal**  
  – Electrochemical PFAS removal in double‑deionized water: 95–98 % removal of PFOA/PFOS in 1 h with no detectable fluoride, indicating predominantly physical removal (electrocoagulation) under those conditions [S10].  

- **Methodological caveats**  
  – Fluoride‑selective electrode study: establishes an MDL around 0.2 mg/L and shows that fluoride sorption to sediments at pH 5–5.5 and matrix effects from persulfate, permanganate, and ascorbic acid can bias recovery [S12]. Fluoride increases are “suggestive” of PFAS degradation but do **not** prove complete mineralization [S12].

Across studies, **defluorination is closer to a destruction endpoint than parent loss**, but:

- it can be **partial** even when parent loss is near‑complete (SCWO, ultrasonication, photocatalysis);  
- it may miss fluoride partitioned to gas or solids; and  
- analytical MDLs and recovery strongly condition what is called “complete” or “near‑complete”.

### 1.4 Fluorine mass balance and TOF/EOF

Several studies attempt to close a fluorine budget:

- **Explicit fluorine mass balances**

  – **HALT – AFFF (batch)**: theoretical F from 40 PFAS (≈21 887 mg F/L) compared to measured fluoride after 2 h with no catalyst; recovery of 120.5 % of theoretical suggests that targeted PFAS do not capture total organofluorine and that additional PFAS were also defluorinated [S13].  
  – **HALT – industrial matrices**: pilot HALT uses TOF (CIC), inorganic fluoride (IC/F‑ISE/¹⁹F NMR), and VOF in gas to calculate %Defluorination and %F mass recovery = ([IF]+[TOF])/([IF]₀+[TOF]₀) [S05].  
  – **Hydrodynamic cavitation**: total fluorine (F⁻ + PFOS‑F) closes at 97–104 %, supporting the conclusion that fluoride release plus residual PFOS account for most fluorine in the aqueous system at 5 mg/L [S06].  
  – **SCWO (batch PFOS)**: total fluorine mass balance >75 % at 475–500 °C, poorer closure at 425–450 °C; authors attribute the shortfall to lower‑MW fluorinated gases or undetected liquid products [S02].  
  – **SCWO (field AFFF validation)**: stoichiometric comparison of fluorine in 12 PFAAs vs effluent fluoride shows that only 27 % of measured fluoride can be explained by those PFAAs; the remainder must derive from non‑target PFAS or other organofluorine [S09].  
  – **Photocatalysis**: a formal balance, F_initial = F_solution + F_residual_parent + ΣF_TP + F_adsorbed + F_unaccounted, was applied. For PFOA in DI water, only 0.0256 mg as fluoride, 0.018 mg in residual PFOA, 0.0067 mg in identified TPs, and 0.01 mg on catalyst are found from 2.568 mg F input; ~2.35 % recovery highlights a large unaccounted fraction [S15].  
  – **BN–ball‑milling of AFFF**: DeF is defined as ([F⁻]_t − [F⁻]_0)/TOF₀, where TOF₀ uses both targeted PFAS and CIC measurements; high DeF (99.7 %) is supported by this combined TOF‑F⁻ accounting [S01].  

- **Bulk organofluorine metrics (TOF/EOF/AOF)**

  – HALT: TOF by CIC is used alongside targeted PFAS and F⁻, enabling tracking of non‑target organofluorine classes before and after treatment [S05].  
  – MCD: EOF (extractable organic fluorine) complements targeted LC‑MS/MS; the EOF result suggested roughly double the PFAS destruction efficiency compared to a sediment MCD study, though no fluoride or gas‑phase fluorine is measured [S08].  
  – Field ultrasound: AOF and fluoride were used for a simplified fluorine mass balance; the mismatch between AOF decrease and F⁻ increase indicates unresolved organofluorine, potentially including ultrashort PFAS not analyzed [S07].  
  – Stonebridge: argues for combining aqueous fluoride with TOF or EOF measurements to interpret degradation, given that targeted PFAS lists can drastically underrepresent total organofluorine [S12].

These fluorine‑balance endpoints are central to separating **destruction** (C–F bond cleavage) from mere **repartitioning** or transformation. Where the balance is poorly closed (photocatalysis, ultrasonication, some SCWO and EO systems), destruction claims must remain highly qualified.

### 1.5 Mineralization and carbon‑fate indicators

True mineralization requires evidence on carbon as well as fluorine:

- **TOC reductions without full carbon closure**

  – Photocatalysis: 93 % and 88 % PFOA/PFOS disappearance correspond to only 27.1 % and 23 % TOC removal in DI water after 120 min [S15]. This gap clearly shows extensive transformation to partially oxidized organics rather than mineralization.  
  – BN‑BM of high‑load AFFF: approximately 50 % of AFFF TOC remains after treatment [S01], despite high PFAS destruction and significant defluorination.  
  – SCWO of PFOS/PFOA with ethanol co‑fuel: CO in the exhaust gas is <1 ppm for T > 510 °C, confirming complete oxidation of ethanol, not of PFAS carbon [S04]. TOC in PFAS streams is not directly reported, so PFAS‑carbon mineralization cannot be inferred beyond TOC trends when given.

- **Conceptual or inferred mineralization**

  – HALT and BN‑BM: authors explicitly equate “quantitative defluorination” or “near‑complete DeF > 95 %” with mineralization and mitigated risk [S05; S01; S13], but they do not present CO₂ or total inorganic carbon data.  
  – Hydrodynamic cavitation: near‑complete PFOS mineralization is inferred from high fluoride yield per degraded PFOS molecule, absence of aqueous shorter‑chain PFCAs, and closed F mass balance [S06], again without direct carbon data.  
  – GAP plasma: 21 % PFOA loss and 17 % defluorination at 180 kJ L⁻¹ are described as a “regime of near complete mineralization” of the degraded fraction, based solely on fluoride plus limited PFAS speciation [S14].  
  – Continuous SCWO (lab and field): authors state that SCWO can “effectively mineralize PFAS at field scale” based on high DRE and partial defluorination [S03; S09], but carbon endpoints (TOC, CO₂ from PFAS) are not reported in the provided text.

- **Bioassay‑based toxicity change**

  – EO of landfill leachate and groundwater: effect‑based bioassays showed strongly reduced activity after EO, and the transformation products identified by HRMS had negligible activity compared with the original pollutants [S16]. However, this is a **toxicity** change endpoint, not proof of molecular mineralization.

In sum, direct carbon‑fate data (TOC + CO₂) are sparse. TOC usually shows **partial** removal at times when parent PFAS are almost gone, making it clear that **disappearance and defluorination cannot safely be equated with carbon mineralization.**

## 2. How Analytical Scope, Time, and Limits Shape Interpretation

### 2.1 Target lists and non‑target coverage

The ability to distinguish removal from destruction depends heavily on analytical scope:

- **Narrow targeted suites**  
  – Many SCWO and HALT studies quantify on the order of 12–40 PFAS species [S04; S09; S13; S17]. In AFFF and industrial matrices, these represent only a fraction of total organofluorine, as directly acknowledged by HALT and SCWO authors [S13; S17].  
  – Electrochemical and hybrid AOP studies typically use limited lists (e.g., five PFAS in continuous electrochemistry [S10]) and no TOF/EOF, so unmonitored intermediates are completely invisible.

- **Broader PFAS and halogenated space**  
  – EO work employed suspect and non‑target screening, revealing entirely new PFAS classes and halogenated nitrogenous byproducts that would be invisible to typical triple‑quadrupole methods [S16].  
  – Photocatalysis and ultrasonication used LC‑HRMS/MS and multiple authentic standards to detect short‑chain PFCAs/PFSAs, plus operando surface probes (XPS, TOF‑SIMS) [S15; S11]. Despite this, fluorine mass balances still show large unaccounted fractions [S15].

- **Bulk organofluorine metrics**  
  – HALT and Stonebridge explicitly argue for TOF/AOF/EOF measurements to capture non‑target organofluorine [S05; S12]. In SCWO and SCWO validation, comparison of PFAA‑bound F to effluent fluoride shows that targeted analytes explain only a small share of total defluorination [S02; S09; S17].

**Implication**: Any claim that “no harmful byproducts are formed” is only as strong as the PFAS and organofluorine scope, gas‑phase coverage, and detection limits. Where only parent loss is measured, destruction must be considered unproven.

### 2.2 Sampling time and transient species

Time resolution is critical:

- **Incubation periods and lag between destruction and defluorination**  
  – BN‑BM of AFFF shows an “incubation period” where PFAS decay and F⁻ yield accelerate only after 4–6 h, depending on AFFF loading [S01]. Interpreting short‑duration tests would miss later defluorination.  
  – Hydrodynamic cavitation: greatest PFOS degradation per pass occurs early (0–8.9 passes; up to 1.6 % pass⁻¹), while defluorination per pass peaks much later (53.4–71.2 passes; 0.44 % pass⁻¹) [S06].  
  – Gliding arc plasma: PFAS destruction occurs faster than defluorination; destruction curves flatten while F⁻ continues to rise [S14].  
  – Ultrasonication: degradation percentages rapidly approach 80–100 %, but defluorination lags and stabilizes at lower values; short‑chain intermediates are present for extended times [S11].  
  – Photocatalysis: 93 % PFOA loss but only ~2.35 % fluorine recovered as F⁻ or identified TPs at 120 min [S15], implying that much of the fluoride release may occur later or via slow transformations.

- **Residence‑time dependence in SCWO**  
  – Batch PFOS SCWO shows asymptotic destruction with residence time at fixed temperature; 60 min at 500 °C is needed for 70 % PFOS destruction and 78 % defluorination [S02].  
  – Continuous SCWO achieves similar or better DRE and defluorination at much shorter residence times due to better mixing and radical flux [S03; S04].

**Implication**: Early‑time measurements tend to overstate the apparent gap between removal and defluorination, while late‑time snapshots can hide transient toxic intermediates. Comprehensive evaluation requires **time‑resolved profiles** of parent, TPs, F⁻, and ideally TOC/TF.

### 2.3 Detection limits, quantification ranges, and matrix effects

- **Fluoride detection**  
  – IC DL for F⁻ in some BM/HALT and photocatalysis studies is ~50 µg/L [S01; S15], while fluoride‑selective electrodes can reach ~0.2 mg/L in optimized conditions [S12].  
  – Stonebridge shows that matrix spike recoveries vary from >96 % (filtered groundwater) to <38 % (with sediments), and oxidants/ascorbic acid distort slopes and recoveries [S12]. F⁻ sorption at pH 5–5.5 is significant [S12].

- **PFAS MDLs and LOQs**  
  – LC‑MS/MS MDLs of 0.01–0.1 µg/L in plasma and SCWO work [S14; S04], 0.05 ppb in PFOS/PFHpS kinetics [S04], and 0.6 µg/L in cavitation work [S06] define what is seen as “complete removal”.  
  – In ultrasonication work, the inability to detect TFA/PFPrA at low levels drives fluorine‑balance uncertainty [S11].  
  – In field SCWO validation, some PFAS were “below MDL” in influent but above MDL in effluent, which can mimic formation but may also reflect dilution and method sensitivity [S17].

- **Gas‑phase and solid‑phase gaps**

  – Many systems do not analyze gases: batch SCWO acknowledges that poor fluorine mass balance at 425–450 °C likely arises from fluorinated gases but cannot quantify them [S02].  
  – SCWO and HALT recognize volatile organofluorines and fluoride salts as key byproducts (e.g., 1H‑perfluoroalkane, trifluoromethane, solid Si–F or fluoride scales), but systematic quantification is rare [S04; S05; S17].  
  – MCD and BN–BM assume Si–F bond formation in solids from prior work, but the solids are not chemically specitated in the present experiments [S08; S01].

Without explicit and sensitive coverage of all phases, **fluorine mass balances are necessarily partial**, and “complete defluorination” refers only to the measured compartment (typically aqueous).

### 2.4 Unidentified products and unresolved fluorine

Multiple studies explicitly report major unidentified fluorine fractions:

- Photocatalysis: only ~2.35 % of PFOA fluorine recovered in measured compartments after 120 min [S15].  
- Ultrasonication (10 ppm): defluorination < degradation across matrices, with analysts stating that ultrashort PFAS and unknown TPs prevent full mass balance [S11].  
- AFFF/FF ultrasonication and real groundwater: mass balance gaps persist even when TOP assay is included [S11].  
- Field ultrasound: organic fluorine mineralized to F⁻ is less than AOF decrease; TFA and other ultrashort PFAS are not monitored [S07].  
- SCWO validation: 28.1 % of fluorine is unrecovered despite very high PFAA destruction [S09].  
- SCWO pilots: 73 % of measured fluoride cannot be explained by quantified PFAS, implying extensive destruction of non‑target organofluorine [S17].  
- EO of leachate and groundwater: numerous halogenated TPs and unknown PFAS classes appear; their total fluorine contribution is not quantified [S16].

**Interpretive consequence**: whenever there is a large “unaccounted F” term, the technology cannot be credibly claimed to fully destroy PFAS, only to:

- degrade monitored parents,  
- partially defluorinate, and  
- transform PFAS into a mixture of shorter‑chain, ultrashort, or novel organofluorines, some of which may be toxic or mobile.

## 3. A Defensible Evidence Hierarchy for PFAS “Destruction”

Drawing from these studies, one can rank the strength of destruction evidence, **always conditioned on matrix, scale, and analytical coverage**.

### Level 0 – Parent removal only (no fluoride, no TPs)

**Evidence**: Parent PFAS concentrations decrease in the sampled phase; neither inorganic F nor products are measured.

**Typical examples**:  
– Continuous electrochemical treatment with only parent PFAS tracked [S10].  

**Interpretation**:

- Demonstrates **removal from the monitored phase** (e.g., water).  
- Cannot differentiate between sorption, phase transfer, and true degradation.  
- **Unsuitable** to claim “destruction,” “defluorination,” or “mineralization”.

### Level 1 – Parent loss + targeted PFAS transformation products

**Evidence**: Parent PFAS decline; some PFAS TPs (short‑chain PFCAs/PFSAs, telomer remnants) are identified; fluoride may or may not be measured.

**Examples**:

- Ultrasonication: rich suite of short‑chain PFAS from single PFAS and AFFF; limited fluoride data [S11].  
- Gliding arc plasma: confirmed PFOA formation from PFOS, PFOS from 8:2 FtS, plus chain‑length changes [S14].  
- AFFF MCD: transient PFCAs and intermediate PFOS profiles from 6:2 FTS and other precursors [S08].  
- SCWO and SCWO field tests: appearance of PFDA, PFTrDA, 6:2 FTS in effluent [S17].

**Interpretation**:

- Confirms **transformation rather than mere removal**.  
- Still does not quantify destruction of total organofluorine.  
- Without F⁻ and TOF/EOF, cannot assess defluorination extent or total fluorine fate.

### Level 2 – Parent loss + fluoride release (defluorination) without comprehensive F mass balance

**Evidence**: Parent decline; F⁻ measured; TPs partially characterized; total fluorine budget not closed (or only in the liquid phase).

**Examples**:

- PFOS batch SCWO with good F balance at high T but poor closure at low T [S02].  
- Continuous SCWO mixture at 510 °C: parent loss, rising fluoride, incomplete defluorination, and unidentified fluorine fraction [S03].  
- Ultrasonication at 10 ppm: concurrent defluorination and TP formation; ultrashort PFAS unresolved [S11].  
- Photocatalysis: PFOA/PFOS defluorination observed; major fraction of F remains unaccounted and TOC only partly removed [S15].  
- GAP plasma: moderate PFOA destruction and defluorination; most fluorine not in measured PFCAs/PFSAs and F⁻ [S14].  
- Field ultrasound: F⁻ increase but AOF loss larger than F⁻ gain; TFA not measured [S07].  

**Interpretation**:

- Demonstrates **partial defluorination** of monitored PFAS.  
- Without gas and TOF/EOF or solid speciation, cannot claim overall F mineralization; destruction claims must be limited to **“partial defluorination under specified conditions”**.

### Level 3 – Quantitative fluorine mass balance in at least one phase

**Evidence**: Systematic accounting of fluorine across parent PFAS, measured TPs, aqueous F⁻, and possibly catalyst‑adsorbed fluorine, with good closure **within the studied phase**.

**Examples**:

- Hydrodynamic cavitation: F⁻ + PFOS‑F within 97–104 %, with ~20 % of PFOS fluorine converted to F⁻ by end of experiment [S06].  
- HALT industrial pilot: TOF, IF, and VOF used to compute %F mass recovery in still bottoms and HPRO concentrate; conditions tuned for high defluorination and minimized VOF [S05].  
- HALT AFFF batch case: 120.5 % fluoride vs theoretical F from 40 PFAS indicates substantially complete defluorination of those PFAS plus additional organofluorine; solids/gases not specitated [S13].  
- BN‑BM AFFF: DeF defined relative to TOF (targeted PFAS + CIC); high DeF values supported by this internal fluorine accounting [S01].

**Interpretation**:

- Strong evidence that **C–F bond cleavage has occurred extensively** for the tracked organofluorine pool.  
- Where mass balance is liquid‑only, destruction is **phase‑limited**; fluorine in volatile or solid compartments is unresolved.  
- Claims of “mineralization of fluorine compounds” should be explicitly tied to the measured compartment (e.g., “aqueous‑phase mineralization to fluoride”).

### Level 4 – Comprehensive fluorine balance (multi‑phase) + partial carbon endpoints

**Evidence**: Fluorine balances that include aqueous F⁻, gas‑phase VOF/FHF where possible, TOF/AOF/EOF, solid phases, **and** carbon‑fate indicators (TOC, COD, pH).

**Examples** (partial):

- SCWO PFOS/PFOA with gas analysis: volatile organofluorines such as 1H‑perfluoroalkane identified in gas at 420–530 °C, alongside PFCA pools in liquid and F⁻ production; CO below 1 ppm for ethanol but PFAS‑carbon mineralization inferred, not measured [S04].  
- HALT industrial pilot: combination of TOF (CIC), F⁻ (IC/F‑ISE/¹⁹F NMR), and VOF (in‑line FTIR + GC‑MS) to optimize conditions that suppress VOF while maintaining high defluorination [S05].  
- SCWO demonstrations: COD reduction from 4 750 mg/L to 5.17 mg/L, strong pH drop indicating acid formation, and fluoride measurements far exceeding what is expected from targeted PFAS [S17].  

**Interpretation**:

- When fluorine is largely recovered as F⁻ and well‑characterized inorganic or solid fluorides, and TOC/COD shows deep oxidation, **destruction of both fluorine and carbon bonds is strongly supported**, though not yet fully quantified.  
- However, gaps remain where:  
  – VOFs and gases are only qualitatively identified,  
  – unmeasured ultrashort PFAS or organofluorine may exist, or  
  – carbon mineralization from PFAS cannot be separated from co‑fuels or co‑contaminants.

### Level 5 – Full elemental mass balance (C, F) and toxicity evaluation

Ideal but rarely achieved:

- **Requirements**:  
  – Full fluorine mass balance across all phases (liquid, gas, solid).  
  – TOC reductions attributable specifically to PFAS carbon, plus CO₂ and inorganic carbon measurement.  
  – Time‑resolved PFAS and TP speciation.  
  – Bioassay evidence that residual mixtures are not toxic at environmentally relevant exposures.

None of the studies in the evidence base reaches Level 5. Some (e.g., HALT pilot, SCWO pilots, EO suspect‑screening work) address several components but not all.

## 4. Practical Recommendations and Future Needs

### 4.1 Minimum measurement sets by objective

**If the goal is PFAS removal from a water stream (not necessarily destruction):**

- Parent PFAS concentrations (with clear MDLs).  
- Matrix description and mass‑balance for solids/gases if present.  

**If the goal is demonstration of transformation:**

- Parent PFAS + targeted short‑chain PFCAs/PFSAs and known precursors (e.g., FTS, FASA).  
- At least screening for ultrashort PFAS where feasible (TFA, PFPrA) [S11].

**If the goal is to demonstrate defluorination and support claims of destruction:**

- Parent + TPs **and** inorganic fluoride over time.  
- At least a partial fluorine balance (F⁻ + parent‑F + major TP‑F) with uncertainties.  
- Discussion of MDLs, F⁻ recoveries, and phase partitioning (e.g., as in Stonebridge [S12]).

**If the goal is to substantiate mineralization claims:**

- Fluorine mass balance across as many phases as practical (aqueous, gas, solids), with TOF/AOF/EOF where possible.  
- TOC and, ideally, CO₂ or TIC to distinguish PFAS carbon oxidation from co‑fuel and co‑contaminant oxidation [S04; S15].  
- Toxicity assays (in vitro or in vivo) or effect‑based bioassays to ensure that transformation products do not harbor significant residual hazard [S16].

### 4.2 Avoiding over‑interpretation

To stay within defensible bounds:

- **Do not treat parent PFAS disappearance as proof of destruction.**  
  – The electrochemical example (95–98 % PFOA/PFOS loss, no fluoride) is an explicit cautionary case where removal was mainly electrocoagulation [S10].  

- **Do not equate high defluorination with full mineralization unless carbon fate is also established.**  
  – HALT, BN‑BM, cavitation, and GAP studies routinely interpret near‑complete defluorination as mineralization [S05; S01; S06; S14], but in the absence of CO₂/TOC mass balances, this remains an inference, not a demonstrated endpoint.  

- **Always interpret fluoride release in the context of analytical scope and mass balance.**  
  – Stonebridge emphasizes that F⁻ increases “should not be considered to represent complete mineralization” and that site geochemistry can bury fluoride on solids [S12].  

- **Be explicit about unaccounted fluorine.**  
  – When F_unaccounted is large (e.g., ~98 % for photocatalysis [S15] or ~28 % for field SCWO [S09]), destruction claims must be tempered, and unidentified TPs must be assumed present.

### 4.3 Specific research gaps

1. **Integrated C and F mass balances**  
   – Very few studies report both near‑closed F balances and TOC/CO₂ attributed to PFAS. Dedicated experiments with ¹³C‑labeled PFAS could close this gap.

2. **Gas‑phase organofluorine quantification**  
   – VOFs and low‑MW fluorocarbons (e.g., trifluoromethane, 1H‑perfluoroalkanes) are qualitatively reported in SCWO and HALT [S04; S17; S05], but robust, quantitative gas sampling (e.g., OTM‑45‑type methods) is needed across technologies.

3. **Ultrashort PFAS and polar fluorinated species**  
   – Multiple studies flag the analytical blind spots for TFA, PFPrA, and related species [S11; S07; S15]. Development of standardized ultrashort PFAS methods is critical.

4. **Non‑target organofluorine monitoring**  
   – Systematic use of TOF/EOF/AOF, coupled to suspect/non‑target HRMS, should become standard practice for destruction technologies, not an exception.

5. **Phase‑resolved balances in solids‑intensive systems**  
   – MCD, BN‑BM, HALT, and cavitation all likely partition fluorine into solid matrices (Si–F, fluoride salts, catalyst‑bound fluorine). Solid‑phase fluorine speciation and leaching tests are needed to ensure these sinks are environmentally benign.

6. **Toxicity of transformation products**  
   – EO work demonstrates how non‑target screening plus bioassays can show radically reduced activity despite many new PFAS classes [S16]. Similar approaches are needed for SCWO, HALT, plasma, cavitation, and advanced oxidation systems.

---

## 5. Summary

In the current evidence base, **no PFAS treatment technology can be said to universally achieve complete mineralization of PFAS in all matrices on the strength of parent loss alone.**

A defensible assessment of “destruction” should be grounded in a **tiered endpoint set**:

1. **Parent PFAS loss (LC‑MS/MS).**  
2. **Transformation‑product detection (targeted and non‑target HRMS).**  
3. **Fluoride formation and partial fluorine balances.**  
4. **Comprehensive fluorine accounting (including bulk TOF/AOF/EOF and, where feasible, gas and solid phases).**  
5. **Carbon‑fate metrics (TOC, CO₂) and toxicity change.**

Only as studies move up this hierarchy—while clearly stating matrix, scale, exposure conditions, and analytical limitations—can claims progress from “removal” to “transformation,” then to “defluorination,” and finally to credibly supported assertions of broad PFAS **destruction**.
